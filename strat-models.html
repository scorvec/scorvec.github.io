Stratosphere model maps

The newest GFS, GDPS, AIFS single and GEOS FP runs, level by level up to 0.4 hPa, with isentropic PV.

About these maps

The models and their levels

GFS: NOAA's Global Forecast System (the FV3 model, about 13 km, 127 layers up to about 80 km), read on the 0.5° latitude–longitude grid of its published output. The 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC runs, every 12 hours to +384 h (16 days); a run appears here about 6 hours after its start time, and its change map is from the run 12 hours earlier, whose steps share its valid times. The maps are the output's 15 pressure levels from 100 hPa (about 16 km, near the tropical tropopause) to 0.4 hPa (about 54 km, around the stratopause): 100, 70, 50, 40, 30, 20, 15, 10, 7, 5, 3, 2, 1, 0.7 and 0.4 hPa. The heights beside the level buttons are this run's global-mean height of each level at its start. The GFS also writes 0.2 to 0.01 hPa, the mesosphere up to the model top; they are left out, because the model's uppermost layers are its damping (sponge) layers and the analysis has few observations to draw on there. Above about 1 hPa the same holds more weakly: read the top levels as the model's picture, not a well-observed one.

GDPS: Environment and Climate Change Canada's Global Deterministic Prediction System (the GEM model, about 15 km, its top near 0.1 hPa), read on the 0.15° grid of its files on the MSC Datamart and averaged over 3 × 3 cells to 0.45° (the stratosphere has no structure at 15 km that these maps could show, and it keeps the files small). The 00 and 12 UTC runs, every 12 hours to +240 h (10 days); a run appears here about 5 hours after its start time. Above 100 hPa the GDPS publishes only 50, 30, 20, 10, 5 and 1 hPa, so its maps have those seven levels and nothing above 1 hPa; it publishes vertical velocity only from 200 hPa down, so it has no vertical-motion map. Every field's units are checked against its magnitude on every run (ECCC's files have carried decametres under a metres label), and the check is reported below.

AIFS single: ECMWF's deterministic machine-learning model (the AIFS, run from the IFS analysis), read on the 0.25° grid of its open data and smoothed to 0.5° (one 1-2-1 pass, then every second point). The 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC runs, every 12 hours to +360 h (15 days); a run appears here about 7½ hours after its start time, and its change map is from the run 12 hours earlier, whose steps share its valid times. Above 100 hPa the AIFS publishes only 50 and 10 hPa, so its maps have those three levels; with no level between 50 and 10 hPa its column cannot resolve the vertical gradient of θ that PV needs, so it has no PV maps, and it has no vertical-motion map. Its temperatures and heights are checked against their expected magnitudes on every step.

GEOS FP: NASA GMAO's Goddard Earth Observing System forward-processing system (72 layers up to 0.01 hPa), whose forecasts start from its own data assimilation, so its +0 h map is an analysis. It is read on every second point of its 0.25° × 0.3125° grid and interpolated in longitude to 0.5°. The 00 UTC run goes every 12 hours to +240 h (10 days), and the 12 UTC run only to +120 h. The maps use the 14 of the GFS's levels that GEOS FP also publishes (it has no 15 hPa). Its potential vorticity is the model's own Ertel PV on its pressure levels, interpolated to the isentropes from its 22 levels between 400 and 0.4 hPa and given the same light smoothing as the PV computed here. Its temperatures, heights and PV are checked against their expected magnitudes on every step.

How the potential vorticity is computed

GEOS FP publishes its own Ertel PV, which is used as it is. Neither the GFS nor the GDPS publishes potential vorticity in the stratosphere (only surfaces of 0.5 to 2 PVU near the tropopause), so for them it is computed here from the temperature and the winds on the pressure levels: for the GFS 27 levels, 400 hPa to 0.4 hPa (the 21 levels of its main output from 400 hPa up, and the six 25 hPa levels between 125 and 375 hPa from its secondary files); for the GDPS its 16 levels from 400 to 1 hPa (400, 350, 300, 275, 250, 225, 200, 175, 150, 100, 50, 30, 20, 10, 5 and 1 hPa). In both cases it is Ertel's potential vorticity in pressure coordinates (Hoskins, McIntyre and Robertson 1985):

PV = −g [ (ζ + f) ∂θ/∂p − ∂v/∂p · ∂θ/∂x + ∂u/∂p · ∂θ/∂y ],   θ = T (1000 hPa / p)R/cp

with the relative vorticity ζ and the horizontal derivatives taken on the sphere (centred differences, periodic in longitude), the vertical ones in ln p across the actual levels, one 1–2–1 smoothing pass in latitude and longitude, and the rows at the poles set to the mean of the ring next to them. PV, the Montgomery streamfunction and ln p are then interpolated to each isentrope linearly in ln θ, column by column. 1 PVU = 10−6 K m2 kg−1 s−1.

Why the GDPS stops at 850 K. PV depends on the vertical gradient of θ, and the GDPS's levels are far apart in the upper stratosphere: none between 5 and 1 hPa, an 11 km gap where 1200 K and 1600 K lie. What coarse levels cost is measured on every GFS run: its PV is recomputed from only the GDPS's 16 levels and compared with its own from all 27, surface by surface (in the checks below). The GDPS's maps show the surfaces where that comparison holds up, 350 to 850 K; even there the coarse levels push PV up somewhat. On the first measurement (the GFS run of 24 October 12 UTC) the patterns kept correlations of 0.996–0.9996 on 350–850 K, and the polar-cap means came out 0–12 % high (most at 850 K in the north), against 18–37 % off at 1200 and 1600 K; the GDPS's own polar-cap PV beside GEOS FP's was high by about the same amounts (+15 % at 850 K in the north, 1–6 % elsewhere). So read the GDPS's PV for its pattern and its changes, and its values as up to about a tenth high.

Why the colours are scaled. In a resting atmosphere PV grows roughly as θ9/2 with height, from tens of PVU at 475 K to hundreds at 850 K and thousands at 1600 K. The colours follow PV × (θ / 475 K)−9/2 (Lait 1994, referenced to 475 K), so a vortex looks alike on every surface and at 475 K the scaled value is the PV itself; the legend and the hover give the surface's real PV.

Checks. The formula and the interpolation were tested on idealised atmospheres before the first run: a resting and a solid-body-rotating isothermal atmosphere (PV within 0.3 % of the exact value at every level) and a strongly sheared, weakly stratified one where the two tilting terms change PV by 8–16 % and the isobaric form above matches the isentropic form −g (ζθ + f) ∂θ/∂p to 0.2 %. How the vertical motion is smoothed

The GFS publishes VVEL (Pa/s) on all 15 levels of the maps; it is shown as VVEL × 864 = hPa/day, positive downward. (It is the model's geometric vertical velocity in pressure units, −w dp/dz, not the motion across pressure surfaces: it also counts the rise and fall of the surfaces themselves, ρg (∂z/∂t + V·∇z), and its mean over a pressure level is far from the zero that mass conservation needs, so read it for its pattern, not as the net descent that warms the stratosphere.) On a 0.5° grid in the stratosphere the raw field is dominated by gravity waves and tides, so it is smoothed hard before it is stored. In space: a Gaussian with a standard deviation of 600 km along the sphere, applied along each latitude circle exactly (in Fourier space: zonal wavenumber k keeps exp(−½(kσ / a cos φ)2) of its amplitude, so wave 3 at 60° keeps 85 % and anything shorter than about 2,000 km less than a fifth) and then along the meridians, continued over the pole onto the opposite meridian. In time: a running mean centred on the valid time over the 12-hourly steps, trapezoidal over ±36 h (a 72 h mean; weight 1 inside, ½ at the ends, which cancels the daily tide exactly), shortened near the ends of the run so it stays centred (24 h at +12 h, 48 h at +24 h); +0 and +384 h have no vertical-motion map. The legend gives the hours each map averages. Colours: for each level the range is the 99th percentile of the same smoothed field’s magnitude poleward of 20° in 48 samples of GFS analyses (the 15th of each month from July 2022 to June 2026, the seven 00 and 12 UTC analyses around each), rounded up, so a colour means the same from run to run and a strong event stands out. The hover converts to a vertical velocity, w ≈ −ω R T / (p g), with the level's temperature.

How the page works

Nothing on this page is a picture. Each field, level and step is shipped as the model's own numbers, rounded to 256 levels, on its latitude–longitude grid (GFS and AIFS 0.5°, GDPS 0.45°), and your browser colours, projects, contours and reads it; the shading is interpolated between grid points, and the hover value is the stored one at the nearest point. The maps are polar stereographic, true at the pole (0° longitude at the bottom of the northern map and at the top of the southern one). Contours are drawn by your graphics card from the same numbers after a light smoothing; their labels are placed once the map is at rest. Data use: opening a map loads only the map on screen and the heights drawn on it (about 45–115 KB); stepping through the levels loads that step at every level (0.5–2 MB), and playing or dragging the time slider loads every step of that map (31–33 files, 1.5–4 MB). The change views load the previous run’s map beside each of this run’s and take the difference in your browser.
