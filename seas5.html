ENSO indices — this issue against the last three

The shaded fan is this issue (P10–P90 and P25–P75 of the 51 members) with its mean in sienna; the coloured lines are the ensemble means of the previous issues, oldest darkest and dotted, newest solid, each drawn over its own valid months, so the run-to-run shift is read directly. East-based indices matter this year: a warm Niño-1+2 with a cooler Niño-4 is a different event from the reverse, and the Trans-Niño index puts that gradient on one axis.

PDO, AMO, Indian Ocean Dipole, Atlantic Niño

Same fields, same treatment. The PDO is the projection of each member’s North Pacific anomaly onto the ERSST EOF pattern the daily monitor uses, with the global mean removed and NCEI’s scale applied; the AMO is the relative form (North Atlantic minus the 60°S–60°N mean), so the warming trend does not masquerade as a basin mode. Because the anomaly base here is the model’s 1993–2016 hindcast rather than 1991–2020 observations, absolute levels can sit a tenth or two from the published indices; the shape and the run-to-run change are what to read.

Maps: anomaly, and what changed since the previous issue

One map at a time, most of the world on a plate carrée grid. Anomaly is this issue’s ensemble mean against its own 1993–2016 start-month hindcast. Change is the same anomaly minus the previous issue’s, each anomalised against its own hindcast, so a difference is a real shift in the forecast rather than drift; periods the earlier issue does not cover have no change map. SST is drawn at 0.2 °C steps with labelled isolines. The table averages the index ensemble means over the months both issues cover.

Calibrated terciles

For each grid point, season and lead, the tercile boundaries come from SEAS5’s own hindcast — 24 years × 25 members, 600 seasonal means — and the 51 forecast members are counted against them. That removes the model’s mean bias and its spread bias point by point, which is the calibration that matters most for a single-system tercile map. Shading is the most likely category and its probability; white means no category reaches 40 %. Near-normal is not drawn: with a realistic spread it is almost never the most likely tercile, and where it is, the signal is weak anyway. Three overlapping seasons follow the start month; pick the variable and the season. Temperature and 500 hPa height are counted against the hindcast’s linear trend extrapolated to the valid year rather than its flat 1993–2016 mean: two decades of warming (and the thickness rise that goes with it) would otherwise sit inside “above normal” before any El Niño signal is added, and the whole tropical belt read 90–100 % warm by construction. What remains is the departure from the trend, which is where the winter picture becomes discriminating; the normals card below puts the same members against the 1991–2020 normal, the last decade and today’s trend.

Teleconnection indices, member by member

The CPC patterns — NAO, PNA, AO, the East Atlantic, West Pacific and the rest — read from each member’s monthly 500 hPa height (sea-level pressure for the AO) with the same projectors the GEPS subseasonal page uses, so the scale is CPC’s own. The Southern Oscillation index is Tahiti minus Darwin pressure. Two things keep this honest. First, the observed record: the black line is ERA5 passed through the same projection, and the red crosses are CPC’s published monthly index, an independent check on sign and size. Second, skill: for every index and month the 24 hindcast years’ ensemble mean is correlated with ERA5, and the observed index is regressed on the forecast to give a calibrated probability of each phase. Where the correlation is below 0.25 the cell is hatched — the members still say something, but the hindcast says not to trust it, and the calibrated bar falls back to climatology by construction. The board reads the six months at a glance; the chart below it lets you look at one index at a time.

Vortex, QBO and annular mode

The zonal-mean 10 hPa wind at 60°N is the standard vortex-strength measure (its 60°S twin covers the southern spring breakdown); the probability of an easterly reading is the sudden-warming side of the distribution. The equatorial 10, 30 and 50 hPa winds are SEAS5’s own QBO, which sets the Holton–Tan tilt on the vortex. The NAM-by-level indices are polar-cap height anomalies standardised by the hindcast and signed so that positive is a strong vortex, from 10 hPa down to the surface — read them top to bottom for downward coupling. Wave-1 and wave-2 amplitudes at 100 hPa are the planetary-wave forcing that precedes a warming.

Population-weighted temperature: the forecast for each month, against normal and record

For the United States and Brazil, each member’s 6-hourly 2 m temperature is weighted by where people live (geonames places of 15,000 or more, on the 1° grid) into one national series, and the monthly mean of every member is bias-corrected into observed space. Each curve is therefore the forecast probability distribution for that month’s population-weighted temperature: gold is this issue, blue is last month’s, the ticks under the curve are the 51 members themselves. The black lines are what a normal month is, 1991–2020 and 2016–2025, from the local ERA5 record; the dotted lines are the warmest and coldest month on record. The text under each panel gives the forecast mean, its distance from normal, the change since last month, and the share of members warmer than each normal and than the record. Degrees Fahrenheit for the United States, Celsius for Brazil.

Threshold days: cold and hot days over the United States and Brazil

From every member’s daily extremes (6-hourly SEAS5 output, the “in the last 24 hours” maximum and minimum): days a month with the minimum at or below 5, 0, −5, −10, −15 and −20 °C and the maximum at or above 30, 35, 38 and 40 °C over the United States; the maximum at or above 30, 32, 35, 37 and 40 °C and the minimum at or below 15, 10 and 5 °C over Brazil. Members are quantile-mapped per cell from SEAS5’s own hindcast extremes onto the observed record, so the model’s too-small diurnal range does not read as fewer freezes. Normal is the NOAA CPC 1991–2020 daily record counted the same way. The table is population-weighted (expected days per month for the average resident); the maps show each cell as a percentage of its normal frequency, hatched where the normal is under half a day a month.

Snowfall: big days and monthly totals over North America

Every member’s daily snowfall from SEAS5’s accumulated snowfall field (daily steps, 51 members, 1° cells, a 10:1 snow-to-liquid ratio). Precipitation minus evaporation by region: Brazil and Colombia

The drought-relevant water balance, month by month. For each region, every member’s monthly precipitation minus evaporation is averaged over the region and the 51 values are drawn as a distribution: gold is this issue, blue is last month’s, the ticks under the curve are the members. The black line is the model’s own normal for that month and the grey band its year-to-year range, so a gold curve sitting left of the band is a forecast drier than most years. Negative values mean the region loses more water to evaporation than it receives. Brazil is shown by ONS subsystem (Southeast/Centre-West, South, Northeast, North); Colombia is one aggregate, the hydro basins together, since a seasonal model at 1° cannot tell them apart. The table lists the months where three quarters or more of the members are drier than normal.

Anomalies and terciles against observed normals, monthly and seasonal

The default terciles above are relative to the model’s own 1993–2016 hindcast, which removes bias but answers a low-bar question in a warming climate. Here the same members are moved into observed space (member minus hindcast mean plus the ERA5 1993–2016 mean at each grid point, multiplicatively for rain and sunshine) and compared with four references: the hindcast itself, the ERA5 1991–2020 normal, the last decade (2016–2025), and the ERA5 1991–2025 trend extrapolated to the forecast month — what normal is expected to be now. Tercile bounds are ±0.43σ of the observed 1991–2020 spread around each reference (detrended for the trend reference; empirical percentiles for rain and sunshine). Polar stratosphere — cap-mean heights

The 60–90° polar-cap mean geopotential height at 100, 50 and 10 hPa, as an anomaly against the hindcast’s linear trend at each lead, extrapolated to the forecast year — heights carry the warming trend, so an anomaly against the 1993–2016 mean would read high before any circulation signal. A high cap at 100 hPa is a weak, warm vortex (the SSW-side reading); a low cap is a strong, cold one. The hindcast ±1σ band shows how far a seasonal model normally strays, which at these levels is not far — read the sign of the tilt and whether the members agree, not the amplitude. Both hemispheres; the 60°S twin shows the southern vortex’s own cycle at the same leads.

Data and method

Forecast: ECMWF SEAS5 (C3S originating_centre=ecmwf, system=51), 51 members, monthly means for lead months 1–6 and the 6-hourly 2 m temperature, from the Copernicus Climate Data Store; hindcast: the same system’s 1993–2016 re-forecasts, 25 members, for the same start month, and likewise for the previous issue. Sea-surface temperature, 500 and 1000 hPa height, sea-level pressure and stratospheric winds on a 2° grid; 2 m temperature, precipitation, evaporation, 10 m wind, solar radiation and 500 hPa height on a 1° grid over the Americas; geopotential at 10, 50 and 100 hPa poleward of 60° in each hemisphere.

Observations: ERA5 from a local archive (daily 1.5° fields, 1959 or 1991 to the present depending on the field), monthly-meaned for normals, trends, records and skill; CPC ERSSTv5 for the observed Niño indices. Teleconnection projectors are the CPC-style regression patterns built for the GEPS page on NCEP/NCAR R1 (1991–2020). Population weights: geonames places of 15,000 or more people.

Method: indices are cosine-weighted box means; anomalies are member minus hindcast mean at the same lead (member minus the hindcast’s linear trend for geopotential heights, which carry the warming signal). Relative Niño-3.4 follows the CPC/ECMWF construction used elsewhere on this site; the Trans-Niño index is Trenberth & Stepaniak (2001), standardised by the hindcast spread. Terciles against the hindcast use the 33.3rd and 66.7th percentiles of the 600 hindcast samples per grid point; against observed references the members are first bias-corrected (member − hindcast mean + ERA5 1993–2016 mean, multiplicatively for rain and sunshine) and the bounds are ±0.43σ of the observed 1991–2020 spread around the reference (detrended for the trend reference; empirical percentiles for rain and sunshine). Standardised anomalies divide the ensemble-mean anomaly by the reference’s year-to-year standard deviation. Skill is the correlation of the 24 hindcast years’ ensemble mean with ERA5 at the valid month. Teleconnection phase probabilities are calibrated by regressing the observed monthly index (ERA5, same projection) on the hindcast ensemble mean over those 24 years for each calendar month and lead, and reading the ±0.5σ tail areas of a Gaussian centred on the regression prediction with the residual spread — an in-sample fit at n = 24, so the quoted r is slightly optimistic; below r = 0.25 the probabilities are treated as climatology. The AO, EPO, WPO and SOI, which have no built-in standardisation, are expressed in ERA5’s 1991–2020 standard deviation for the calendar month, applied identically to model and observations. What this page reads, and how

ECMWF’s seasonal system, read in full and checked against observations: the ENSO indices from the coastal Niño-1+2 to the central-Pacific Niño-4, each against the previous three issues; the Pacific, Atlantic and Indian Ocean indices from the same fields; what changed since last month; the CPC teleconnection patterns and the stratosphere, each with the model’s own hindcast skill; anomaly and tercile maps against the hindcast and against observed normals; and the forecast for population-weighted temperature in the United States and Brazil set against normals and records. Model drift is removed everywhere by anomalising each member against SEAS5’s own 1993–2016 hindcast for the same start month and lead.
