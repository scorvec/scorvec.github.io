Why the atmosphere spins up in El Niño

In El Niño years the westerly winds strengthen and the day grows a fraction of a millisecond longer: the atmosphere spins up and the solid Earth slows. The extra spin can only come from the ground. When surface pressure is higher on the east side of a mountain range than on its west side, the air presses westward on the mountains; by Newton’s third law the mountains push the air eastward, adding westerly angular momentum to the atmosphere and taking the same amount from the solid Earth, whose rotation slows very slightly. In El Niño the warm ocean reshapes the pressure pattern around South America: the southeast Pacific high west of the Andes weakens and pressure rises over the continent, so pressure stays higher on the east side of the range for months. That standing difference keeps the mountain torque positive month after month and brings the extra westerly momentum in; friction, through weaker trade winds, works against it. This study measures that chain, and why a reanalysis cannot fully account for it: ERA5 four times a day from 1959 to 2026, 41 CMIP6 models, and an hour-by-hour budget of the 2026 rise.

The short answer

  • 1. The Andes torque supplies it; friction brakes it. The El Niño rise in the atmosphere's angular momentum starts in the ocean and comes in through mountain torque. As the eastern Pacific warms, the southeast Pacific high west of the Andes weakens and pressure rises over South America: a pressure difference across the range that lasts for months (the part of the torque that lasts 30 days and more), not more weather events. In December–February the Andes torque is 2.9 Hadley higher than in neutral winters (p = 0.007; in CMIP6 +1.46, all 19 models with daily data of one sign), and the Andes torque regressed on Niño-3.4 is significantly positive from at least six months before the Niño-3.4 peak to seven months after: a long, steady push. Over the six months before the peak the mountain torque supplies +1.44 [+0.44, +2.50] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin of Niño-3.4 in ERA5, and +1.54 with all 41 CMIP6 models of one sign. The Andes are the largest single source (+1.92 in ERA5, +0.98 in CMIP6 with every model agreeing), then the Rockies. Friction is not the driver: the weaker trade winds hand the atmosphere less westerly momentum from the spinning Earth, so friction works against the rise, taking back about 58% of the mountain torque in the models and, in ERA5, more than all of it (result 2). The individual east-side highs are no more frequent or stronger: none of 24 range–season tests of their number, strength or variance is significant. In May 2026 the Andes torque was 13.3 Hadley above normal, second since 1959 only to May 1997.
  • 2. ERA5's model loses westerly momentum, and the analysis puts it back. At each of ERA5's twice-daily 4D-Var window starts the analysis adds westerly angular momentum: +0.048 and +0.022 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ at 09 and 21 UTC, about +8 Hadley held all day, every day, in every month of 2025–26. ERA5's own torques, sampled four times a day, miss the annual budget by −11.3 Hadley. The physics ensemble IFS-ENS drains angular momentum in free forecasts (−0.31 ×10²⁵ by day 15) while closing its own torque budget, so its torques remove the momentum; the data-driven AIFS-ENS shows no global drain. ERA5 runs IFS cycle 41r2, before ECMWF's 2022 retuning of orographic drag, and the increments correct whatever loses the momentum: too much drag is the leading candidate, not the only one.
  • 3. Too much drag, but not where the mountains are. Operational experience with GEM, among other models, is that more drag gives better scores at the cost of too-weak low-level flow in places, and ECMWF raised its turbulent orographic form drag over the years "to optimise forecast skill" (Kanehama et al., 2022). The increment maps do not point to the mountains, though: of the angular momentum the analysis adds below 850 hPa, 6% [4, 8] lands over or within 500 km downstream of rough terrain, which covers 22% of the globe, and 89% [83, 96] over the tropical and other oceans, where the analysis weakens trade easterlies that the model makes too strong. Near the surface in mid-latitudes the analysis weakens the westerlies; it adds westerly momentum above the boundary layer and over the storm-track oceans (56% [54, 59] of the whole-column increment). The pattern is an oceanic boundary layer that carries too much momentum to the surface and an extratropical free troposphere short of westerlies, not excess form drag over the ranges.
  • Which comes first. Day to day, high mountain torque comes before the rise in global angular momentum and the subtropical jet, not after it, and the 500 hPa pattern that sets up an east-side high (a ridge upstream, a trough downstream) is the same in El Niño and neutral years.

1. What angular momentum is, and why it rises in El Niño

Angular momentum about the Earth's axis measures how much the atmosphere turns faster than the planet. Most of it is in the westerly winds: a parcel moving east at 30 m s⁻¹ in the subtropical jet carries far more than one at the same speed near a pole, because it is farther from the axis. A smaller part, the mass term, comes from how the air is spread between latitudes, since the rotating Earth carries mass at the equator round faster. Angular momentum is conserved: the atmosphere can gain it only by taking it from the solid Earth and oceans, so when the atmosphere spins up the Earth slows. Geodesists see it in the length of the day, which tracks the atmosphere's angular momentum and, as Rosen et al. (1984) first showed, rises in El Niño. For this budget, 10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ of extra atmospheric angular momentum lengthens the day by about 0.17 ms.

Why El Niño spins the atmosphere up. El Niño warms the whole tropical troposphere, not just the eastern Pacific. ENSO is the dominant cause of the year-to-year variability that is symmetric about the equator: tropical warming with bands of cooling in the middle latitudes of both hemispheres, from the surface to the tropopause (Seager et al., 2003). By the thermal-wind relation a warmer tropics against cooler subtropics means stronger westerly shear on the equatorward side of the subtropical jets, so the westerlies in the upper troposphere strengthen there, in both hemispheres. In ERA5 the global angular momentum regressed on Niño-3.4 peaks 1 month after the Niño-3.4 anomaly at +0.69 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin, about 0.11 ms of length of day per kelvin.

From warm ocean to faster winds. The thermal-wind change says where the westerlies strengthen, not where their momentum comes from: angular momentum is conserved, so stronger jets with no torque would have to be paid for by easterlies somewhere else. The momentum is drawn from the solid Earth, mostly across the Andes, in four steps. (1) The central and eastern Pacific warm. (2) The pressure pattern responds: the southeast Pacific high, which normally sits west of the Andes, weakens and pressure rises over South America; in northern winter the deeper Aleutian low does the same west of the Rockies (Figure 6). (3) Pressure is then higher on the east side of both ranges, not for a few days but for months, so their mountain torque stays positive month after month. (4) That torque moves westerly angular momentum from the Earth to the atmosphere. Friction is the brake: the trade winds normally hand the atmosphere westerly momentum from the spinning Earth, and in El Niño they weaken, so less comes in. The rise is what the mountain torque supplies minus what friction holds back (section 3).

What the record shows. GFZ Potsdam's record of the atmosphere's angular momentum, computed from ECMWF's analyses separately from the ERA5 calculation used in the rest of this study, shows the rise plainly (Figure 1). Since 1979 global angular momentum has been above normal in 84% of El Niño seasons and below normal in 84% of La Niña seasons. On average it is +0.78 [+0.51, +1.02] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ in El Niño seasons, −0.69 [−0.91, −0.48] in La Niña seasons and near zero in neutral ones, a swing of about 0.24 ms in the length of the day. It rises by +0.73 [+0.59, +0.83] ×10²⁵ per kelvin of the ONI (r = 0.71, p < 0.001), and the GFZ and ERA5 monthly anomalies correlate at 0.99. The ONI does not explain everything: intraseasonal swings, the MJO's among them (Weickmann et al., 1997), move angular momentum by a few tenths from month to month even in three-month means. 2026 stands out. In July–September angular momentum was 3.1 ×10²⁵ above normal, second in the record only to early 1983, and about twice what an ONI of 2.2 K alone would predict (section 6).

How the momentum gets in. All of it has to come through the surface:

$$\frac{dM}{dt} = T_{\text{mountain}} + T_{\text{friction}} + T_{\text{gravity-wave drag}},\qquad T_{\text{mountain}} = -\iint p_s\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial\lambda}\,dA = \iint h\,\frac{\partial p_s}{\partial \lambda}\,dA .$$

Torques are in Hadley (10¹⁸ N m); one Hadley held for a day changes $M$ by 0.0086 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹. The mountain torque on the atmosphere is positive when pressure is higher on the east slope of a range than on the west: the air presses westward on the mountain, and so on the Earth, and by Newton's third law the Earth pushes the atmosphere eastward, adding westerly momentum. Friction works the other way round: surface easterlies, the trades, give the atmosphere westerly momentum from the spinning Earth, and surface westerlies give it back, so weaker trades in El Niño mean less westerly momentum coming in. Gravity-wave drag is the model's account of the push of unresolved mountains. A cold anticyclone banked against the east side of a range makes a positive mountain torque, and those are familiar weather: cold surges down the lee of the Rockies (Colle and Mass, 1995), cold-air incursions east of the Andes (Garreaud, 2000) and the East Asian surges that follow the Siberian high. Such events supply most of the day-to-day mountain torque and much of the swing in angular momentum over weeks (Iskenderian and Salstein, 1998; Weickmann et al., 1997; Lott et al., 2004). See the mountain torque and angular momentum explainers.

Two things are less settled: whether El Niño's extra momentum arrives through a change in those synoptic events or through the time-mean pressure pattern, and how it divides between mountain and friction torque. Reanalysis budgets do not close exactly (Huang et al., 1999; Egger et al., 2007), which limits what a reanalysis alone can say; climate models, which have no analysis increments, are the natural cross-check.

2. Data and method

  • ERA5 (Hersbach et al., 2020) from the ARCO-ERA5 store on Google Cloud, read in GitHub Actions: surface pressure, the turbulent and gravity-wave surface stresses and the vertical integral of eastward mass flux (an exact full-column wind term), at 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC every day from 1 January 1959 to 3 October 2026, 24,748 days, none missing. Four times a day, not two: at 00 and 12 UTC the semidiurnal pressure tide has the same phase, so a twice-daily mean keeps its alias. The daily value is the mean of the four. The mass term of $M$ comes from surface pressure. For 2026 the same fields were read every hour from 1 March. The analysis period is 1979–2025; 1959–2025 is a sensitivity test.
  • Torques are the site's: friction $-a^3\cos^2\varphi\,\tau_\lambda$, gravity-wave drag likewise from ERA5's own stress, mountain torque $h\,\partial p_s/\partial\lambda$ on the 0.25° grid with ERA5's orography, summed over the site's six boxes (5° cells; table below), over six latitude bands and over the globe.

  • Tests: each season-year is one unit. El Niño minus neutral differences are tested by permuting the phase labels (20,000 draws) with bootstrap 95% intervals; regressions on the ONI use a moving-block bootstrap (blocks of three season-years). Across the 24 range–season cells of each statistic the false discovery rate is held at 10% (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Wilks, 2016). Monthly regressions on the detrended ERSSTv6 Niño-3.4 anomaly at lags of −6 to +12 months use a 24-month block bootstrap (2,000 draws), with the FDR across lags. Map tests are per grid cell with the FDR over the field.
  • CMIP6 historical runs 1950–2014 from the Pangeo Google Cloud mirror (Eyring et al., 2016): 41 models, 103 members (up to three per model), each on its own grid with its own orography: monthly mountain torque from surface pressure (the torque is linear in $p_s$, so the torque of the monthly mean is the monthly-mean torque), surface stress tauu, angular momentum from ua masked by surface pressure, and Niño-3.4 from ts. 13 models also publish the orographic gravity-wave-drag tendency utendogw (Gerber and Manzini, 2016) and 19 have daily surface pressure, which gives event statistics on each model's own ENSO (ONI-like index of its detrended Niño-3.4). A model result is robust when the across-model test passes the FDR and at least 80% of models share the sign.

3. How the momentum gets in: the torque budget

Summing each budget term's regression on Niño-3.4 over lags −6 to −1 months gives the angular momentum it supplies in the six months before the anomaly (Figure 3 and the table). In ERA5 the tendency term supplies +0.47 [+0.32, +0.63] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin. (The regression of angular momentum itself rises by +0.42 between those lags; the two differ because one sums monthly tendencies taken across month edges and the other differences monthly means.) The mass term's share is negligible (its regression is about +0.02 per kelvin).

The mountain torque is the source and friction the brake, and both act for a long time. Regressed on Niño-3.4 at lags from −6 to +12 months (Figure 2 shows the global terms), the Andes torque is significantly positive at every lag from −6 to +7 months (the global mountain torque −5 to +2 months; robust in CMIP6 from −6 to +6 months), and friction is significantly negative from −6 to +7 months, mostly in the tropics, where the trades weaken (the 30°S–0 and 0–30°N friction rows of the table).

The two data sets agree on the mountain torque and its geography: Andes +1.92 in ERA5 and +0.98 in CMIP6 (100% of models), Rockies +0.71 and +0.58, Himalaya/Tibet −0.37 in CMIP6 (not significant in ERA5). They differ on friction. In the models friction takes back about 58% of the mountain torque, and the remainder, +0.66, is about 1.6 times the models' tendency, +0.42. In ERA5 friction takes back more than all of it, −1.86 against +1.44, so the torques sum to −0.33 (not significant) while angular momentum rises significantly; the difference sits in ERA5's residual (+0.80, not significant on its own), mostly the analysis increments (section 7). ERA5's monthly budget otherwise closes reasonably (correlation of tendency with summed torques 0.87, slope 0.74), but its residual is slow (lag-one autocorrelation 0.90), which is what an interannual imbalance looks like.

Composites of El Niño onsets tell the same story without reaching significance in ERA5's small sample (13 onsets against 14 neutral years, April–December): the change in angular momentum, the summed torques and the mountain torque differ by +0.56, +3.65 and +3.46 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ with p = 0.37, p = 0.22 and p = 0.15; only gravity-wave drag is significant (+0.65, p < 0.001) and it is small. In CMIP6 the onset composites are robust: angular momentum +1.01, mountain torque +2.52 and the Andes +1.23; friction (−0.93, 75% agreement) is not robust.

4. The Andes: a standing shift, not more events

This is the pressure difference of section 1 seen range by range: a slow shift that forms as the Pacific warms and lasts the season, so the torque it makes is positive day after day, rather than more or stronger weather events.

The synoptic events do not change with El Niño (Figure 4, middle and bottom). Positive Andes events in DJF average 7.00 a season in El Niño winters and 7.07 in neutral ones (−0.07 [−0.72, +0.59], p = 0.85); the Rockies differ by −0.60 (p = 0.27) and the Himalaya by +0.96 (p = 0.07, not significant after the FDR). Their amplitude does not change either (Andes DJF −0.12 Hadley, p = 0.87). Across all 24 cells, 0 event-count, 0 amplitude and 0 variance differences are significant. Against the ONI as a continuous index, 3 of 24 cells show a significant change in event count, all small and of mixed sign (Himalaya/Tibet MAM −0.75 per unit ONI; Andes SON +0.35 per unit ONI; Alps/Europe MAM +0.60 per unit ONI).

The standing part does change (Figure 4, top). Against the ONI it is significant in 7 cells, six of them large: the Andes in DJF (+1.9 Hadley per unit ONI [+1.2, +2.4]) and JJA (+2.3 [+1.1, +4.2]), the Rockies in DJF (+1.1 [+0.6, +1.6]) and MAM (+1.1 [+0.6, +1.8]), and the Himalaya/Tibet in MAM (−1.2 [−1.9, −0.6]) and SON (−0.8 [−1.4, −0.3]); the seventh is the Alps in SON (+0.19 [+0.02, +0.39]). Nearly all of the El Niño difference in the seasonal-mean torque is this standing part; the windows around positive events contribute nothing significant:

Why El Niño winters look eventful. Counted on the total anomaly, Andes DJF has 7.25 positive events per season in El Niño winters against 5.33 in neutral ones (p = 0.009, just short of the FDR) and 2.3 fewer negative ones (p < 0.001). Shifting each neutral winter by the El Niño–neutral difference in the standing part predicts 6.60; the excess, +0.65 [−0.47, +1.47], is not significant (p = 0.31), nor is it for the Rockies (−0.17, p = 0.73). In the models the extra threshold events are robust (Andes DJF +0.90 a season, 100% of models; Rockies DJF +0.75, 84%) while the synoptic counts are not (the strongest, Himalaya DJF, +0.42 with 79% agreement). Two cells go the other way: in El Niño springs the Himalaya/Tibet has fewer events than the shift alone gives (−1.69 [−2.50, −0.78], significant after the FDR), as does Greenland in SON (−1.33).

5. Which comes first

Figure 7 correlates each range's synoptic torque, day by day, with what the atmosphere does before and after. High torque follows a few days of low global angular momentum (lowest correlation −0.30 at lags of −2 to −4 days) and comes before a rise (up to +0.45 2 days later). The zonal-mean subtropical jet follows weakly: +0.15 1 day after Rockies torque in DJF and +0.14 5 days after Andes torque in DJF; the Himalayan torque in DJF shows nothing in the zonal mean (largest −0.07). Before Andes torque the jet is, if anything, slightly weaker (−0.11, 1 day before). The torque leads; it is not a response to a jet that is already stronger.

The precursor is the textbook one for a lee-side surge: a ridge upstream and poleward of the range and a trough downstream, over the Urals and northern China for the Himalaya, Alaska and the central United States for the Rockies, and southern Chile and the southwest Atlantic for the Andes (57%, 50% and 47% of each map significant). El Niño does not change it: no cell of the El Niño minus neutral difference is significant for any of the three ranges in DJF (3% of the map for the Andes in JJA). Projecting each day's 500 hPa field on the precursor pattern and counting its peaks, El Niño seasons have no significantly different number of precursors and no different share that are followed by an event within four days (all 10 tests not significant after the FDR; the closest are −1.01 precursors a season over the Himalaya in MAM, p = 0.05, and a −0.16 hit rate over the Rockies in DJF, p = 0.04).

6. The 2026 event

By the CPC ONI, 2026's El Niño grew from +0.46 in MAM to +2.16 in JAS. In May the Andes torque was +13.3 Hadley above its seasonal normal, the second-largest May since 1959 after May 1997 (+14.0), the start of that year's very strong El Niño. Over 15 April – 19 September the Andes torque anomaly added +5.6 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ (the second-largest of 48 years, z = +1.8; +4.4 with ERA5's torque trend removed), against +1.0 in the average El Niño onset.

The budget does not close. The angular momentum anomaly rose by +6.0 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ over the window while the torque anomalies summed to −0.9: the Andes term ran close to the angular momentum curve, mountain torque outside the six boxes added +3.4, but the other five ranges (−3.9) and friction in the tropics (−4.5) worked against it (Figure 9). That residual, +6.9, is not unusual for ERA5: the same window leaves +2.2 ± 4.6 in 1979–2025, so 2026 sits +1.0 standard deviations from the usual. The honest reading is that ERA5 cannot attribute the 2026 rise: the Andes signal is real and among the largest on record, but in ERA5 the torques as a whole explain none of the rise, and the analysis increments carry it (section 7). In the models, where nothing is added between time steps, friction gives back only about 58% of a mountain torque of this kind.

The analysis-increment floor. With every hour of 2026 the budget can be followed through ERA5's twelve-hour 4D-Var windows (Figure 11). Inside a window the residual is +6.3 ± 2.4 Hadley each hour. In the hour from 09 to 10 UTC it jumps to +173 ± 87 and from 21 to 22 UTC to +62 ± 93: measured against the in-window mean, the analysis adds +0.060 and +0.020 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ of angular momentum at each start. A three-hourly product sees the same thing (+42 ± 35 Hadley across window starts against +7.0 ± 2.9 inside). The daily means of the hourly residual are +15.6 ± 4.4 Hadley, with almost no memory from day to day (lag-one correlation 0.10); its spectrum is a comb of lines at whole cycles per day on a low floor, and at periods longer than about two days the torques carry nearly all of the variance of the tendency.

GFZ as a check. The GFZ effective angular momentum functions (Dobslaw et al., 2010), built from ECMWF's operational analysis every three hours with the pressure tides removed and an inverted-barometer ocean, put the absolute change over the window at +1.73 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ (wind term +0.83, mass term +0.90) against +2.79 from ERA5's daily means of hourly fields; their daily tendencies correlate at 0.90. The mass terms are not the same quantity: GFZ's includes the ocean's inverted-barometer response.

7. Why a reanalysis budget does not close: ERA5's analysis increments

ERA5's open archive carries no analysis-increment fields, but its hourly fields jump at each 4D-Var window start, so the increment can be estimated from the jump. For any band's angular momentum $X$ with torques $T$, let $R(h) = X(h) - X(h-1) - T(h)\,\Delta t$ be the residual of the hour ending at $h$; the increment at the 09 UTC window start is

$$J_{10} = R(10) - \tfrac{1}{2}\left[R(09) + R(11)\right],$$

and likewise $J_{22}$ from 20–23 UTC: the window-start hour minus the mean of the hours either side, which removes the smooth part of everything that is not an increment (the transport of angular momentum between bands, the tides, the local trend). The torques themselves barely change across the jump (−0.0005 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹, negligible). The surface fields were read at 08–11 and 20–23 UTC for every day from 2025-01-01 to 2026-10-03 (641 days) and integrated in 5° bands; the 37-level winds at the same hours give the vertical structure (640 days), and the same estimator applied to the wind itself on a 2.5° grid, every third day, gives the maps. Intervals are 10-day block bootstraps over days; the FDR is held at 10% across bands, across levels and bands for the section, and across grid cells for the maps. A jump is not purely an increment: it is the step between two consecutive analysis trajectories, so it also contains any adjustment of the model in the first hour of the new trajectory. And ERA5 is IFS cycle 41r2 (Hersbach et al., 2020): the increments describe that model, before ECMWF's 2022 revision of orographic drag (Kanehama et al., 2022).

How much and where. Summed over the bands, the analysis adds +0.048 [+0.044, +0.052] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ of angular momentum at the 09 UTC start and +0.022 [+0.016, +0.027] at 21 UTC, about +0.070 a day, the equivalent of a steady torque of +8.1 Hadley. (The global series of section 6, with the in-window mean as the baseline, gave +0.060 and +0.020; the two estimators differ in the baseline, not in substance.) The wind term is the same at both starts, +0.050 [+0.046, +0.054] and +0.050 [+0.045, +0.055]; the difference between them is in the mass term, −0.002 [−0.004, −0.001] at 09 UTC against −0.029 [−0.030, −0.027] at 21 UTC, a surface-pressure increment. Where the low-level increment goes

The maps test the drag hypothesis directly: if ERA5's model lost momentum mainly to excess low-level orographic drag (blocking and turbulent orographic form drag), the low-level increments would sit over and just downstream of the Rockies, the Andes, Tibet, the East African highlands and the Antarctic margins. They do not. At 925 hPa the analysis adds westerly momentum over most of the trade-wind oceans and around Antarctica; at 850 hPa it removes it over the subtropical oceans, the upper half of the dipole in Figure 14. In the zonal mean the 1000 hPa wind is increased by +0.31 m s⁻¹ a day at 10–30° (weaker trade easterlies) and decreased by −0.14 m s⁻¹ at 40–60° (weaker surface westerlies); the 10 m wind likewise (+0.18 and −0.11). Over the mid-latitude oceans the analysis adds westerlies from 925 hPa up (+0.08 m s⁻¹ a day at 40–60°), while at 850 hPa between 40 and 55°N it removes them (−0.11).

Over and downstream of rough terrain, 22% of the globe, the analysis puts 6% [4, 8] of its low-level angular momentum, less than an even spread would give; even in northern winter, when blocking drag matters most, the share is 15% [13, 16], and in JJA it is negative. The tropical, subtropical and polar oceans outside 30–65° take 89% [83, 96], most of it in the trade-wind boundary layer. In the whole column the storm-track oceans at 30–65° dominate (56% [54, 59] on 28% of the area).

Possible explanations

Models are tuned toward more drag. The parameters that set parametrised surface drag "are poorly constrained and often tuned to obtain the desired model behaviour, i.e. forecast skill or mean climate" (Sandu et al., 2016, technical memorandum version), and models of comparable resolution differ over land by up to 20% in zonal-mean subgrid surface stress, and more in how it is split between schemes. At ECMWF the turbulent orographic form drag coefficient "has been increased over time to optimise forecast skill": in cycle 40r1 it and the blocking coefficient were raised to offset the loss of skill from reducing turbulent diffusion in stable layers, a repartition "purely made to optimise skill, in the absence of any constraints on the drag processes", which left the IFS with much more form-drag stress than the Met Office model; ECMWF also reports IFS surface winds that are too weak over orography (Kanehama et al., 2022). ERA5's cycle 41r2 came after 40r1 and before the 2022 revision that reduced form drag again. Earlier, budget studies of model drift had pushed models towards more low-level and less stratospheric drag (Klinker and Sardeshmukh, 1992, as summarised by Brown, 2004), and Brown (2004) found the parametrised orographic torque excessive at low resolution between 20 and 50°N in winter. Operational experience with GEM is similar: more drag gave better scores at the cost of too-weak 850 hPa flow in places. None of these sources measures what drag does to a model's angular momentum budget; that a model tuned this way loses westerly momentum, which the analysis then puts back, is an inference, and ERA5's increments are consistent with it.

But not in the form of low-level orographic drag. The maps put the low-level increment over the oceans, not the mountains, and near the surface they weaken both the trades and the mid-latitude westerlies: the model's surface winds over the oceans are too strong, not too weak. In ERA5 the 850 hPa westerlies of the northern mid-latitudes are, if anything, too strong as well, unlike the GEM experience. What the increments describe is an oceanic boundary layer that carries too much momentum down to the surface (or surface-wind observations, from scatterometers and buoys, that pull the surface winds down), with too little left above it, and an extratropical free troposphere that is short of westerly momentum. Too strong a surface wind over the westerly belts also means too much surface stress and so too much friction torque: drag of the boundary-layer kind rather than the orographic kind remains a candidate for the lost momentum.

Gravity-wave drag aloft. COORDE (van Niekerk et al., 2020) found, as Kanehama et al. (2022) summarise it, that the IFS, like several other models, underestimates orographic gravity-wave drag in the lower stratosphere. On its own that would leave too much westerly momentum there and the analysis would have to remove it. Over 25–50°N between 50 and 150 hPa, the latitudes of the Himalaya and the Rockies, the increments instead add westerly momentum: +0.0031 [+0.0027, +0.0036] at 09 UTC and +0.0042 [+0.0037, +0.0048] at 21 UTC, against +0.0065 [+0.0052, +0.0077] and +0.0095 [+0.0085, +0.0105] in the troposphere below 400 hPa. A zonal mean cannot isolate the air over and downstream of the ranges, which is where COORDE looked, so this tests the zonal-mean budget, not COORDE's regional result.

The steady residual inside the windows. Within a window the hourly residual is +6.3 Hadley. Regressing it on each range's mountain torque, the friction and the gravity-wave drag (2026, 4,773 hours, day-block bootstrap) explains 17% of its variance:

A coefficient of +1% means the residual behaves as if the model's own torque were 1% larger than the one recomputed here from the 0.25° output. The "share" is the coefficient times that term's mean torque.

The residual is mostly a constant (+11.3 Hadley), not a scaled copy of any range's torque: the largest significant range coefficients are a few per cent and of both signs, and together with friction and gravity-wave drag they account for −5.0 Hadley of the mean. A constant offset between the model's own mountain torque on its N320 grid and the torque recomputed from 0.25° output is plausible, since the mean mountain torque changes by tens of Hadley between 0.25° and 1° (section 9), and so is non-conservation in the semi-Lagrangian dynamics; the vertical integrals cannot tell them apart. Nor can they give the latitude structure of this in-window residual: a band's budget also contains the transport of angular momentum across its edges, which needs the 3-D wind. Increments and initial tendencies have long been read as diagnostics of model error in this way (Klinker and Sardeshmukh, 1992; Rodwell and Palmer, 2007).

The forecast side

A physics model whose torques drain angular momentum should lose it in free forecasts, and IFS-ENS does: in the site's winter 2025–26 test it loses angular momentum steadily, −0.31 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ by day 15, while closing its own torque budget almost exactly (Do AI weather models conserve angular momentum?): its torques take the momentum out. That is the forecast-side counterpart of ERA5's positive increments. The data-driven AIFS-ENS, trained on analyses that already contain the increments' correction, shows no global drain in this site's archive (119 to 145 forecasts per lead, inits from 20 July to 11 October 2026): +0.021 [+0.007, +0.039] ×10²⁵ at day 1, then about −0.03 and not significant through day 15 (−0.033 [−0.188, +0.123]). It redistributes angular momentum between the hemispheres instead: the Northern Hemisphere gains (+0.102 [+0.023, +0.172] at day 15, significant from day 14) and the Southern loses (−0.042 [−0.068, −0.017] at day 7, significant at 8 of the 13 leads from day 3; −0.136 [−0.297, +0.020] at day 15). The comparison is not like for like: different seasons (December–February against July–October), different IFS cycles (ERA5 is cycle 41r2), and an increment is not a free-forecast drift.

9. Caveats

  • The mean mountain torque is not resolution-converged. Averaging orography and surface pressure together to 1° or 2.5° moves the global mean from about +1 to −26 and −33 Hadley and the Andes box from −223 to −148, while the anomalies correlate at 0.96 or better with the 0.25° values and their ENSO regressions barely change (global +1.00, +1.07 and +0.99 Hadley per kelvin). Anomalies from CMIP6's 1–2.5° grids are therefore comparable with ERA5's; absolute values are not. The Andes DJF standing shift at 2.5° is +2.1 Hadley (p = 0.009), short of the FDR.
  • Tide aliasing. Twice-daily sampling at 00 and 12 UTC does not remove the semidiurnal tide; four times a day does, and hourly removes the rest (the four-versus-24 offsets are below 0.2 Hadley).
  • What CMIP6's tauu is. It is the surface (boundary-layer) stress. The Met Office models state that it includes turbulent form drag from small hills; parameterised orographic gravity-wave drag is reported separately (utendogw, in 13 models). ERA5's turbulent stress likewise includes turbulent orographic form drag. Because the gravity-wave-drag contribution to the El Niño rise is not significant in either data set, the answer does not depend on where it is booked.
  • ERA5's torques drift. The global mountain and friction torques trend by +0.5 and +0.8 Hadley per decade over 1979–2025 with no matching trend in angular momentum, so they are detrended for the ENSO analysis; the 2026 attribution is shown both ways.
  • ENSO phase is the ONI of each season against fixed thresholds, not CPC's rule of five overlapping seasons, and the CMIP6 models use ±0.5 K on their own index whatever the size of their ENSO.
  • Model independence. Several CMIP6 models share code (four CESM2 variants, NorESM2 and others built on CAM, three MPI models), so "41 models" overstates the independent evidence; a one-model-per-centre check was not made.
  • Samples. Super El Niños (ONI ≥ 2.0; three DJFs) are too few to test and are not used as evidence. The zonal-mean jet comes from the local ERA5 store, northern hemisphere 1979–2023 and southern 1991–2020. The torque boxes are the site's and do not close the terrain at their edges, so a box's mean includes part of the large-scale pressure gradient over high ground.
