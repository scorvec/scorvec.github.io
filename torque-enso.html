Why the atmosphere spins up in El Niño

In El Niño the westerlies strengthen and the day lengthens by a fraction of a millisecond: the atmosphere takes angular momentum from the solid Earth. This study traces how it gets in, mainly through a pressure difference across the Andes that lasts for months, and why ERA5's budget closes only with its analysis increments. Data: ERA5 four times a day for 1959–2026, 41 CMIP6 models and an hourly budget of 2026.

The short answer

  • 1. The Andes torque supplies it; friction brakes it. As the eastern Pacific warms, the southeast Pacific high west of the Andes weakens and pressure rises over eastern South America. Pressure stays higher on the east side of the range for months, so the mountain torque stays positive. In December–February the Andes torque is 2.9 Hadley higher than in neutral winters (p = 0.007; CMIP6 +1.46, all 19 models with daily data agreeing in sign). It is significantly positive from at least six months ahead of Niño-3.4 to seven months after it. Over the six months leading Niño-3.4, mountain torque supplies +1.44 [+0.44, +2.50] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin of Niño-3.4 in ERA5 and +1.54 in CMIP6, with the Andes the largest source. The weaker trades hand the atmosphere less westerly momentum, so friction takes back about 58% of the mountain torque in the models and more than all of it in ERA5. The individual east-side highs are no more frequent or stronger.
  • 2. ERA5's model loses westerly momentum, and the analysis puts it back. ERA5's torques fall 11.3 Hadley short of the annual budget. At its two daily 4D-Var window starts the analysis adds angular momentum, together the equivalent of a steady torque of about 8 Hadley, in every month of 2025–26. The physics ensemble IFS-ENS loses angular momentum in free forecasts through its own torques; the data-driven AIFS-ENS does not. Too much drag in ERA5's model (IFS cycle 41r2, before the orographic-drag revision ECMWF made operational in cycle 48r1 in June 2023) is the leading candidate, not the only one. About 74% of the momentum the analysis adds goes into mid-latitude westerlies, 87% of that between 650 and 100 hPa; in the tropics the gain below 875 hPa and the loss at 850–700 hPa nearly cancel.
  • 3. Too much drag, but not over the mountains. ECMWF's own record (Kanehama et al., 2022) shows the IFS tuned toward more drag because it improved scores. Yet only 6% [4, 8] of the momentum the analysis adds below 850 hPa lands over or just downstream of rough terrain, which covers 22% of the globe; 89% [83, 96] lands over the tropical and other oceans. In the tropics the whole trade-wind boundary layer is too fast: the analysis slows the trades from the surface to about 875 hPa, more than it speeds them up at 850–700 hPa. In mid-latitudes the boundary layer is too slow and the air just above it too fast, and the column balances. Neither pattern sits over mountains, and only the mid-latitude one looks like too much drag.
  • Which comes first. Day to day, mountain torque leads the rise in global angular momentum, as the budget requires, and the subtropical jet follows only weakly (r ≈ 0.15). The 500 hPa pattern that sets up an east-side high (a ridge upstream, a trough downstream) is the same in El Niño and neutral years.

1. What angular momentum is, and why it rises in El Niño

Angular momentum about the Earth's axis measures how much faster than the planet the atmosphere turns. Most of it is in the westerly winds, weighted toward low latitudes, which are farther from the axis; a smaller mass term depends on how the air is spread between latitudes. The atmosphere can gain it only from the solid Earth and oceans, so when the atmosphere spins up the Earth slows and the day lengthens. Rosen et al. (1984) showed this in the 1982–83 El Niño; Stefanick (1982) had already tied year-to-year swings in angular momentum and day length to the Southern Oscillation. Here 10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ of extra atmospheric angular momentum lengthens the day by about 0.17 ms.

Why El Niño spins the atmosphere up. El Niño warms the whole tropical troposphere, with bands of cooling in the middle latitudes of both hemispheres (Seager et al., 2003). By the thermal-wind relation the stronger temperature gradient strengthens the upper westerlies on the equatorward side of both subtropical jets. In ERA5, global angular momentum regressed on Niño-3.4 peaks 1 month after the Niño-3.4 anomaly, at +0.69 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin (0.11 ms of day length).

From warm ocean to faster winds. The thermal wind says where the westerlies strengthen, not where their momentum comes from. Without a torque, stronger jets would have to be paid for by easterlies elsewhere. The momentum comes from the solid Earth, mostly across the Andes, in four steps. (1) The central and eastern Pacific warm. (2) The southeast Pacific high west of the Andes weakens and pressure rises over eastern South America; in northern winter the deeper Aleutian low does the same west of the Rockies (Figure 6). (3) Pressure stays higher on the east side of both ranges for months. (4) Their mountain torque therefore stays positive and moves westerly momentum into the atmosphere. Friction is the brake, because the weaker trades hand the atmosphere less westerly momentum. The rise is what the mountain torque supplies minus what friction holds back (section 3).

What the record shows. GFZ Potsdam's record of angular momentum, computed from ECMWF's analyses independently of this study's ERA5 calculation, shows the rise plainly (Figure 1). Since 1979 global angular momentum has been above normal in 84% of El Niño seasons and below normal in 84% of La Niña seasons. On average it is +0.78 [+0.51, +1.02] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ in El Niño and −0.69 [−0.91, −0.48] in La Niña, a swing of about 0.24 ms in day length. It rises by +0.73 [+0.59, +0.83] ×10²⁵ per kelvin of the ONI (r = 0.71, p < 0.001), and the GFZ and ERA5 monthly anomalies correlate at 0.99. Intraseasonal swings, the MJO among them (Weickmann et al., 1997), still move it by a few tenths from month to month. In July–September 2026 it was 3.1 ×10²⁵ above normal, second only to early 1983 and about twice what an ONI of 2.2 K (provisional) alone predicts (section 6).

How the momentum gets in. All of it has to come through the surface:

$$\frac{dM}{dt} = T_{\text{mountain}} + T_{\text{friction}} + T_{\text{gravity-wave drag}},\qquad T_{\text{mountain}} = -\iint p_s\,\frac{\partial h}{\partial\lambda}\,dA = \iint h\,\frac{\partial p_s}{\partial \lambda}\,dA .$$

Torques are in Hadley (10¹⁸ N m); one Hadley held for a day changes $M$ by 0.0086 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹. The mountain torque is positive when pressure is higher on the east slope of a range than on the west: the air pushes westward on the mountain, and the mountain pushes the air eastward. Through friction, surface easterlies (the trades) give the atmosphere westerly momentum and surface westerlies take it back. Gravity-wave drag is the model's account of unresolved mountains. Cold highs banked against the east side of a range, such as cold surges east of the Rockies (Colle and Mass, 1995), cold-air incursions east of the Andes (Garreaud, 2000) and the East Asian surges, supply most of the day-to-day mountain torque and much of the week-to-week swing in angular momentum (Iskenderian and Salstein, 1998; Weickmann, 2003; Lott et al., 2004). See the mountain torque and angular momentum explainers.

Two questions are less settled. Does El Niño's extra momentum come through those weather events or through the time-mean pressure pattern? And how does it split between mountain and friction torque? Reanalysis budgets do not close exactly (Huang et al., 1999; Egger et al., 2007), so climate models, which have no analysis increments, are the cross-check.

2. Data and method

  • ERA5 (Hersbach et al., 2020) from the ARCO-ERA5 store on Google Cloud (Carver and Merose, 2023), read in GitHub Actions: surface pressure, the turbulent and gravity-wave surface stresses and the vertical integral of eastward mass flux (an exact full-column wind term). Data are at 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC from 1 January 1959 to 3 October 2026 (24,748 days, none missing); the last two to three months are ECMWF's preliminary ERA5T release. Four times a day rather than two, for the reason in section 8. The mass term of $M$ comes from surface pressure. For 2026 the fields were also read hourly from 1 March. The analysis period is 1979–2025, with 1959–2025 as a sensitivity test.

  • Tests: each season-year is one unit. El Niño minus neutral differences are tested by permuting the phase labels (20,000 draws), with bootstrap 95% intervals; regressions on the ONI use a moving-block bootstrap (blocks of three season-years; Künsch, 1989). The false discovery rate is held at 10% across the 24 range–season cells of each statistic (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Wilks, 2016). Monthly regressions on the detrended ERSSTv6 (Huang et al., 2025) Niño-3.4 anomaly at lags of −6 to +12 months use a 24-month block bootstrap (2,000 draws), with the FDR across lags. Maps are tested per grid cell with the FDR over the field.
  • CMIP6 historical runs 1950–2014 from the Pangeo Google Cloud mirror (Eyring et al., 2016; Abernathey et al., 2021): 41 models and 103 members (up to three per model), each on its own grid with its own orography. The fields are monthly mountain torque from surface pressure (the torque is linear in $p_s$, so monthly means suffice), surface stress tauu, angular momentum from ua masked by surface pressure, and Niño-3.4 from ts. 13 models also publish the orographic gravity-wave-drag tendency utendogw (Gerber and Manzini, 2016). 19 have daily surface pressure, which gives event statistics on each model's own ENSO. A model result is robust when the across-model test passes the FDR and at least 80% of models share the sign.
  • Analysis increments (section 7): surface fields at 08–11 and 20–23 UTC every day from 1 January 2025 to 3 October 2026 (641 days), integrated in 5° bands, and the 37-level winds at the same hours (640 days; on a 2.5° grid every third day for the maps). Intervals are 10-day block bootstraps over days, with the FDR at 10% across bands, across levels and bands for the section, and across grid cells for the maps. The vertical sum of the 37-level increment tracks the column estimate from the surface fields (correlation 0.997) but is about 15% larger (+0.058 against +0.050 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per window), probably because 37 pressure levels do not integrate the model levels exactly.

3. How the momentum gets in: the torque budget

Summing each term's regression on Niño-3.4 over lags −6 to −1 months gives the angular momentum it supplies in the six months before the anomaly (Figure 3 and the table). In ERA5 the tendency supplies +0.47 [+0.32, +0.63] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin. The regression of angular momentum itself rises by +0.42 between those lags; the two differ because the tendencies are taken across month edges. The mass term is negligible (about +0.02 per kelvin).

Mountain torque is the source and friction the brake, and both act for months (Figure 2). The Andes torque is significantly positive at every lag from −6 to +7 months. The global mountain torque is significant from −5 to +2 months and robust in CMIP6 from −6 to +6 months. Friction is significantly negative from −6 to +7 months, mostly in the tropics, where the trades weaken.

ERA5 and CMIP6 agree on the mountain torque and where it acts: Andes +1.92 in ERA5 and +0.98 in CMIP6 (100% of models), Rockies +0.71 and +0.58. They differ on friction. In the models friction takes back about 58% of the mountain torque, leaving +0.66, about 1.6 times the models' tendency of +0.42. In ERA5 friction takes back more than all of it (−1.86 against +1.44). ERA5's torques therefore sum to nothing significant while angular momentum rises significantly. The gap sits in the budget residual, which is itself not significant; whether ERA5's analysis increments (section 7) vary with ENSO was not tested. The monthly budget otherwise closes reasonably (correlation of tendency with summed torques 0.87, slope 0.74), but the residual is slow (lag-one autocorrelation 0.90), as an interannual imbalance would be.

In ERA5's composites of El Niño onsets (13 onsets against 14 neutral years, April–December) only gravity-wave drag differs significantly (+0.65 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹, p < 0.001); the sample is too small for the other terms. In CMIP6 the onset composites are robust: angular momentum +1.01, mountain torque +2.52 and the Andes +1.23. CMIP6 friction (−0.93, 75% agreement) is not robust.

4. The Andes: a standing shift, not more events

Range by range, the signal is the pressure difference of section 1: a slow shift that forms as the Pacific warms and lasts the season. It is not more or stronger weather events.

The synoptic events do not change (Figure 4, middle and bottom). Positive Andes events in DJF average 7.00 a season in El Niño winters and 7.07 in neutral ones (p = 0.85), and their amplitude does not change (p = 0.87). Across the 24 range–season cells, none of the 72 differences in event count, amplitude and variance is significant. Against the ONI, 3 of 24 cells show a significant change in event count, all small and of mixed sign (Himalaya/Tibet MAM −0.75 per unit ONI; Andes SON +0.35 per unit ONI; Alps MAM +0.60 per unit ONI).

The standing part does change (Figure 4, top). Against the ONI it is significant in 7 cells, six of them large: the Andes in DJF (+1.9 Hadley per unit ONI [+1.2, +2.4]) and JJA (+2.3 [+1.1, +4.2]), the Rockies in DJF (+1.1 [+0.6, +1.6]) and MAM (+1.1 [+0.6, +1.8]), and the Himalaya/Tibet in MAM (−1.2 [−1.9, −0.6]) and SON (−0.8 [−1.4, −0.3]); the seventh is the Alps in SON (+0.19 [+0.02, +0.39]). Nearly all of the El Niño difference in the seasonal-mean torque is this standing part:

Why El Niño winters look eventful. Counted on the total anomaly, Andes DJF has 7.25 positive events a season in El Niño winters against 5.33 in neutral ones (p = 0.009, just short of the FDR). Two cells go the other way, with fewer events than the shift alone gives: the Himalaya/Tibet in spring (−1.69 [−2.50, −0.78]) and Greenland in SON (−1.33), both significant after the FDR.

5. Which comes first

Day by day (Figure 7), high torque follows a few days of low global angular momentum (lowest correlation −0.30 at lags of −2 to −4 days) and comes before a rise (up to +0.45, 2 days later). The zonal-mean subtropical jet follows weakly, peaking 1 day after Rockies torque (r = +0.15) and 5 days after Andes torque (r = +0.14) in DJF, while the Himalayan torque in DJF shows almost nothing. Before Andes torque the jet is slightly weaker (r = −0.11, 1 day before). That the torque leads angular momentum follows from the budget itself; the jet result says the torque is not a response to a jet that is already stronger, but the jet's response is small (about 2% of its day-to-day variance).

The precursor is the textbook one for a lee-side surge: a ridge upstream and poleward of the range and a trough downstream. It sits over the Urals and northern China for the Himalaya, Alaska and the central United States for the Rockies, and southern Chile and the southwest Atlantic for the Andes (57%, 50% and 47% of each map significant). El Niño does not change it: no cell of the El Niño minus neutral difference is significant for any of the three ranges in DJF (3% of the map for the Andes in JJA). El Niño seasons also have no significantly different number of precursors, and no different share of them followed by an event within four days (none of 10 tests significant after the FDR).

6. The 2026 event

By the CPC ONI, 2026's El Niño grew from +0.46 in MAM to +2.16 in JAS (provisional: CPC revises the latest seasons for about two months). In May the Andes torque was +13.3 Hadley above normal, the second-largest May since 1959 after May 1997 (+14.0), the start of that year's very strong El Niño. Over 15 April – 19 September the Andes torque anomaly added +5.6 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ (the second-largest of 48 years, z = +1.8; +4.4 with ERA5's torque trend removed), against +1.0 in the average El Niño onset.

The budget does not close. The angular momentum anomaly rose by +6.0 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ while the torque anomalies summed to −0.9 (Figure 9). The Andes term tracked angular momentum and mountain torque outside the six boxes added +3.4, but the other five ranges (−3.9) and tropical friction (−4.5) worked against it. A residual of +6.9 is not unusual for ERA5: the same window leaves +2.2 ± 4.6 in 1979–2025, so 2026 is +1.0 standard deviations from the usual. ERA5 therefore cannot attribute the 2026 rise. The Andes signal is real and among the largest on record, but the torques as a whole explain none of the rise, and the analysis increments carry it (section 7).

The analysis-increment floor. Hourly fields let the budget be followed through ERA5's twelve-hour 4D-Var windows (Figure 11). Inside a window the residual is +6.3 ± 2.4 Hadley each hour. In the hours from 09 to 10 UTC and 21 to 22 UTC it jumps to +173 ± 87 and +62 ± 93: the analysis adds +0.060 and +0.020 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ at each window start. At periods longer than about two days the torques carry nearly all of the tendency's variance.

GFZ as a check. GFZ's functions, from ECMWF's operational analysis every three hours with the pressure tides removed and an inverted-barometer ocean, put the change over the window at +1.73 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ (wind term +0.83, mass term +0.90), against +2.79 from ERA5's daily means of hourly fields. Their daily tendencies correlate at 0.90. The mass terms differ by construction, since GFZ's includes the ocean's inverted-barometer response.

7. Why a reanalysis budget does not close: ERA5's analysis increments

ERA5's open archive has no analysis-increment fields, but its hourly fields jump at each 4D-Var window start, so the increment can be estimated from the jump. With $R(h) = X(h) - X(h-1) - T(h)\,\Delta t$ the budget residual of the hour ending at $h$, for a band's angular momentum $X$ and torques $T$, the increment at the 09 UTC window start is

$$J_{10} = R(10) - \tfrac{1}{2}\left[R(09) + R(11)\right],$$

and likewise $J_{22}$ at 21 UTC. Subtracting the hours either side removes whatever changes smoothly (transport between bands, the tides, trends); the torques barely change across the jump (−0.0005 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹). A jump also holds any model adjustment in the first hour of the new trajectory, and it describes ERA5's model, IFS cycle 41r2 (Hersbach et al., 2020), before the orographic-drag revision of IFS cycle 48r1 (operational June 2023; Kanehama et al., 2022). Data and tests: section 2.

How much and where. The analysis adds +0.048 [+0.044, +0.052] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ at the 09 UTC start and +0.022 [+0.016, +0.027] at 21 UTC, about +0.070 a day, equal to a steady torque of +8.1 Hadley (section 6's in-window baseline gives +0.060 and +0.020). The wind term is the same at both starts, +0.050 [+0.046, +0.054] and +0.050 [+0.045, +0.055]; the difference is a surface-pressure (mass-term) increment, −0.002 [−0.004, −0.001] at 09 UTC against −0.029 [−0.030, −0.027] at 21 UTC. Through the year. The global increment is positive in every month, smallest in northern summer (July 2025: +0.024 ×10²⁵ a day) and largest in northern spring (March 2026: +0.111), with the extratropical gain and tropical loss in every month (Figure 13).

In the tropics the whole trade-wind boundary layer is too fast, and the too-slow layer above it offsets about 72% of that, so the lower troposphere carries too much easterly momentum. That fits too little drag on the trades, or too-strong low-level pressure gradients; the increments cannot tell them apart. In mid-latitudes the 1000 hPa westerlies are slightly too strong, but the boundary layer as a whole is too slow and the layer above it too fast, and the column below 700 hPa balances: momentum is not mixed down into the boundary layer fast enough, or is taken out of it too fast.

Where the low-level increment goes

If ERA5's model lost momentum mainly to excess low-level orographic drag (blocking and turbulent orographic form drag), the low-level increments would sit over and just downstream of the Rockies, the Andes, Tibet, the East African highlands and the Antarctic margins. They do not. At 925 hPa the analysis adds westerly momentum over most of the trade-wind oceans and around Antarctica; at 850 hPa it removes it over the subtropical oceans. In the zonal mean the 1000 hPa wind rises by +0.31 m s⁻¹ a day at 10–30° (weaker trade easterlies) and falls by 0.14 m s⁻¹ at 40–60° (weaker surface westerlies), and the 10 m wind does the same (+0.18 and −0.11). In mid-latitudes the analysis adds westerlies at 925 hPa (+0.08 m s⁻¹ a day at 40–60°) but removes them at 850 hPa between 40 and 55°N (−0.11).

Possible explanations

Models are tuned toward more drag. Surface-drag parameters "are poorly constrained and often tuned to obtain the desired model behavior" (Sandu et al., 2016). ECMWF raised its turbulent orographic form drag "to optimise forecast skill", notably in cycle 40r1, which left the IFS with much more form-drag stress than the Met Office model; by 2022 its surface winds were also too weak over orography (Kanehama et al., 2022). ERA5's cycle 41r2 came between that change and the cycle 48r1 revision (operational June 2023) that reduced form drag again. Budget studies of model drift had earlier pushed models toward less stratospheric and more low-level drag (Klinker and Sardeshmukh, 1992, as summarised by Brown, 2004). No source measures what such tuning does to a model's angular momentum budget, so the link to the lost westerly momentum is an inference, one that ERA5's increments are consistent with.

But not as low-level orographic drag. The increments weaken the surface winds over the oceans, both the trades and the westerlies, so the model's ocean surface winds are too strong, not too weak. Its northern mid-latitude 850 hPa westerlies are, if anything, too strong as well. Scatterometer and buoy winds pulling the surface winds down would look the same at 10 m and 1000 hPa, but would not by themselves speed up the mid-latitude wind at 950–900 hPa. Too-strong surface westerlies also mean too much friction torque, so boundary-layer drag, rather than orographic drag, remains a candidate for the lost momentum.

Gravity-wave drag aloft. COORDE (van Niekerk et al., 2020) found the IFS, like several other models, short of orographic gravity-wave drag in the lower stratosphere (as summarised by Kanehama et al., 2022), which would leave excess westerly momentum there for the analysis to remove. Over 25–50°N at 50–150 hPa the increments instead add westerly momentum, +0.0031 [+0.0027, +0.0036] ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per window at 09 UTC and +0.0042 [+0.0037, +0.0048] at 21 UTC, against +0.0065 [+0.0052, +0.0077] and +0.0095 [+0.0085, +0.0105] from 400 hPa to the surface. A zonal mean cannot isolate the air near the ranges, where COORDE looked, so this tests only the zonal-mean budget.

The steady residual inside the windows. Within a window the hourly residual is +6.3 Hadley. Regressed on each range's mountain torque, friction and gravity-wave drag (2026, 4,773 hours, day-block bootstrap), it is mostly a constant, and the terms explain 17% of its variance:

A coefficient of +1% means the residual behaves as if the model's own torque were 1% larger than the one recomputed here from the 0.25° output. The "share" is the coefficient times that term's mean torque.

The significant range coefficients are a few per cent and of both signs; all the terms together account for −5.0 Hadley of the mean, against a constant of +11.3. The constant could be an offset between the model's own mountain torque on its N320 grid and the torque recomputed from 0.25° output (the mean mountain torque changes by tens of Hadley between 0.25° and 1°; section 10), or non-conservation in the semi-Lagrangian dynamics; the vertical integrals cannot tell them apart. Increments and initial tendencies have long been read as diagnostics of model error in this way (Klinker and Sardeshmukh, 1992; Rodwell and Palmer, 2007).

The forecast side

A physics model whose torques drain angular momentum should lose it in free forecasts, and IFS-ENS does: in the site's winter 2025–26 test it loses 0.31 ×10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ by day 15 while closing its own torque budget almost exactly (Do AI weather models conserve angular momentum?). That is the forecast-side counterpart of ERA5's positive increments. The data-driven AIFS-ENS, trained on analyses that already contain the correction, shows no global drain (119 to 145 forecasts per lead, inits 20 July to 12 October 2026): +0.021 [+0.007, +0.039] ×10²⁵ at day 1 and no significant change after. It shifts angular momentum between the hemispheres instead. The Northern Hemisphere gains (+0.102 [+0.023, +0.172] at day 15, significant from day 14) and the Southern loses (−0.042 [−0.068, −0.017] at day 7, significant at 8 of the 13 leads from day 3). The comparison is not like for like: the seasons and IFS cycles differ, and an increment is not a free-forecast drift.

9. How this fits with earlier work

Mountain torque as the source. This is the usual picture, drawn mostly from case studies of the largest events. The sharp rise in early 1983 came mostly from mountain torque over the American and Eurasian ranges (Ponte and Rosen, 1999). Eastern-Pacific El Niños put low pressure west of the Andes, Rockies and Himalaya and lengthen the day more than twice as much as central-Pacific ones (de Viron and Dickey, 2014). In most El Niños after 1975 angular momentum rose by roughly 10²⁵ kg m² s⁻¹ per kelvin of Niño-3.4 (Huang et al., 2003). This study differs in three ways. It uses every El Niño since 1979 through regressions, not single events. It splits each range's torque into a standing part and synoptic events, and finds that only the standing part changes. And it checks the result in 41 CMIP6 models, which have no analysis increments.

Friction as the brake. Marcus et al. (2011) found the same pairing in the 1976–77 Pacific climate shift: mountain torque over the Andes, Rockies and Himalaya was largely offset by friction over the equatorial Pacific. More generally, mountain torque creates changes in angular momentum and friction damps them (Egger et al., 2007). Not every event follows the rule. In 2015–16 the mountain torque was weaker than in 1982–83 and 1997–98 and friction added to it, yet the day lengthened by a similar amount (Lambert et al., 2017). The friction share found here, about 58% of the mountain torque in the models, is an average over events, not a rule for each one.

Where this study disagrees: the Himalaya. The case studies count the Himalaya as a source in large eastern-Pacific events. Averaged over all events its contribution here is slightly negative: −0.37 in CMIP6 (robust; not significant in ERA5), with a significant negative standing shift in spring (−1.5 Hadley, p = 0.008; section 4). The likely reasons are the samples (every event here, the extremes there) and the mix of eastern- and central-Pacific events, which this study does not separate.

Reanalysis budgets that do not close. NCEP's torques fall about 10 Hadley short of its observed change (Huang et al., 1999), and ERA-20C's total torque has a strong negative bias (Veerman and van Heerwaarden, 2019). ERA5's fall 11.3 Hadley short, the same sign and size. What is new is measuring the analysis increments directly from the jumps at ERA5's 4D-Var window starts, together the equivalent of a steady 8 Hadley torque, and mapping where they fall. Veerman and van Heerwaarden (2019) inferred the same correction indirectly in ERA-20C, whose forecasts lose about 9 Hadley that the analyses restore; a literature search found no earlier direct estimate, which does not prove none exists.

Surface winds over the oceans. Extra mixing in stable boundary layers, although known to be wrong for those layers, has long been used to tune operational models and partly stands in for orographic drag (Sandu et al., 2013). Against ASCAT scatterometer winds, ERA5's mean surface westerlies over the mid-latitude oceans are too strong (Belmonte Rivas and Stoffelen, 2019), which fits the increments weakening them (section 7). That comparison is of the analysis itself, so it shows the correction is incomplete rather than measuring the model's own error.

10. Caveats

  • The mean mountain torque is not resolution-converged (Brown, 2004; van Niekerk et al., 2016). Averaging orography and surface pressure together to 1° or 2.5° moves the global mean from about +1 to −26 and −33 Hadley, and the Andes box from −223 to −148. The anomalies still correlate at 0.96 or better with the 0.25° values, and their ENSO regressions barely change (global +1.00, +1.07 and +0.99 Hadley per kelvin). Anomalies from CMIP6's 1–2.5° grids are therefore comparable with ERA5's; absolute values are not. The Andes DJF standing shift at 2.5° is +2.1 Hadley (p = 0.009), short of the FDR.
  • What CMIP6's tauu is. It is the boundary-layer surface stress; the Met Office models state that it includes turbulent form drag from small hills. Orographic gravity-wave drag is reported separately (utendogw, in 13 models). ERA5's turbulent stress likewise includes turbulent orographic form drag. Gravity-wave drag's part in the El Niño rise is not significant in either data set, so the answer does not depend on where it is booked.
  • ERA5's torques drift. The global mountain and friction torques trend by +0.5 and +0.8 Hadley per decade over 1979–2025 with no matching trend in angular momentum, so they are detrended for the ENSO analysis. The 2026 attribution is shown both ways.
  • ENSO phase is each season's ONI against fixed thresholds, not CPC's rule of five overlapping seasons. The CMIP6 models use ±0.5 K on their own index whatever the size of their ENSO.
  • Model independence. Several CMIP6 models share code (four CESM2 variants, NorESM2 and others built on CAM, three MPI models), so "41 models" overstates the independent evidence; a one-model-per-centre check was not made.
  • Samples. Super El Niños (ONI ≥ 2.0; three DJFs) are too few to test and are not used as evidence. The zonal-mean jet comes from 1.5° daily ERA5 fields, northern hemisphere 1979–2023 and southern 1991–2020. The torque boxes are the site's and do not close the terrain at their edges, so a box's mean includes part of the large-scale pressure gradient over high ground.
