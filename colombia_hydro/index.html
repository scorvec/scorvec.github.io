Colombia · Hydropower Basin Rainfall

Where Colombia's hydro inflows are born

Colombia's grid is ~65–70 % hydroelectric, and its system operator XM tracks reservoir inflows ("aportes hídricos") across six hydrological regions. XM defines the regions as lists of monitored rivers, not as maps — so this page builds the missing polygons: each river's catchment from IDEAM's official Zonificación Hidrográfica (and, as a cross-check, upstream traces through HydroSHEDS HydroBASINS), then averages GPM IMERG satellite rainfall over each region, daily. Rain here today is turbine water in the coming weeks.

The terrain that makes the system

The same basins over elevation: all three cordilleras and both great valleys are visible, and the dams cluster exactly where rivers drop off the ranges — the eastern slope above the llanos (Oriente), the Magdalena's canyon exits (Centro), and Antioquia's dissected plateau.

The regions

Membership exactly as returned by XM's public API (ListadoRios); polygons are the union of the IDEAM subzonas holding each river's catchment. The regions are deliberately basins, not departments — XM's own dashboards shade indicative department-scale areas, but only these catchments actually convert rainfall into inflow. The emitted polygons are strictly disjoint: where a basin feeds one region's plants before flowing into another region's dam (the coffee-axis subzonas feed Caldas' run-of-river plants, then Ituango), the upstream region keeps it and the downstream polygon is carved around it — Antioquia's Ituango area is shown net of Caldas.

Validation — does basin rain predict XM's measured inflows?

3-day rainfall totals vs 3-day inflow-energy totals (AporEner, XM API), cross-correlated at 0–15-day lags, in three flavours per region: raw, anomaly (both series minus their seasonal normals), and burst (rain above a fitted baseline threshold — testing whether steady light rain just holds soil/evap steady state while only downpours move inflow). Now computed on a continuous two-year record (IMERG Final, gauge-corrected, backfilled via OPeNDAP; Early for recent months). Verdict: raw 3-day totals win almost everywhere at short lags (0–2 days); anomalies help only Valle (0.27→0.34), the burst threshold only Centro (marginally), and the exotic variants that looked good on the thin 8-month record largely deflated — the region-scale relationship is close to linear and fast. Best per region: Antioquia r=0.44 (raw, 1d) · Caldas r=0.52 (raw, 1d) · Caribe r=0.42 (raw, 2d) · Centro r=0.40 (burst>1mm, 2d) · Oriente r=0.48 (raw, 0d) · Valle r=0.34 (anom, 1d). Oriente 0.34→0.48 and Valle 0.19→0.34 improved the most with record length; Centro slipped (its big rivers are the regulated ones — see below).

The same relationship as scatter — every dot is one 3-day block (rain shifted by the region's best lag). The slope is the region's effective rainfall-to-energy conversion; the widest clouds belong to the regions whose big rivers are regulated (Centro's Bogotá/Quimbo, Oriente's Batá).

The linear-axis view above undersells the coupling: rain and inflow are both strongly right-skewed, so a few extreme days dominate a straight Pearson r and the bulk of the cloud collapses near the origin. In log-log space, on weekly blocks, the relationship shows itself as the power law it is — regional rlog ≈ 0.54–0.67 (Valle 0.35), i.e. rain explains roughly 30–45 % of weekly inflow variance at region scale.

Per river the split is even cleaner: free-flowing rivers plot as tight power-law clouds (Guavio and Sogamoso rlog=0.68, Sinú 0.64, San Carlos 0.61, Grande 0.58 — and at monthly blocks Guavio reaches rank-ρ≈0.83), while Bogotá N.R. is flat at rlog=0.17 — a third of Centro's inflow with essentially zero rainfall signal, pure operator decisions. Where physics sets the flow, IMERG rainfall is a genuinely good predictor; the residual sits with the handful of regulated series.

A better kernel than any fixed window: catchments behave like leaky reservoirs, so rain should be accumulated with an exponential die-off — St = Rt + e−1/τ·St−1, the classic antecedent-precipitation / linear-reservoir model — and the decay timescale τ scanned per basin. It beats the 3-day boxcar in every region on the honest (deseasonalized) basis: Antioquia 0.44→0.48 (τ≈4d) · Caldas 0.52 (τ≈2d) · Caribe 0.42→0.46 (τ≈4d) · Centro 0.40→0.42 (τ≈5d) · Oriente 0.48 (τ≈1d) · Valle 0.34→0.40 (τ≈3d). The solid raw curves keep rising toward τ=30 d in Caldas and Oriente — that's not skill, it's the filter reconstructing the shared wet-season cycle, which is why the dashed anomaly curves (seasonal cycle removed from both series) are the ones to trust at long τ. The fitted τ is a real physical fingerprint: ~1 d for Oriente's steep eastern-slope catchments, 3–7 d for the big Andean basins.

Per river (anomaly basis), the fitted memory sorts the system into fast and slow water: steep tunnel-and-intake catchments at τ≈1 d (Guavio, San Carlos, Porce II, Alto Anchicayá), the big regulated Cauca systems at τ≈7–14 d (Ituango, Salvajina, Grande), and Amoyá the standout at r=0.60 with τ=3 d. The regulated laggards (EPM diversions, Porce III, El Quimbo, Bogotá) stay weak under every kernel — no rainfall model fixes operations.

Which rivers carry the water?

XM meters inflow river-by-river — this ranks all 43 active rivers by their share of their region's inflow energy over the trailing year, hatched by how precisely our polygons capture each catchment. The audit confirms every metered river's catchment is inside the polygons (none unmapped), and the big carriers — Ituango (29% of Antioquia), Sogamoso + Bogotá + El Quimbo (83% of Centro), Batá + Guavio (80% of Oriente) — are exactly the catchments given dedicated treatment. Note: cascade chains meter the same water at each plant, so shares are of energy, not volume.

And the river-level cut of the validation: rainfall averaged over each river's own subzonas vs its own inflow series, same 3-day-block method, on the full two-year record. Three findings. (1) Response is fast everywhere — every river peaks at 0–3 days (the 11–12-day lags that appeared for the Cauca/Porce systems on the shorter record were a small-sample artifact; with two full years, Grande, Guadalupe, Porce and San Carlos all sit at 0–1 d with r≈0.5). (2) The laggards are the regulated and diverted series, not the rainy ones — Bogotá N.R. (r=0.28; heavily managed system), El Quimbo (0.30; large reservoir), Batá (0.31; Chivor diversions), the EPM tunnel diversions (0.18) and Calima (0.18) — while free-flowing neighbours in the same regions reach 0.5+. Centro's regional slip is exactly this: two of its three big rivers are the regulated pair. (3) Sogamoso remains the most rain-forecastable big river (r=0.55 at 2 d on a third of Centro), with the cleanest small catchments — Prado 0.57, San Carlos 0.56, Amoyá 0.54, San Miguel 0.53, Digua 0.52 — close behind.
