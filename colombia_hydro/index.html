Colombia · Hydropower Basin Rainfall

Where Colombia's hydro inflows are born

Colombia's grid is ~65–70 % hydroelectric, and its system operator XM tracks reservoir inflows ("aportes hídricos") across six hydrological regions. XM defines the regions as lists of monitored rivers, not as maps — so this page builds the missing polygons: each river's catchment from IDEAM's official Zonificación Hidrográfica (and, as a cross-check, upstream traces through HydroSHEDS HydroBASINS), then averages GPM IMERG satellite rainfall over each region, daily. Rain here today is turbine water in the coming weeks.

Two views of the same regions: the contributing basins (left — used for all rainfall averaging on this page) and the official department groups from XM's Mapa Hidrología SIN (right), with the true basins outlined in black. Rain outside the black outlines never reaches a turbine.

Basin rainfall, daily

Ground truth — IMERG vs the IDEAM gauge network

Satellite rainfall with ~400 IDEAM automatic rain gauges overlaid on the same color scale — where dots vanish into the background the satellite is right; where they pop, it isn't. Left: raw IMERG. Right: gauge-corrected IMERG — a cell-wise multiplicative correction field built from 518 stations' two-year satellite-vs-gauge ratios (median 553 paired days each), interpolated across the grid and relaxing to ×1 away from stations. The geography it fixes is systematic: IMERG runs low over the wet Antioquia slopes (the classic orographic underestimate) and high in the dry upper-Magdalena valley where 0.1° cells straddle wet cordillera walls. Accumulations use paired feed days only, so the gauge feed's multi-week ingest holes can't skew either side.

Backtested, held out: rebuilding the field with each station excluded and predicting it from its neighbors, raw IMERG misses a station's two-year total by a median factor of ×2.04; the corrected field cuts that to ×1.50 — a 43% error reduction, 70% of 518 stations improved — and station factors correlate 0.61 across the two years, so the field is persistent geography, not noise. In inflow-prediction head-to-heads the corrected satellite beats in-basin gauge averages everywhere the network is thin (everywhere but the dense Bogotá corridor), which is why it is the model's rainfall driver.

The bias map — where the satellite is wrong, and by how much

The correction field itself, with all 549 stations' individual gauge/satellite ratios on the same scale. Colombia is dominantly satellite-wet (brown): the darkest core (×0.33–0.5) sits on the Bogotá/upper-Magdalena corridor, nailed down by the dense station cluster. Greens — satellite too dry — are confined to the llanos edge and to scattered wet-slope stations embedded in brown terrain: the 0.1° cells straddling cordillera walls are too wet for their valleys and too dry for their peaks at the same time. Compare the Brazil bias map: same satellite, same methodology, a completely different bias regime.

Raw vs corrected — the comparisons

The rain → inflow model — memory is the skill

Each basin's IMERG rainfall anomaly is passed through a causal exponential memory kernel (a running exponentially-weighted mean with time constant τ) and regressed against the fleet-corrected inflow % of norm. The kernel is the physics: rain doesn't become inflow on the day it falls — it drains out of soils and groundwater over weeks. Fitting τ per basin lifts correlations from r ≈ 0.13–0.23 (same-day rain) to r ≈ 0.51–0.67 — the fitted memories (τ = 20–45 days for the slow-draining Andean basins, 7 days for the flashier Centro/Magdalena) are themselves a hydrological fingerprint of each basin. The red curve is rainfall alone predicting inflows: it captures the wet/dry regimes and their transitions, while the sharpest daily spikes (single storms, operational effects) live above the kernel. These fitted kernels are what the AIFS-ENS rainfall forecasts will drive to project inflows ahead — and because τ is long, a 15-day rain forecast moves the kernel slowly and predictably.

What about evaporation? Tested three ways — a daily RONI regressor on the 2-year record, ENSO composites of 64 years of ERA5 latent heat flux, and a 23-year leave-one-year-out water balance (rain vs P−E vs rain + ET term) — the direct evapotranspiration channel adds at most +0.04 correlation and usually nothing: over these basins ENSO's influence on inflows is almost entirely mediated by rainfall itself. The model stays rainfall-driven, with the RONI term as its ET channel and reservoir-surface evaporation carried inside the storage balance's ENSO-adjusted outflow.

Model v2 (green dotted): the rain driver is now the gauge-corrected IMERG (the 549-station field from the ground-truth section, applied to rain and climatology alike), and a slow soil-moisture kernel — a 90-day exponential memory of the same rain anomaly — joins the fast kernel and the RONI term as a third regressor. Antecedent wetness the fast kernel forgets is exactly what it adds: Oriente and Centro, the big slow basins, gain the most (r +0.02); elsewhere it is correctly near-zero rather than harmful. The RONI term remains the model's evapotranspiration channel. Model v3 (purple dash-dot) adds yesterday's reservoir storage anomaly (% full vs its seasonal norm) as a measured basin-state regressor — part groundwater proxy, part cascade plumbing (upstream releases are downstream inflows at Betania and on the Cauca). It is the largest single upgrade where regulation is strongest: Valle r 0.53→0.74, Centro 0.53→0.65. In the forecast the anomaly is held at its latest observed value (it decorrelates over months), and the fan is anchored to the current observed inflow with the residual decaying over ~10 days — the fan starts at reality and relaxes to the model.

Per-dam models — only the rain that matters to each dam

The regional models average over whole hydro regions; these use each river's own upstream catchment (IDEAM subzona unions traced upstream from every dam — nested drainage preserved, so Ituango's polygon correctly contains the Caldas catchments whose water passes through it). Gauge-corrected IMERG is masked to each polygon: rain outside the divide or downstream of the dam wall never enters that dam's model. Twelve majors, same machinery as the regional v3 (fast + slow memory kernels, ENSO, regional storage state), each with its own fitted τ and its own ensemble fan from catchment-masked AIFS+IFS members. The free-flowing rivers fit better than their parent regions — Cauca Salvajina r = 0.78, Betania 0.74, Grande 0.71, Sogamoso 0.68 — because the mask removes rain that was never going to reach the turbines. Regulated systems get an automatic REGULATED flag when their rain skill falls below r = 0.30 — operator decisions, not rainfall, set those flows. Notably, Bogotá N.R. climbed off the flag (kernel r 0.25 → 0.40) once the verification truth began density-weighting the dense Bogotá gauge network: real rain signal the satellite alone couldn't see. Batá (Chivor diversions) remains the weakest rain responder.

What is an "inflow"? From raindrop to gigawatt

The quantity on this page is energy, not water. XM's raw measurement is the aporte hídrico — streamflow in m³/s where each monitored river enters its reservoir — but what XM publishes per river (and what we plot) is AporEner: that water converted to its electric-energy equivalent via each river's conversion factor (kWh per m³), which encodes the head — the vertical drop, in metres, from reservoir surface to turbine; the energy in a cubic metre of water is its weight times how far it falls (about 0.23 kWh per m³ per 100 m at typical efficiency) — and efficiency of the plant chain the water can fall through, with cascades summing the head of every plant in sequence. So AporEner = Q × 86,400 s × factor, and a river feeding a deep cascade is "worth" several times the GWh per cubic metre of a low-head run-of-river plant. Divide by 86,400 × factor to recover m³/s.

The pathway from rainfall to inflow: rain falls on the catchment (only rain inside the black basin outlines ever reaches a turbine) → a large share — roughly 40–60 % in these tropical basins — returns to the sky as evaporation and transpiration → the remainder becomes runoff on two timescales: a fast storm response (hours to a couple of days) and a slow soil-and-groundwater release that drains over weeks — this memory is why inflows stay elevated long after the rain stops, and why our rain→inflow model uses an exponential-decay kernel rather than same-day rain → channels route the water to the reservoir gauge (bigger basins, longer lags: 1–4 days is typical here) → the metered m³/s is scaled by the plant head into energy.

Why the total grows as dams are added, with zero change in the weather: (1) a new dam on a previously unmetered river adds its whole catchment's water to the ledger; (2) a new plant downstream on an already-metered river raises that river's conversion factor — the same water now falls further and earns more kWh; and (3) the regional totals are energy-weighted, so high-head basins count extra. Metered rivers grew from 18 in 2000 to 41 today (Porce III 2011, Sogamoso 2014, El Quimbo 2015, Ituango — now roughly a third of Antioquia — 2018+), which is why the raw record doubles while a fixed 17-river panel rises only ~10–15 %. Every "% of normal" on this page is therefore computed against per-river climatologies aggregated to today's fleet, so fleet growth cancels and what remains is hydrology.

Inflows vs seasonal norms — the 26-year context

XM's measured inflow energy (AporEner) for every region, every day since 2000 — including the historical river names that vanished from today's listings (NARE, MAGDALENA BETANIA, PORCE II, FLORIDA II; their succession seams verified exact, so the record is continuous and complete). Each panel: the day-of-year climatology (median, p25–p75 and p10–p90 envelopes, ±10-day windows) with the last 200 days overlaid — above or below the band is immediately legible as wet or dry for the date. Bottom: 90-day and 365-day running means of the national total against the identically-windowed norm; the annual mean is in record-high territory in 2025–26, with recent months falling back toward normal as El Niño develops.

The orange dashed fan extending each red line is the 15-day inflow forecast: every member of the AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS (~101 members, 00/12Z cycles) has its basin rainfall bias-mapped into IMERG-equivalent units, converted to an anomaly against the satellite climatology, spliced onto the observed rain history and propagated through that basin's fitted memory kernel (τ, lag, gain and ENSO term from the rain→inflow model above). Bands are the weighted 10–90 and 25–75 member percentiles. The two models are blended by out-of-sample verification: every archived forecast day is scored against IMERG once it matures, and the per-basin, per-lead bias factors and inverse-error weights update themselves twice a day — the blend starts 50/50 and earns its tilt as the forecast archive grows.

How good are the rain forecasts? — the live scorecard

Every 00/12Z cycle's day-1 basin rainfall (ensemble mean of the first full 24 h) is scored against gauge-corrected IMERG as soon as the satellite day matures, and accumulates here permanently — black is what fell, blue diamonds AIFS-ENS, red diamonds IFS-ENS, with running MAE and bias ratio per basin in each legend. This is the record that drives the blend: the per-basin, per-lead bias factors and model weights update from it twice a day. The IFS starts from an informed prior of ×0.75 (its tropical wet bias is well documented); the prior is deliberately light, so a week of verified days overrides it with the measured number.

Reservoir storage — the number the market actually watches

Inflows are the flow; storage is the state. XM publishes every reservoir's useful stored volume and useful capacity daily in energy-equivalent units (kWh at each plant's conversion factor), aggregated here to the same hydro regions and shown as % of useful capacity against day-of-year norms — the ratio is fleet-seam-free because both sides are summed over the same-day reporting fleet. The orange fan is the storage forecast: each of the ~101 ensemble members' inflow trajectories is integrated forward through the water balance S′ = S + inflow − outflow, where outflow (generation + spill + evaporation, implied from the 26-year record as inflow − ΔS) is forecast as half the recent 14-day reality, half the ENSO-adjusted seasonal norm. The ENSO adjustment is fitted from the full record and carries both reservoir evaporation and El Niño-conditioned dispatch — a small, deliberately second-order term.

Actual hydro generation — the end of the chain

Rain → inflows → storage → generation: XM's hourly per-plant output (161 hydro plants among 2,276 resources), aggregated to daily national average hydro power from 2000. Top: the annual mean has grown from ~3.1 to ~7.7 GW (demand + fleet); the collapses are El Niño 2009–10 and 2015–16, when hydro's share of the mix fell from its usual 75–85% to 60% annual (30% on the worst days) — the gap is thermal dispatch, which is what moves prices. Bottom: generation against its day-of-year envelope with the forecast fan. Generation is more forecastable than inflows at short leads — reservoirs low-pass-filter the weather and dispatch follows storage and demand. The model: a trailing 3-year norm + weekday offsets (the weekly dispatch cycle is r = 0.82 at lag 7) + a blend of persistence (fitted decay, dominant early) and the state model — inflow anomaly, storage anomaly, Niño-3.4 — which alone scores r = 0.59 out-of-sample across 23 leave-one-year-out years. Bands widen with lead by the blend's residual uncertainty, and the member spread rides the same AIFS+IFS rain ensemble as everything above.

The terrain that makes the system

The same basins over elevation: all three cordilleras and both great valleys are visible, and the dams cluster exactly where rivers drop off the ranges — the eastern slope above the llanos (Oriente), the Magdalena's canyon exits (Centro), and Antioquia's dissected plateau.

The regions

Membership exactly as returned by XM's public API (ListadoRios); polygons are the union of the IDEAM subzonas holding each river's catchment. The regions are deliberately basins, not departments — XM's own dashboards shade indicative department-scale areas, but only these catchments actually convert rainfall into inflow. The emitted polygons are strictly disjoint: where a basin feeds one region's plants before flowing into another region's dam (the coffee-axis subzonas feed Caldas' run-of-river plants, then Ituango), the upstream region keeps it and the downstream polygon is carved around it — Antioquia's Ituango area is shown net of Caldas.

Which rivers carry the water?

XM meters inflow river-by-river — this ranks all 43 active rivers by their share of their region's inflow energy over the trailing year, hatched by how precisely our polygons capture each catchment. The audit confirms every metered river's catchment is inside the polygons (none unmapped), and the big carriers — Ituango (29% of Antioquia), Sogamoso + Bogotá + El Quimbo (83% of Centro), Batá + Guavio (80% of Oriente) — are exactly the catchments given dedicated treatment. Note: cascade chains meter the same water at each plant, so shares are of energy, not volume.
