Stratosphere & Polar Vortex Monitor

The winter polar vortex is the flywheel of subseasonal prediction: when planetary waves disrupt it, the surface circulation (AO/NAO) feels the result for a month or more. This page tracks the vortex the way the momentum budget actually works — wave driving in, residual-circulation descent over the pole, adiabatic warming out — using ERA5 analyses and the ECMWF AIFS-ENS ensemble, which now resolves the 10 hPa level in open data.

Polar-Cap Descent Index (TEM Residual Circulation)

The transformed-Eulerian-mean residual circulation is what actually delivers a stratospheric warming: wave driving forces a poleward surge, continuity turns it into descent over the polar cap, and compression does the warming. This index measures that descent directly — the residual meridional wind $\bar v^*$ crossing 60° converted to cap-mean sinking (hPa/day) by mass continuity — with the polar-cap temperature it drives (red). In the 28-event NH record, descent pulses of +0.5 hPa/day lead reversals by 5–10 days; the implied adiabatic heating closes the observed warming budget to within radiative damping.

The SSW Risk Index: what it is and what moves it

The SRI is a probability — P(major sudden stratospheric warming within the next 15 or 30 days) — from a ridge-regularized logistic model fit on every NDJFM day from 1974 to 2026 (28 major warmings) and validated leave-one-winter-out: every skill number on this page comes from forecasts made by a model that never saw the winter being scored. Twenty-three inputs, four families: the vortex’s own geometry (a displaced, wave-1-distorted vortex is the single best predictor there is), the wave forcing arriving from below (integrated 100 hPa eddy heat flux, split by zonal wavenumber), the tropospheric doors and shields (where blocking sits decides whether planetary waves are funneled up or deflected), and slow modulators (ENSO — non-monotonically, QBO, and the calendar). Two counter-intuitive entries are real, validated results: currently-weak vortex winds argue against an imminent event once the other inputs are known, and central-Pacific blocking is protective.

Backtest: every major warming since 1974, out of sample

The median event sees its probability climb from the ~4% base rate a month out to 17–18% at the event, with the upper quartile above 30%. The case studies show the range: 2013 and 2021 were textbook ramps; 2018 fired twice (arguably one extended vortex breakdown); 2009 is the honest scar — the great wave-2 split gave almost no classical warning, and the index only reached ~45% five days out, via the wave-2 burst channel described below. Splits are short-fuse events; no observational index escapes that.

Reliability: the probabilities are calibrated

A 15% forecast verifies about 13% of the time; a 26% forecast about 28% — out of sample, across five decades. Days above 20% carry seven times the climatological risk. The one caveat is the rare >35% bin, which runs overconfident (51% forecast, 27% observed): treat anything above ~35% as “high alert” rather than a precise number.

Who can see an SSW coming — and how far ahead

The predictor ladder, by lead window. At 1–15 days the 10 hPa wave-1 amplitude is nearly a sufficient statistic — the displaced vortex has already integrated everything else — and the full model’s value is calibration and robustness rather than raw discrimination. At 16–30 days the ensemble of preconditioners genuinely beats any single input. Note the fate of the folklore “weak vortex” rule: by weeks 3–4 it is inverted — an already-disturbed vortex is transiently protected, not primed.

Two doors and a shield

Sweeping 60°-wide blocking sectors around the hemisphere against “SSW within 16–45 days” finds two doors: the canonical Ural sector (Cohen & Jones 2011; Kretschmer et al. 2017) and a slightly stronger, rarely-named Labrador/northeast-Canada sector — kin to the Atlantic-blocking precursors of Martius et al. (2009) and the northeastern-America lobe of Garfinkel et al.’s (2010) optimal precursor pattern. Both doors are the same physics: ridging placed to constructively reinforce the climatological wave-1. Blocking in the central Pacific does the opposite — destructive interference that shields the vortex — which is why “more blocking = more SSW risk” is wrong without an address. Honesty note: the Labrador box was identified in this 1974+ record; its out-of-sample test against pre-1974 winters is in progress and this section will be updated with the verdict.

Two kinds of warning

Displacement events announce themselves for weeks: wave-1 amplitude and integrated heat flux ramp for 20–30 days before the reversal. Split events strike in days: the wave-2 heat-flux channel is silent until roughly a week out, then erupts — the two largest wave-2 bursts in the 52-year record are February 2018 and January 2009, both majors. The index carries a dedicated thresholded “burst” term for exactly this channel (it is what rescued 2009 in the backtest above), and its 16–30-day coefficient is negative: a wave-2 burst means imminent-or-never, never “a month away.”
