The vortex, days 1–15

Vortex strength and sudden-warming odds · every model's u(60°N) at 10 hPa, polar-cap height and 10 hPa temperature · explainer
The stratospheric vortex is the slow variable that sets the odds for the surface weeks later, so it is worth watching before it moves. Top: the zonal-mean zonal wind at 10 hPa, 60°N — the standard sudden-stratospheric-warming diagnostic. A reversal to easterly here in winter is a major SSW by the wind criterion (Charlton and Polvani 2007; the original WMO definition also asks for the temperature to rise from 60°N to the pole, which the Temperature view follows), which is why the zero line is drawn heavy and the member fraction crossing it is called out. Middle: the same wind at 100 hPa. Anomalies that reach this far down are the ones with a path to the troposphere — a 10 hPa event that never appears at 100 hPa usually never reaches the surface. Bottom: the 100 hPa polar-cap (65–90°N) height anomaly, positive for a weak or displaced vortex; this is the field that actually leads the AO/NAO response. Each model’s band is its members’ 10th–90th percentile and the solid line their mean; dashed lines are deterministic runs (GDPS, and NASA’s GEOS FP, whose 00Z run reaches day 10; GEOS FP is the operational sibling of MERRA-2, the reanalysis the climatology bands come from). The black line up to the start is the GEOS FP analysis (daily mean of its 00/06/12/18Z analyses), its polar-cap height moved onto the MERRA-2 scale by their measured offset (−14 m at 100 hPa). The AIFS-ENS polar-cap height uses the same 50 perturbed members plus the control as the winds. GEFS (30 members + control) and GEPS (20 + control) run to day 16. IFS-ENS is drawn with all 50 of its perturbed members (its ensemble files carry no control at these levels), and the IFS control (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), from its own open-data files, dashed to day 15, as is AIFS single (ECMWF’s deterministic AIFS run, its own model, not a member of AIFS-ENS). The chart is drawn about 90 minutes after the AIFS products (around 09:30 and 21:30 UTC), once IFS-ENS day 15 is on the mirror.

Polar temperature and the vortex, 10 hPa · every ensemble's mean and members, the controls and AIFS single
Polar temperature at 10 hPa. A sudden warming is, first of all, a warming: air descending over the pole as the vortex is displaced or split warms by tens of degrees in a few days, and the westerlies at 60°N then weaken or reverse. These maps show the 10 hPa temperature as its departure from the ERA5 1991–2020 normal for that day of the year, and the 10 hPa geopotential height, whose contours outline the vortex (the flow runs along them): a warm patch moving over the pole and the height lines crowding off to one side or pinching in two is the warming in progress.

Vortex winds · 10 and 100 hPa: every ensemble, both controls and AIFS single
The vortex as it actually looks, rather than as an index. Wind speed is shaded and contoured with wind barbs over it (in m s−1: half barb 5, full barb 10, pennant 50) and the geopotential height in black (dam, every 24 dam at 10 hPa and 16 at 100): the wind runs along the height lines, so the vortex’s centre and shape read from them and the shading says how fast. The pole itself is left blank poleward of 86°, where a latitude–longitude grid has a seam and nothing about the vortex is read. One hemisphere at a time, at 10 hPa. This is where the vortex lives and where an SSW is declared — the level the WMO diagnostic is evaluated on. The red contour is u = 0: where the westerlies end in the zonal sense (not the PV-gradient vortex edge), and the outer bound of the corridor $0<\bar{u}<U_c$ in which planetary waves can propagate vertically; outside it they cannot. The dashed ring is 60°, the latitude the SSW diagnostic is evaluated at. Speeds below the first contour are left white, which is why the summer hemisphere is largely blank — there is no vortex there to draw. Both hemispheres share the colour scale, so they are directly comparable.

Upper stratosphere · to 1 hPa: GEFS, GFS, GEOS FP, GDPS
The stratosphere does not stop at 10 hPa. Its top, the stratopause near 1 hPa (about 48 km), is where the polar vortex usually changes first: a sudden warming generally shows up there as a warming and a slackening of the westerlies days before it reaches 10 hPa, and it then works its way down, the way the dripping-paint charts show lower down. The spring final warming that ends the southern vortex (September–November) also comes down from the top. Most forecast products on this site stop at 10 hPa; four models publish higher, and this item puts them side by side. ECMWF’s AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS cannot join them: their open data stops at 10 hPa. Nor can GEPS: ECCC’s Datamart publishes its pressure levels only up to 10 hPa.

The waves driving it, days 1–15

Eddy heat flux, 100 hPa · [v′T′] and the SSW warning meter, every ensemble · explainer
The wave maps show how large the planetary waves are and where their ridges sit; they do not show whether they propagate upward. That is the vertical component of the Eliassen–Palm flux, which at 100 hPa is proportional to the zonal-mean poleward eddy heat flux [v′T′]. A large wave that is vertically stacked carries almost none; one that tilts westward with height carries a lot. Positive is poleward in both hemispheres, which is wave activity entering the stratosphere.

How unusual is the wave driving · every ensemble's heat-flux peak against the ERA5 record
What it shows. How unusual the forecast wave driving would be, against the ERA5 record. For each ensemble and its control run, over days 1–15: the peak of the poleward eddy heat flux at 100 hPa, 45–75°, the measure of the planetary waves entering the stratosphere, as all waves, wave 1 or wave 2, or as the trailing 10-day mean of all waves that the SSW warning meter uses. Each peak is ranked among every ERA5 value within 15 days of the same calendar date in 1990–2026 (the percentile: 99 means higher than 99% of them) and set against the highest of them, the record for that time of year, with its date.

E–P flux · GEFS wave driving to 1 hPa, k = 1–3 · explainer
Where the torque budget shows how the Earth exchanges angular momentum with the atmosphere at the surface, the Eliassen–Palm flux shows how the waves redistribute it internally — the same quasi-geostrophic diagnostic used to diagnose sudden stratospheric warmings, computed live from the NOAA GEFS ensemble (the control and 30 perturbed members) and stepped through the 15-day forecast. Each member’s flux is computed and then averaged: the flux is quadratic in the waves, and the flux of the ensemble-mean fields would fade with lead time as the members’ wave phases drift apart.

Refractive index · GEFS waveguide for waves 1 and 2, 300–1 hPa
Reading it. The refractive index a²n² describes the planetary waves’ waveguide, the way a lens guides light. Stationary waves travel where it is positive (blue) and bend toward higher values. Where it is negative (grey, inside the red zero line) they cannot travel, and they fade over a short distance. Where the wind is easterly or zero (blank) they meet a critical line and are absorbed. The top row is wave-1 and the bottom row wave-2. Wave-2’s guide is narrower (the k²/cos²φ term), so the polar cap closes to it first. Three patterns matter: a corridor of positive values from the tropopause up into the vortex lets waves reach and weaken it; positive values only on the subtropical side turn them away from the pole; and a negative layer over the polar cap, above a positive one, can turn them back down.

Regional reflection index · Siberia vs Canada wave flux at 100 hPa (Messori et al. 2022), every ensemble
Planetary waves leave the troposphere mostly over Siberia and the North Pacific. When the stratosphere above turns them back, they come down over Canada, and the troposphere below answers with a ridge over Alaska and cold air over North America (Messori et al. 2022). The index is the contrast between the two: v*T* (the eddy heat flux, departures from the zonal mean) at 100 hPa over Siberia (45–75°N, 140–200°E) and over Canada (45–75°N, 230–280°E), each standardised by its ERA5 1991–2020 day-of-year mean and standard deviation: RI = z(Siberia) − z(Canada). An event is RI above 1 for ten days or more with the Siberian flux upward and the Canadian downward on average over it.

The season ahead

December vortex: C3S multi-model · calibrated; hindcast r 0.67, significant
Monthly seasonal forecasts of the stratospheric polar vortex were tested before this card was built: every C3S centre’s 1993–2016 hindcast of the monthly-mean wind at 60°N, 10 hPa (and 100 hPa) against MERRA-2, with the tests and the multiple-comparison correction fixed in advance. From the October start, only December passed: the equal-weight mean of the seven centres’ ensemble-mean anomalies correlated with the observed December at r 0.67 (95 % interval 0.36–0.86, Holm-corrected p 0.003), the Met Office (r 0.73) and DWD (0.61) individually; January, February, the DJF mean and every 100 hPa target did not. (BoM was left out on 16 October 2026, as on the ENSO forecasts page, and the test re-run over the seven remaining centres, 32 tests instead of 36; with BoM the December result was r 0.66, Holm p 0.005.) The skill is robust to dropping any single winter and is not October persistence. This card is that one significant result, made into a forecast.

December vortex, day by day · C3S members, spells and sudden warmings
The daily follow-up to the December card: the same seven C3S centres from their October starts, but day by day, so the charts can show what a monthly mean hides — spells of weak vortex, sudden warmings and when they fall. Each centre’s members are measured against that model’s own 1993–2016 daily hindcast normal (models disagree by up to 12 m/s on the strength of the vortex), and the multi-model numbers weight every centre equally.

SEAS5: vortex, QBO, annular mode · 51 members, seven months; hindcast skill for the annular mode
The zonal-mean 10 hPa wind at 60°N is the standard vortex-strength measure (its 60°S twin covers the southern spring breakdown); the probability of an easterly reading is the sudden-warming side of the distribution. The equatorial 10, 30 and 50 hPa winds are SEAS5’s own QBO, which sets the Holton–Tan tilt on the vortex. The NAM-by-level indices are polar-cap height anomalies standardised by the hindcast and signed so that positive is a strong vortex, from 10 hPa down to the surface — read them top to bottom for downward coupling. Wave-1 and wave-2 amplitudes at 100 hPa are the planetary-wave forcing that precedes a warming.

SEAS5: sudden-warming risk · no demonstrated skill: the model's view
The vortex day by day: the zonal-mean wind at 60°N and 10 hPa for each of the 51 members, against SEAS5’s own normal for the same start date and lead — its hindcast, 25 members × 36 winters (1981–2016) — so the model’s drift and bias are in the reference, not in the anomaly. A major sudden stratospheric warming is counted the standard way (Charlton & Polvani 2007): the daily wind turns easterly between 1 November and 31 March, 20 westerly days separate two events, and a reversal that never recovers for 10 days before 30 April is the final warming, not an SSW. The risk index is the share of members with at least one, set against the model’s base rate (same start, same rule) and the observed rate (MERRA-2). Its skill is tested on the hindcast: each winter’s member fraction against what MERRA-2 observed. 100 hPa is monthly only: the daily hindcast at that level is not in the AWS copy and would take hundreds of thousands of CDS fields per start month.

SEAS5: polar-cap heights · 100, 50 and 10 hPa, both hemispheres
The 60–90° polar-cap mean geopotential height at 100, 50 and 10 hPa, as an anomaly against the hindcast’s linear trend at each lead, extrapolated to the forecast year — heights carry the warming trend, so an anomaly against the 1993–2016 mean would read high before any circulation signal. A high cap at 100 hPa is a weak, warm vortex (the SSW-side reading); a low cap is a strong, cold one. The hindcast ±1σ band shows how far a seasonal model normally strays, which at these levels is not far — read the sign of the tilt and whether the members agree, not the amplitude. Both hemispheres; the 60°S twin shows the southern vortex’s own cycle at the same leads.

The QBO and today's analyses

QBO from radiosondes · 13 equatorial stations, the whole record
The Quasi-Biennial Oscillation — the ~28-month heartbeat of the equatorial stratosphere — read directly from balloons. Within ~10° of the equator the annual cycle in stratospheric zonal wind nearly vanishes, so a monthly multi-station mean of radiosonde winds at 10–100 hPa is the QBO: westerly and easterly regimes descending about 1 km/month. Built from 13 stations (Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, Bintulu, the Micronesia chain, six Amazon stations, Nairobi) — the same raw archive behind the Sounding Explorer.

QBO zero-wind line · analyses and the AIFS-ENS / IFS-ENS forecast, 70–10 hPa
Holton and Tan (1980) proposed that the quasi-biennial oscillation reaches the polar vortex through a critical line. Planetary waves coming up and equatorward from the winter midlatitudes cannot cross the latitude where the zonal-mean wind [u] turns easterly. With easterly QBO winds in the lower stratosphere that line sits in the winter subtropics, the waveguide is narrower, and more wave activity is steered toward the vortex. This item tracks where that line is, day by day, at 70, 50, 30 and 10 hPa. The QBO and the vortex item in the history group shows the equatorial wind itself.

Momentum budget · what drives the vortex and the upwelling
The zonal momentum budget says, term by term, what is speeding up or slowing down the stratospheric westerlies. It is written in the transformed Eulerian mean (TEM) form of Andrews, Holton and Leovy (1987), in which the waves' effect on the mean flow is a single term, the divergence of their Eliassen–Palm flux: $$\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial t} \;=\; \hat f\,\bar v^{*} \;-\; \bar w^{*}\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial z} \;+\; \frac{\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf F}{\rho_0\,a\cos\phi} \;+\; X_{\rm GWD} \;+\; X_{\rm ANA} \;+\; \text{residual},\qquad \hat f = f-\frac{1}{a\cos\phi}\frac{\partial(\bar u\cos\phi)}{\partial\phi}$$ The left side is the observed change of the zonal-mean wind over each day. On the right: the Coriolis force on the residual (Brewer–Dobson) meridional flow $\bar v^{*}$; vertical advection by the residual vertical flow $\bar w^{*}$; the drag of the resolved waves (planetary and synoptic, also split into zonal wavenumbers 1, 2 and 3+); the model's parameterized gravity-wave drag, orographic and non-orographic together (GEOS FP publishes only the total); and the analysis increment, the push the data assimilation gives the model to keep it on the observations. The residual is what is left over: numerical damping and the sampling error of the eddy fluxes. A negative term pushes westward and slows a westerly vortex.

Brewer–Dobson circulation · tropical upwelling, downward control
The Brewer–Dobson circulation is the slow overturning of the stratosphere: air rises through the tropical tropopause, drifts poleward and sinks over the winter pole. It sets how fast air, water vapour and ozone move through the stratosphere, and it is driven from above — waves breaking in the extratropical stratosphere pull the circulation along. This product estimates it by downward control (Haynes et al. 1991): the zonal momentum forcing — the divergence of the Eliassen–Palm flux of the resolved waves plus the parameterized gravity-wave drag — divided by the Coriolis term gives the residual meridional wind, integrated from the top down into the residual streamfunction Ψ*. The tropical upward mass flux is Ψ* at the two subtropical turnaround latitudes, fixed for each level from MERRA-2’s annual-mean circulation (41°S and 36°N at 70 hPa, 23°S and 33°N at 100 hPa): sought day by day, the turnaround sat on the edge of its search range on 39% of days and jumped by up to 18° as the flat summer-hemisphere profile changed, which moved the index with every change of the climatology. Estimating it from the analysed mean meridional wind or vertical velocity directly does not work: in both GEOS FP and MERRA-2 that version shows no northern-winter maximum at all, because the tropical upwelling (~0.3 mm/s) is buried in the noise of a daily zonal mean.

Water-vapour tape recorder · Aura MLS + GEOS FP, how fast air rises
Air enters the stratosphere through the cold tropical tropopause, which freeze-dries it to the water-vapour saturation of its coldest point. That cold point is coldest in northern winter and warmest in northern summer, so the air entering carries an annual cycle of water vapour — about 3 ppmv in February, 5 in September at 100 hPa — and the slow tropical ascent of the Brewer–Dobson circulation lifts it like a signal written on a rising tape: the tape recorder (Mote et al. 1996). The slope of its dry and moist bands is the ascent speed; the value at 100–70 hPa is the entry value the cold point has just set.

Sudden warmings since 1980 · timeline, catalogue, vortex shapes · explainer
A major sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) follows Charlton and Polvani (2007): the first day the zonal-mean wind at 60°N and 10 hPa turns easterly, November to March. A second event in the same winter needs 20 straight westerly days in between, and a reversal that never recovers (at least 10 westerly days before 30 April) is the spring final warming, not an SSW. On MERRA-2 that finds 30 reversals since 1980. Two of them, 17 February 2002 and 28 November 2025, were one day of easterlies weaker than 0.2 m/s that NCEP R1 never shows; they are listed as unconfirmed and left out of every composite and rate. That leaves 28 events in 24 of 46 winters.

Dripping paint · how an event reaches the surface
Baldwin and Dunkerton (2001) showed that large stratospheric anomalies tend to work their way down over a few weeks and then hold the surface in the same state for a month or two. Drawn against time and height, the composite looks like paint dripping down a wall. This is that chart from MERRA-2: the polar-cap (65–90°N) geopotential height anomaly at 42 levels from 1000 to 1 hPa, standardised for each level and day of year, with each level’s linear trend removed. Above 5 hPa a step at August 2004 is removed too, when MERRA-2 began assimilating Aura MLS; that is a data change, not climate. The standard deviation is floored at a third of its winter peak, so late-spring days do not blow up.

Who moves first · waves, vortex, tropical upwelling
Does anything in the tropics give warning of a sudden warming? This chart answers the part of that question about the Brewer–Dobson circulation: whether the tropical lower stratosphere starts to rise faster (and so to cool) before the polar vortex breaks, or only once the planetary waves that break it are already arriving. The QBO and the vortex · observed and CMIP6
Holton and Tan (1980) found the winter vortex weaker when the equatorial winds of the quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO) blow from the east in the lower stratosphere. Left: the correlation between the October–November equatorial wind at each level (FU Berlin/KIT, Canton, Gan and Singapore soundings) and the zonal-mean wind at 60°N, 10 hPa in November–December and January–February, over 68 winters from 1958/59 (MERRA-2 from 1980, NCEP R1 before). The relation is strongest at 30–50 hPa: r = +0.44 to +0.52 with the November–December vortex (p < 0.001) and +0.30 to +0.39 with January–February (p ≤ 0.012). El Niño and sudden warmings · CMIP6, 25,000 winters
Does El Niño bring sudden warmings? The observed record is too short to say: since 1957 neither El Niño nor La Niña winters differ significantly from neutral ones in how often a warming came (Fisher’s exact test, p 0.25 and 0.14), so the observed winters are not drawn.

Age of stratospheric air · MERRA-2 GMI, 1980–2019
The age of stratospheric air is how long ago, on average, the air at a place was last at the ground. Air enters the stratosphere through the cold tropical tropopause, is lifted by the tropical branch of the Brewer–Dobson circulation, spreads poleward and sinks at high latitudes, while breaking waves mix old air back toward the tropics. Age therefore grows with height and toward the poles. It sums up the whole circulation in one number — a faster overturning makes younger air — and it is the quantity on which models and observations disagree (Waugh and Hall 2002).

How it works

1. Lining up. The 500 hPa flow always carries a standing wave-1 and wave-2, set by the Rockies, the Tibetan Plateau and the contrast between oceans and continents. A forecast’s wave is that normal wave plus the anomaly the weather adds. Where the anomaly’s ridges sit on the normal ridges, the two add up to a bigger wave; half a wave away, they cancel. That, more than the anomaly’s size, sets how much wave activity goes up: in ERA5 the largest anomalies have been followed by several times more heat flux at 100 hPa lined up than opposed (the curve above).

2. Leaning. A planetary wave carries heat toward the pole only if it leans westward with height. In the schematic the air follows the meandering jet: north on the western side of each ridge, south on the eastern side. Stand the wave upright and the warm air sits right over the ridges, so as much warm air goes south as north and nothing is carried. Lean the upper ridge west and the warm air moves onto the northward side: heat goes north, and at 100 hPa that heat flux is the upward flux of wave activity into the vortex. It grows with the square of the wave’s size, so the bigger wave that lining up makes carries far more. Lean it east and heat goes south: wave activity coming back down.

3. The door. The waves still have to get in. Planetary waves climb only through westerly winds that are not too strong (Charney and Drazin 1961): the summer easterlies stop them all, and a winter vortex lets through only the longest, waves 1 and 2 above all. Once inside, the vortex steers them: one already disturbed can funnel them toward the pole, and a strong jet aloft can turn them back down. That is why lining up explains only part of the 100 hPa flux, about a quarter of its five-day changes in ERA5; the rest is the lean, the travelling waves and the door. The refractive index shows this forecast’s waveguide, and the regional reflection index whether waves are being turned back.
