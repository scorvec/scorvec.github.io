Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI)

RONI is an ENSO index that subtracts the tropical-mean SST anomaly (20°S–20°N) from the Niño-3.4 anomaly, then applies a 3-month running mean. By removing the slowly-rising tropical background, it isolates the ENSO signal more cleanly than the conventional ONI — increasingly useful as overall ocean temperatures trend upward.

How to read it: Red bars (positive) lean El Niño, blue bars (negative) lean La Niña; the dashed lines mark the ±0.5°C guides. Following the NOAA-CPC / ECMWF method, the relative anomaly is rescaled by a per-calendar-month factor s = σ(ONI) / σ(relative) — which restores the variance lost by removing the tropical mean, so RONI stays in °C and is directly comparable to the conventional ONI (same ±0.5°C thresholds). That factor has a strong seasonal cycle — largest in spring (~1.3, when removing the tropical background strips the most signal) and near 1 in mid-summer. Computed directly from NOAA OISST v2.1 (1991–2020 base).

Daily Equatorial Pacific SST Indices

All three daily metrics on one axis: the Niño-3.4 anomaly (the basis of ONI), the tropical-mean anomaly (20°S–20°N), and their difference — the daily relative index that underlies RONI. These are single-day values, so they are noisier than the 3-month running indices above, but they show the most recent evolution.

What to look for: When the red (Niño-3.4) and green (tropical-mean) lines move together, the blue relative line stays flat — that's background warming, not ENSO. The blue line separating from zero is the genuine ENSO signal.

MEI.v2 Daily Nowcast

NOAA PSL's Multivariate ENSO Index v2 is the leading combined-EOF of five tropical-Pacific fields (sea-level pressure, SST, surface zonal & meridional wind, and OLR), but it is published only as an overlapping bimonthly value with a ~5-week lag. This is a daily nowcast of it: a regression of the published MEI.v2 onto freely-available daily ENSO components — the Niño-3.4 SST anomaly, the Southern Oscillation Index, and the equatorial 850-hPa zonal wind — driven forward each day. Two trailing windows are shown: a faithful 30-day curve and a responsive 14-day “leading edge”.

How to read it: The grey steps are the official bimonthly MEI.v2; the red line is our daily estimate, which leads the official value by roughly five weeks. The shaded band is the ±0.30 leave-one-year-out cross-validation error — the honest out-of-sample skill (the fit reproduces MEI.v2 at R = 0.94). Caveat: the model is SST-weighted, so during a fast SST-led transition it can run ahead of MEI's slower atmospheric components — read a rapidly-rising live value as direction-and-timing rather than exact magnitude.

Equatorial Convection — Synthetic OLR Hovmöller

Where the deep convection is, and which way it’s moving. NOAA’s interpolated-OLR product ended in 2022, so this derives an OLR proxy in real time from the GMGSI global longwave-IR satellite mosaic: the IR brightness temperature (cold cloud tops) is converted to outgoing longwave radiation, and the 5°S–5°N zonal mean is stacked as a longitude × time Hovmöller.

How to read it: yellow/orange/red = low OLR = deep convection; blue = high OLR = suppressed / clear sky. An eastward (top→bottom-right) tilt is the MJO / convective envelope propagating east; convection parked at the dateline is the El Niño-shifted warm pool. Newest day at the bottom.

NOAA GMGSI longwave-IR global mosaic · McIDAS T_b + Ohring-Gruber OLR · 5°S–5°N daily mean updated through the day

Tropical Pacific — Live IR Satellite Loop

The actual clouds behind the OLR Hovmöller. NOAA already blends GOES-East/West, Himawari and Meteosat into the GMGSI global longwave-IR mosaic on a regular lat/lon grid — so this is seamless and georeferenced (no stitching, no satellite-switch parallax). We crop the tropical Pacific, draw coastlines for orientation, and loop the last 72 hours.

What to look for: white = cold cloud tops = deep convection. Watch the convective clusters near the Maritime Continent and along the ITCZ, and any flare-up east of the dateline — that’s the El Niño-shifted warm pool firing. Use the slider to step through the loop.

Colombia & Panama — True-Colour Satellite Loop

High-resolution true-colour (visible) imagery over the Colombia/Panama region from GOES-East (NOAA’s GeoColor product via NASA GIBS), which sits almost directly overhead here — so the view is about as sharp as geostationary gets. Country borders and coastlines are overlaid in yellow for orientation.

Because it’s visible imagery, the loop runs through the daylight hours of the last two days (night frames are skipped). Cyan + marks are GOES GLM lightning flashes from the 15 minutes before each frame, so you can see exactly which cells are electrified. Use the slider to watch the convection and sea-breeze cloud build over the Andes, the Pacific coast and the Caribbean.

Tropical Waves and Variability — OLR Hovmöller

Longitude × time Hovmöller of the equatorial outgoing-longwave-radiation (OLR) anomaly, averaged 5°S–5°N around the full globe. OLR is derived in real time from the GMGSI longwave-IR satellite mosaic (McIDAS brightness temperature → OLR), and the anomaly is taken against NOAA’s interpolated-OLR daily climatology (1979–2022). The shaded field is the total anomaly — green = low OLR / deep convection, brown = high OLR / suppressed.

Each overlaid component is isolated from that anomaly field, contoured at its convective (solid) and suppressed (dashed) phase. Kelvin, MJO and equatorial Rossby are Wheeler–Kiladis wavenumber–frequency bandpass filters of the anomaly (2-D FFT in longitude and time): Kelvin = eastward wavenumber 1–14, period 2.5–30 d, equivalent depth 8–90 m; MJO = eastward wavenumber 1–5, period 30–96 d; ER = westward wavenumber 1–10, period 9.7–48 d, equivalent depth 8–90 m. Low-frequency is a 120-day Lanczos low-pass in time with the zonal mean removed.

About these plots

This monitor pulls together a range of ENSO and tropical-climate diagnostics, each computed from public source data (not screen-scraped) and regenerated automatically on its own schedule, then committed to the site's GitHub repository. Daily: the global and tropical-Pacific SST anomaly maps, RONI, and indices (NOAA OISST); the equatorial subsurface-temperature cross-section (NOAA/PMEL TAO/TRITON moorings); observed equatorial surface winds (Copernicus Marine scatterometer / ASCAT); and — from the ECMWF AIFS-ENS / IFS-ENS ensembles — the equatorial wind Hovmöller, the Southern Oscillation Index forecast, and atmospheric angular momentum. Monthly: the CanSIPS Niño-3.4 seasonal forecast (Environment and Climate Change Canada). Weekly: the El Niño analog comparisons against 1997, 2015 and 2023. RONI is computed directly — the cosine-latitude-weighted Niño-3.4 anomaly minus the 20°S–20°N tropical mean, 3-month smoothed — rather than fetched, then rescaled by the per-calendar-month σ(ONI)/σ(relative) factor (NOAA-CPC / ECMWF method) so it stays in °C and comparable to ONI (1991–2020 OISST).

Data & acknowledgments: ECMWF AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS forecasts (wind Hovmöller, SOI, atmospheric angular momentum) are used under the ECMWF open-data licence (CC BY 4.0, © ECMWF). Climatologies and the reanalysis-based fields use ERA5 from the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) / ECMWF — generated using Copernicus Climate Change Service information; neither the European Commission nor ECMWF is responsible for any use of the data. Other sources: NOAA OISST & PSL, NOAA/PMEL TAO/TRITON, Copernicus Marine Service (ASCAT winds), the Australian Bureau of Meteorology / Queensland Govt LongPaddock (SOI), and Environment and Climate Change Canada (CanSIPS).
