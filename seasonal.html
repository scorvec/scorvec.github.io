Seasonal model forecasts

Eight C3S seasonal systems from seven centres, each read against its own 1993–2016 hindcast so the models compare even though their biases do not. Every system carries the global maps and the tercile probabilities; ECMWF SEAS5 carries the member-level work as well — ENSO and ocean indices, teleconnections and the impact products, with its stratosphere on the stratosphere page — because its full ensemble is the one the site stores. NOAA’s SFS beta, the successor to CFSv2, has its own group: 31 members against its own 1991–2020 reforecast. Canada’s CanSIPS has a group read straight from Environment and Climate Change Canada that runs for twelve months; its months 7–12 are CanSIPS alone. Pick a figure from the rail.

Anomaly maps — one system at a time, or their mean

Each system’s ensemble-mean anomaly, taken by C3S against that system’s own 1993–2016 hindcast. That is the comparison you want — a seasonal model’s raw field is dominated by its own drift — and it is what makes eight systems affordable: a megabyte a field, against the gigabytes the members and their matching hindcast run to. The multi-model mean weights every system equally rather than by member count, so NCEP’s 124 members and ECCC’s 20 count the same. Where the systems disagree, step through them: the mean alone will not tell you.

Tercile probabilities — how firmly, not just which way

Each system’s live members counted against its own 1993–2016 hindcast distribution at every grid point — the 33rd and 67th percentiles of that model’s own climate. Colour is the probability of whichever category is most likely, only where it reaches 40 % and the members’ split differs significantly from one third each (chi-square, p < 0.05 and a false-discovery rate of 0.10 over the map: from 51 members a 40 % category is well within chance); near-normal is never drawn, because it is the category a model lands on when it has nothing to say. Temperature is counted against the hindcast’s linear trend extrapolated to the valid year — against a flat 1993–2016 base the warming alone puts most of the tropics in the upper tercile every month. The multi-model map averages the per-system probabilities with equal weight per system.

Population-weighted temperature — monthly means, every system

How warm the month is for the people who feel it: every member weighted by population on the same geonames cells the SEAS5 card uses, for the United States and Brazil. Anomalies are each member minus that system’s own 1993–2016 hindcast mean at the same lead, so the systems are comparable; raw levels carry each model’s bias and are there for the read within one system. The band is P10–P90 across that system’s members, with P25–P75 darker; Multi-model pools every system’s members with equal weight per system, so NCEP’s 124 and ECCC’s 20 count the same.

The black line is observed — CPC daily Tmax/Tmin on the same cells, averaged to a monthly mean, with anomalies against the observed 1993–2016 mean so history and forecast share one base. It carries no band: the width here is the spread across members, and an observed month has one realisation.

This is a monthly distribution, not a daily one. The width is the spread of monthly MEANS, which is narrower than the day-to-day spread and says nothing about a cold snap — that question needs 6-hourly members, which is why the daily version below exists for ECMWF alone.

Population-weighted temperature — the daily distribution, every system

Every member, every day, population-weighted into one national number and pooled by month: 1,500 member-days for a 50-member system, 3,600 for NCEP’s 120. This is the distribution a cold snap lives in — the monthly card above shows the spread of monthly MEANS, which is a different and much narrower thing. Bands are P1–P99, P5–P95 and P25–P75 of the pooled member-days, with the median in sienna.

The black line is observed: CPC Global Unified daily Tmax/Tmin, population-weighted on the same cells and pooled the same way, with anomalies against the observed 1993–2016 mean — the models’ hindcast period, not CPC’s 1991–2020 normal — so history and forecast share one base. Its P10–P90 band is drawn on the raw basis only.

Days are matched on valid time, not lead: UKMO, NCEP and BoM are lagged ensembles whose members start on different days, so the same lead hour is a different calendar day for each of them. A day missing more than one of its four 6-hourly samples is dropped rather than averaged into a false daily mean, which is why a system shows slightly fewer members here than in its monthly ensemble. Anomalies use each system’s monthly hindcast mean at that lead; calibrating the tails themselves needs a daily hindcast, which has not been pulled.

PDO, AMO, Indian Ocean Dipole, Atlantic Niño

Same fields, same treatment. The PDO is the projection of each member’s North Pacific anomaly onto the ERSST EOF pattern the daily monitor uses, with the global mean removed and NCEI’s scale applied; the AMO is the relative form (North Atlantic minus the 60°S–60°N mean), so the warming trend does not masquerade as a basin mode. Because the anomaly base here is the model’s 1993–2016 hindcast rather than 1991–2020 observations, absolute levels can sit a tenth or two from the published indices; the shape and the run-to-run change are what to read.

Maps: anomaly, and what changed since the previous issue

One map at a time, most of the world on a plate carrée grid. Anomaly is this issue’s ensemble mean against its own 1993–2016 start-month hindcast. Calibrated terciles

For each grid point, season and lead, the tercile boundaries come from SEAS5’s own hindcast — 24 years × 25 members, 600 seasonal means — and the 51 forecast members are counted against them. That removes the model’s mean bias and its spread bias point by point, which is the calibration that matters most for a single-system tercile map. Shading is the most likely category and its probability; white means no category reaches 40 %, or the 51 members’ split is not significantly different from one third each (chi-square, p < 0.05 and a false-discovery rate of 0.10 over the map). Near-normal is not drawn: with a realistic spread it is almost never the most likely tercile, and where it is, the signal is weak anyway. Four overlapping seasons follow the start month (three before the October 2026 issue; the fourth ends at lead month 7, FMA for an October start); pick the variable and the season. Temperature and 500 hPa height are counted against the hindcast’s linear trend extrapolated to the valid year rather than its flat 1993–2016 mean: two decades of warming (and the thickness rise that goes with it) would otherwise sit inside “above normal” before any El Niño signal is added, and the whole tropical belt read 90–100 % warm by construction. What remains is the departure from the trend, which is where the winter picture becomes discriminating; the normals card below puts the same members against the 1991–2020 normal, the last decade and today’s trend.

Teleconnection indices, member by member

The CPC patterns — NAO, PNA, AO, the East Atlantic, West Pacific and the rest — read from each member’s monthly 500 hPa height (sea-level pressure for the AO) with the same projectors the GEPS subseasonal page uses, so the scale is CPC’s own. The Southern Oscillation index is Tahiti minus Darwin pressure. Two things keep this honest. First, the observed record: the black line is ERA5 passed through the same projection, and the red crosses are CPC’s published monthly index, an independent check on sign and size. Second, skill: for every index and month the 24 hindcast years’ ensemble mean is correlated with ERA5, and the observed index is regressed on the forecast to give a calibrated probability of each phase. Where the correlation is not significantly above zero (one-sided 5 % test over the 24 years: r below 0.34) the cell is hatched — the members still say something, but the hindcast says not to trust it, and the calibrated bar is set to climatology, at lead month 7 exactly as at the other leads. The board reads the seven months at a glance; the chart below it lets you look at one index at a time.

Cold-snap risk: runs of extreme days across the whole winter

The convex risk in one number: for every member and 1° cell, the daily-mean temperature (the mean of the day’s maximum and minimum, quantile-mapped from SEAS5’s hindcast onto the CPC record) is standardised against the CPC 1991–2020 day-of-year climatology, and the whole season is scanned for runs of three or more consecutive days beyond the threshold — November to February for United States cold, December to February for Brazil heat — so a snap that straddles a month boundary counts once. Shown as the chance of at least one such run, as that chance relative to the CPC record’s own seasons, and as the expected number of days inside such runs relative to normal. In the tropics a persistent 1.5 σ warmth is the El Niño and trend background rather than a heat wave; read Brazil at 2.5 σ. Click a city for its members.

Weather regimes: how often each circulation pattern occurs, against SEAS5’s own climate

The four winter weather regimes of the subseasonal pages — the same k-means clusters of 5-day-mean 500 hPa anomalies (NCEP/NCAR R1 1991–2020), for North America and the Pacific and for the Euro-Atlantic sector — counted day by day in every SEAS5 member. Each member-day is read against SEAS5’s own climate at the same start and lead (its whole 1981–2016 hindcast, 25 members, 36 years), so the model’s own bias in how often it produces each regime cancels, and the height trend is taken out first so warming does not masquerade as ridging. A dot is the forecast’s departure from that climate in percentage points of days, with the 90 % range from resampling the members. A shift is coloured only where it is meaningful: the hindcast, one year held out at a time, must show that SEAS5’s predicted regime shifts correlate with what ERA5 then observed (significant after a false-discovery correction across every cell, green columns), and the member range must clear zero. Everything else is grey, and a hatched column is one where the hindcast has shown no skill. Black diamonds are what strong El Niño winters actually did (ERA5 1959–2025, 1982 and 1991 left out for El Chichón and Pinatubo; hollow where that composite is not significant); open circles are SEAS5’s own hindcasts of the same winters.

Hindcast skill: correlation, over the 36 hindcast years, of SEAS5’s predicted frequency anomaly (each year held out of the model climate) with the observed one, with its 90 % bootstrap interval; printed only where it passes the false-discovery test (q = 0.10), “n.s.” elsewhere. RPC is the ratio of predictable components: above 1 the forecast is more skilful than its own spread implies, i.e. its shifts are too small — the seasonal “signal-to-noise paradox”.

El Niño winters in the hindcast (): the frequency shift SEAS5 predicted in those winters and the shift ERA5 observed, in percentage points, each against random sets of the same number of hindcast years; a number is printed only where it passes the false-discovery test.

What this page reads, and how

ECMWF’s seasonal system, read in full and checked against observations: the ENSO indices from the coastal Niño-1+2 to the central-Pacific Niño-4, each against the previous three issues; the Pacific, Atlantic and Indian Ocean indices from the same fields; what changed since last month; the CPC teleconnection patterns, with the model’s own hindcast skill (the stratosphere, tested the same way, is on the stratosphere page); anomaly and tercile maps against the hindcast and against observed normals; and the forecast for population-weighted temperature in the United States and Brazil set against normals and records. Model drift is removed everywhere by anomalising each member against SEAS5’s own 1993–2016 hindcast for the same start month and lead. The other seven systems are on this page too, in the maps and tercile cards above; what stays ECMWF-only here is the member-level work, because SEAS5 is the ensemble the site stores in full. NOAA’s SFS beta is read separately, member by member, against its own reforecast (the NOAA SFS group).

Data and method

The multi-model cards read three collections. Anomaly maps and the ocean indices come from seasonal-postprocessed-single-levels, which publishes each system’s ensemble-mean anomaly against its own 1993–2016 hindcast — about a megabyte a field, which is what makes eight systems affordable, and the reason those cards carry no spread. The tercile maps read seasonal-monthly-single-levels: this issue’s members and the matching 1993–2016 hindcast on the 1° grid, per system, with the hindcast cached per start month and reused every year; ECMWF is read from the copy already held for the member-level products. The Niño-3.4 plume is the full ensemble of every system, which is affordable because the box is small. Multi-model means weight every SYSTEM equally, never every member, so a 124-member ensemble does not speak for the group.

Forecast: ECMWF SEAS5 (C3S originating_centre=ecmwf, system=51), 51 members, monthly means for lead months 1–6 and the 6-hourly 2 m temperature, from the Copernicus Climate Data Store, and from the October 2026 issue a seventh lead month (April for an October start) rebuilt from the same run’s sub-daily fields (see Lead month 7 below); hindcast: the same system’s 1993–2016 re-forecasts, 25 members, for the same start month, and likewise for the previous issue. Sea-surface temperature, 500 and 1000 hPa height, sea-level pressure and stratospheric winds on a 2° grid; 2 m temperature, precipitation, evaporation, 10 m wind, solar radiation and 500 hPa height on a 1° grid over the Americas; geopotential at 10, 50 and 100 hPa poleward of 60° in each hemisphere.

Lead month 7 (from the October 2026 issue). The CDS monthly product stops at lead month 6, but SEAS5 runs 215 days, so the seventh month (April for an October start) is rebuilt from the same run’s sub-daily fields: instantaneous fields averaged over every step from 06 UTC on the 1st (12 UTC for pressure levels) to 00 UTC on the 1st of the next month, accumulations as the month’s total over its seconds. Applied to month 6 of the same issue, that rule reproduces the monthly product to its packing precision (largest difference 0.002 K in 2 m temperature, 0.3 m²/s² in 500 hPa geopotential, 1e-11 m/s in precipitation). The 1993–2016 hindcast reference for month 7 comes from the daily copy of the C3S SEAS5 archive on AWS (Planette, Copernicus licence) for the instantaneous fields and from the CDS sub-daily hindcast for the accumulations and the 50/100/1000 hPa heights and 30/50 hPa winds. Checked at months 5 and 6 against the CDS monthly hindcast, the AWS copy is identical for smooth fields (polar-cap and Níño-box means, 500 hPa height, 60°N wind) but differs on the grid for 2 m temperature (rms 0.2 K, up to 3 K on coasts and mountains), coastal SST box means (up to 0.18 K) and snow depth; those offsets do not change between months 5 and 6, so each such field is moved onto the CDS hindcast mean using its month-6 offset. Not extended, so shown to month 6: 10 m wind speed and solar radiation (the monthly wind speed is not reproducible from the sub-daily winds), and the 100 hPa vortex wind. The seventh month has less skill; it goes through the same calibration and trend handling as the others and the same tests where a product has one (the teleconnection skill test, the tercile maps’ signal test); the anomaly and change maps are untested ensemble means at every lead, and the change maps have nothing to compare it with in the previous issue.

Observations: ERA5 (daily 1.5° fields, 1959 or 1991 to the present depending on the field), monthly-meaned for normals, trends, records and skill; CPC’s Niño indices (the ERSSTv5 table, RONI on ERSSTv6) for the observed Niño lead-in, continued past CPC’s last published month by the site’s OISST v2.1 estimate, drawn dashed; NOAA CPC Global Unified daily maximum and minimum temperature (0.5°, 1991–2020, via NOAA PSL) for the threshold-day normals. Teleconnection projectors are the CPC-style regression patterns built for the GEPS page on NCEP/NCAR R1 (1991–2020). Population weights: GeoNames (CC BY 4.0) places of 15,000 or more people. The hindcast and observed references are reduced once to compact tables, so each monthly run in GitHub Actions downloads only the new forecast.

Method: indices are cosine-weighted box means; anomalies are member minus hindcast mean at the same lead (member minus the hindcast’s linear trend for geopotential heights, which carry the warming signal). Relative Niño-3.4 follows the CPC/ECMWF construction used elsewhere on this site; the Trans-Niño index is Trenberth & Stepaniak (2001), standardised by the hindcast spread. Terciles against the hindcast use the 33.3rd and 66.7th percentiles of the 600 hindcast samples per grid point; against observed references the members are first bias-corrected (member − hindcast mean + ERA5 1993–2016 mean, multiplicatively for rain and sunshine) and the bounds are ±0.43σ of the observed 1991–2020 spread around the reference (detrended for the trend reference; empirical percentiles for rain and sunshine). Standardised anomalies divide the ensemble-mean anomaly by the reference’s year-to-year standard deviation. Skill is the correlation of the 24 hindcast years’ ensemble mean with ERA5 at the valid month. Teleconnection phase probabilities are calibrated by regressing the observed monthly index (ERA5, same projection) on the hindcast ensemble mean over those 24 years for each calendar month and lead, and reading the ±0.5σ tail areas of the regression’s predictive distribution — Student t on 22 degrees of freedom, its spread widened by √(1 + 1/n + (x − x̄)²/Sxx) because the fit is in-sample on the same 24 years. Skill is tested with a one-sided t-test of r > 0 on n − 2 degrees of freedom (one start a year, so the years are independent): at n = 24 a cell needs r ≥ 0.344 for p ≤ 0.05; below that the probabilities are climatology (the observed mean and spread of the 24 years). The AO, EPO, WPO and SOI, which have no built-in standardisation, are expressed in ERA5’s 1991–2020 standard deviation for the calendar month, applied identically to model and observations. Built by scripts/sst/seas5_outlook.py and its companions; the JSON behind the charts: seas5_outlook.json, seas5_tele.json, seas5_popT.json, seas5_pme_regions.json, seas5_normals.json, seas5_regimes.json.

NOAA SFS beta (the NOAA SFS group, moved here from its own page in October 2026): NOAA’s next-generation Seasonal Forecast System, the UFS-based replacement for CFSv2, running in near-real-time beta since March 2026. NOAA publishes the raw ensemble output openly but few graphical products; the Niño-3.4/RONI plumes, the IOD/SASD/PDO indices and the monthly anomaly-map loops are computed directly from the full 31-member zarr output and refreshed with each monthly issue (published by the 6th of each month). It is a research prototype, not yet an operational product — ensemble size, physics and post-processing are still evolving, and months may occasionally be late or revised. The reforecast climatology uses 11 members per year over 1991–2020, init-month specific, so lead-dependent drift cancels by construction. Data: NOAA Open Data Dissemination, noaa-oar-sfsdev-pds on AWS. Built by scripts/sfs/*.py; the JSON behind the charts: sfs_nino34.json, sfs_indices.json.

CanSIPS (the CanSIPS group, October 2026): Environment and Climate Change Canada’s 40-member system read from its MSC Datamart to twelve months, with its own 1990–2020 hindcast (1993–2016 used, as for C3S). Our anomaly maps colour only significant cells; the probability maps are ECCC’s own. Built by scripts/sst/cansips_dd.py and cansips_maps.py in cansips.yml. Data Source: Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC Data Servers End-use Licence).
