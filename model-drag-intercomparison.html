Conservation and drag in the US, ECMWF and Canadian global models

Every global forecast model has to put angular momentum into the atmosphere and take it out again through the ground: mountains push on the air where the surface pressure differs across them, and the surface, its roughness and its unresolved hills drag on the wind. How strongly a model does this, and whether its angular momentum then changes by exactly what its torques say, are questions about its drag and its conservation. This study runs eight global systems from the United States, ECMWF and Canada through one angular-momentum budget, on each model's own grid and with one arithmetic, against ERA5 and against the 2012 WGNE Drag Project. 00Z forecasts from 2026-09-14 to 2026-10-14; the sample grows daily.

1. What the 2012 drag intercomparison found, and what has changed since

The WGNE Drag Project (Zadra et al., 2013; Sandu et al., 2016) asked ten models for their subgrid surface stress averaged over the first 24 hours of a month of forecasts, January and July 2012. Over the oceans, where all the stress comes from the boundary-layer scheme, the models largely agreed. Over land they did not: models of comparable resolution differed by as much as 20% in zonal-mean total subgrid stress, and by a factor of two to three in its split between the boundary-layer and the subgrid-orography schemes. IFS had the largest boundary-layer stress over mountains and the smallest orographic stress; the Met Office, JMA and Bureau of Meteorology models had the opposite split; the Canadian GDPS (then 25 km) and Météo-France's ARPEGE lay in between. The project began after the Canadian centre reported at WGNE in 2012 that adjusting its orographic blocking and boundary-layer schemes had improved large-scale forecasts, and Sandu et al. (2016) showed that the partition itself changes the circulation. Operational experience since has generally pointed the same way: more low-level drag has tended to improve medium-range scores.

All three model families here have changed their drag since 2012:

  • GEM (ECCC). The 2019 upgrade touched every part of GEM's drag. The GDPS went from 25 to 15 km and from 80 to 84 levels in July 2019 (GDPS 7.0.0; ECCC, 2019). Its physics was rebuilt (McTaggart-Cowan et al., 2019), including the low-level blocking, gravity-wave drag, boundary-layer and surface-layer schemes; the GDPS boundary layer became a revised moist-TKE scheme with a new mixing length and boundary-layer clouds (ECCC, 2019). The orography filter was weakened: the old smoother damped wavelengths out to about 20 grid lengths, the new scale-selective low-pass filter cuts off at 3 grid lengths in the GDPS (Zadra, 2018; Zadra, 2022), giving a much sharper model orography. GEPS took the same GEM 5 physics and the sharper orography in December 2021 (GEPS 7.0.0, 39 km, 84 levels), with its own critical phase for the blocking height and a dry-air mass constraint where the GDPS conserves total mass (ECCC, 2021); it runs at 25 km since June 2024, with stochastically perturbed parameters that include the strength of low-level blocking, the boundary layer's critical Richardson number and the non-orographic gravity-wave source (ECCC, 2024). Since 26 May 2026 the GDPS is a hybrid: between about 850 and 250 hPa its winds and temperature at scales longer than about 2,500 km are spectrally nudged toward ECCC's AI model GEML (GDPS 10.0.0; ECCC, 2026). GEPS is not nudged, so GEPS is the cleaner test of the 2019 physics.
  • IFS (ECMWF). Kanehama et al. (2022) rebuilt the subgrid-orography fields to represent scales below the model's effective resolution, weakened turbulent orographic form drag and strengthened low-level flow blocking, which raised the total parametrized stress in the northern mid-latitudes by 4–8% and made the momentum budget consistent across resolutions. Cycle 48r1 (June 2023) introduced improved orographic fields for the drag and the 9 km ensemble.
  • GFS (NOAA). GFS v16 (March 2021) replaced the boundary layer with a scale-aware TKE-EDMF scheme, added non-stationary gravity-wave drag, and went to 127 levels with a model top near 80 km (NCEP EMC, 2021). GEFS is still v12 (September 2020), at C384 on 64 levels.

What this study adds to the 2012 comparison: the 2026 versions; three AI models, which have no drag scheme at all and whose torques are whatever their pressure and wind fields imply; a free-forecast budget that asks whether each model's drag drains or feeds its angular momentum against its own analysis and ERA5, by lead and hemisphere; the resolved mountain torque on each model's own grid, the place where GEM's 2019 change should show because GEM publishes no surface stress; and day-1 stresses in the WGNE form for the models that publish them, in January and July 2026, next to the 2012 values.

Inits used so far; "own surface stress" is what the model publishes in its open data. IFS-ENS member 0 is the 9 km deterministic run, identical in configuration to the ensemble control since cycle 48r1, which the open data publish to day 10. GDPS forecasts run to day 10. Raw fields are read once and reduced on the fly; nothing is stored but the diagnostics.

  • Angular momentum $M = M_r + M_\Omega$: the wind term $M_r = \frac{a^3}{g}\int u\cos^2\varphi\,d\lambda\,d\varphi\,dp$ between level mid-points, from 0 hPa to the surface pressure (layers below ground removed), and the mass term $M_\Omega = \frac{\Omega a^4}{g}\int p_s\cos^3\varphi\,d\lambda\, d\varphi$. The part of $M_\Omega$ that changes only because a model's total mass changes ($\tfrac23\Omega a^2\Delta m$) is taken out of the budget, since no torque produces it. Comparisons between models use the 11 levels every model publishes (1000, 925, 850, 700, 500, 400, 300, 250, 200, 100, 50 hPa); each model's own budget uses all its levels.
  • Torques on the atmosphere, positive when they add westerly momentum: mountain $T_m = a^2\int h\,\partial p_s/\partial\lambda\,\cos\varphi\,d\lambda\,d\varphi$ from the model's own orography and surface pressure on its own published grid, and again after averaging both to 0.5° and 1.5° (Brown, 2004); friction $T_f = -a^3\int \tau_x\cos^2\varphi\,d\lambda\,d\varphi$ from (a) one bulk law for every model, $\tau = C_d\,\rho\,|V_{10}|\,u_{10}$ with $C_d$ fitted per 5° cell and month to ERA5's turbulent stress 1991–2020 (the site's torque product), and (b) the model's own published stress where there is one. The bulk law cannot see a drag scheme; its differences between models are differences in their 10 m wind. Torques are 6-hourly (so the daily and semidiurnal tides cancel) and averaged over each forecast day; angular momentum is taken at the 24-hour steps.
  • References. Each model's own analysis is its control member's step 0 on the verifying day (for the AI models the analysis they start from: NOAA's GDAS for AIGFS, ECMWF's for AIFS and AIFS-ENS); ERA5 (ARCO-ERA5, 2026-09-14 to 2026-10-07) goes through the same code on 37 levels, with its own turbulent and gravity-wave stresses. Drift against ERA5 is the forecast-minus-ERA5 difference at day d minus the same difference at day 0.
  • Regions as in the torque study: rough terrain (above 1000 m, or subgrid orography standard deviation above 100 m) plus 500 km downstream; ocean at 30–65°; other ocean; other land; each by hemisphere. One ERA5 land-sea mask for all models.
  • Statistics. Members are averaged within an init, so an init is the unit. Means carry 95% moving-block bootstrap intervals (blocks of 3 consecutive inits, 2,000 draws); model differences are paired on the same inits; each family of tests (leads × hemispheres, leads × regions, ranges) is controlled with Benjamini–Hochberg at a 10% false-discovery rate. Values that fail it are shown as n.s. and are not findings.

"own" uses the model's published stress, "bulk" the common friction law; "explained" is one minus the residual variance over the variance of dM/dt on day 1.

A model with complete published torques should close its budget to within its numerical errors. A residual with the model's own stress therefore measures what its open data leave out (IFS's subgrid-orography stress; any non-orographic gravity-wave momentum that leaves the top; momentum fixers) plus non-conservation in the dynamics. The bulk residual adds the error of the common friction law, the same law for every model, so its differences between models are differences in their 10 m winds and pressure. The GDPS residual also contains the nudging toward GEML, which adds or removes momentum in the free troposphere without any torque.

4. Free-forecast drift: draining or feeding angular momentum

The torque study found ERA5's analysis putting back westerly momentum that its forecast model loses, mostly below 850 hPa over the oceans and 83% of it in the Southern Hemisphere, and read that as boundary layers that mix too much momentum down. The free-forecast counterpart is a drift of each model's angular momentum away from its own analysis.

7. Mountain torque, resolution and GEM's 2019 changes

What the GEM numbers can and cannot separate. GEM publishes no surface stress, so the four 2019 changes show in different places. Finer resolution and the weaker orography filter should show as a larger resolved mountain torque on the native grid, in the part a 0.5° grid cannot see: compare the GDPS on its 0.15° output with itself averaged to 0.5°, with GEPS (0.5° output), and with the other models on both grids. The blocking and boundary-layer changes show only in totals: the drift of angular momentum against the model's own analysis and ERA5, by hemisphere (section 4), and the change of the low-level wind with lead over the oceans and over rough terrain (section 6). This study cannot split blocking from the boundary layer. 9. What is not like for like

  • Resolution and grids. 9 to 31 km native grids, published on 0.15° to 0.5° grids by interpolation (GFS's torque fields on its own Gaussian grid). Mountain torque is resolution-sensitive by tens of Hadley (Brown, 2004; the 0.5° and 1.5° columns); the friction torque is not much.
  • Vertical sampling. 12 to 41 published levels. The comparison uses the 11 common levels, which change the size of angular momentum more than its change (section 7). Below-ground layers are removed by each model's own surface pressure.
  • Stress definitions. GFS's UFLX is its turbulent stress and U-GWD its orographic gravity-wave and blocking stress; IFS's ewss includes turbulent orographic form drag but not blocking or gravity-wave drag; ERA5's turbulent stress includes form drag and its gravity-wave stress includes blocking. 2012 WGNE "PBL" and "SGO" follow each model's own split.
  • The bulk friction law was fitted to ERA5; applied to another model it measures that model's 10 m wind, not its drag.
  • Sample and season. Autumn 2026 (September–October), with El Niño developing; GEM inits only from 14 September and, for GEPS, every second day. Winter results can differ (the earlier IFS test was in December–February).
  • GDPS is a hybrid since May 2026: its large scales between 850 and 250 hPa follow an AI model, so its drift and budget are not GEM's physics alone. GEPS is the cleaner measure of GEM's physics.
  • Published orography. Each mountain torque uses the orography the model publishes with its surface pressure. GEM's fields are interpolated from its Yin-Yang grid to the output grid; if its published orography and surface pressure were interpolated or filtered differently, a spurious torque would follow, and the published fields cannot rule that out. The same holds, to a smaller degree, for every model published on a grid other than its own.
  • Members. GEPS members perturb drag parameters by design; IFS-ENS member 0 stops at day 10; ensembles use control + 4.

10. How this fits the earlier studies

Do AI weather models conserve angular momentum? tested AIFS-ENS, AIFS and IFS-ENS in winter 2025–26 with the same budget; Why the atmosphere spins up in El Niño measured ERA5's analysis increments and proposed that models' boundary layers mix too much westerly momentum down over the oceans. This study extends the free-forecast side of that test to the US and Canadian systems and puts their drag next to the 2012 comparison.
