How the scores are made

Made with Claude Code. The verification package behind this page, mverify, was written by Claude, Anthropic's AI model, with guidance from Shawn Corvec. The scores are a first sample and will grow as new runs are added.

Rain over the US. 24 hour totals ending 12Z against the Stage IV analysis, on the analysis's 4 km grid. The equitable threat score is the share of rain areas the model gets right beyond chance (1 is perfect). The fractions skill score asks the same question in neighbourhoods of growing size, so it forgives a band forecast a few tens of kilometres off.

Rain errors on the map. The same Stage IV totals averaged onto the common 0.5° grid wherever they cover at least half a cell, against each model's total for the same 24 hours, pooled over the runs and over the windows of a lead group (days 1–2, 3–5, 6–9). Each cell's bias is tested over the valid days: the day's error, averaged over the runs that verify it, goes through the scorecard's paired t-test with its allowance for errors that persist from day to day, once a cell has 8 days. The p values of all the cells of a map then go through the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at a 10% false-discovery rate, so the coloured cells are ones whose bias is unlikely to be noise. RMSE and the mean amounts are shown everywhere.

Rain gauges. The 24 hour totals that stations report (GHCNh; at 12Z from US ASOS stations, the same window as Stage IV) are scored like the other station variables, each model's total for those 24 hours interpolated to the gauge from the common grid, every model on the same readings. In the US they check the gridded scores at points rather than independently: Stage IV's analyses take in ASOS gauges.

NOAA's GFS, ECMWF's physics model (IFS), ECMWF's AI model (AIFS) and Environment Canada's global model (GDPS) are scored here against the same observations: hourly surface stations, about 650 radiosondes a day through the whole troposphere and, for rain over the US, the Stage IV radar and gauge analysis. Every model gets the same treatment. Each is first averaged onto one 0.5° grid, then interpolated to each station over land only, 2 m temperature is moved to the height of the station, and stations where the model's terrain is more than 100 m off are left out. Each radiosonde level is compared with the model where and when the balloon actually was.

  • Observations. Surface stations come from NOAA's GHCNh hourly archive (METAR and synoptic reports), screened for range, spikes and stuck sensors. Radiosondes come from NOAA's IGRA2 archive and the scorvec.com sounding mirror and are reduced to the standard levels from 1000 to 100 hPa, each level at the position the balloon reached it. Specific humidity is computed from the reported dew point. Precipitation is NCEP's Stage IV analysis.
  • One grid. Every model is averaged onto the same 0.5° grid before it is compared with anything, by the area each of its cells shares with each 0.5° cell, and its terrain and land-sea mask with it. Interpolated from their own grids, the 0.15° GDPS and the 0.25° models would be scored from cells of different size: a finer grid carries small-scale detail whose misplacement counts twice, and a model published on a grid finer than its own resolution looks sharper than it is. 0.5° is coarser than every model's own resolution.
  • Matching. Forecasts are interpolated bilinearly to each station from land points only, so coastal stations are not scored against sea temperatures. 2 m temperature is moved to station height with a standard lapse rate, 10 m wind is adjusted to the anemometer height, and stations where model and station heights differ by more than 100 m are dropped.
  • Surface pressure. Mean sea-level pressure is a reduction below ground that every model and station makes its own way, so pressure is scored as the model's surface pressure against the station's own reading. It is not moved to the station's height, so only stations whose height is within 5 m of every model's terrain are used: pressure falls about 0.12 hPa per metre near sea level, so the mismatch stays within 0.6 hPa, inside the observation error.
  • Pressure. Pressure is scored as mean sea-level pressure, because one model does not publish surface pressure; each model's own reduction to sea level is compared with the station's.
  • Scores. RMSE, bias, MAE and correlation for each variable, region and lead; precipitation on the analysis grid with the equitable threat score, frequency bias and the fractions skill score (Roberts and Lean 2008).
  • Same sample. Each model is scored only on the observations every model was matched to, so a model whose terrain or coastline drops a station is not judged on an easier set.
  • Significance. The scorecard tests each model pair with a paired t-test on the per-run mean squared errors. Runs a day apart share much of their weather, so the test counts fewer effective runs when the run-to-run differences are correlated (Geer 2016). The small number in each scorecard cell is that count of runs; hovering a cell gives the paired runs and the observations behind it.
  • Data. GFS from NOAA on AWS; IFS and AIFS from ECMWF open data (Google Cloud mirror), all published at 0.25°; GDPS from the ECCC Datamart at 0.15°; all scored on the common 0.5° grid.

The package is open source (github.com/scorvec/nwp_verification), so anyone can score their own model against the same observations. It reads GRIB, netCDF and Zarr:

pip install git+https://github.com/scorvec/nwp_verification
mverify db ghcnh --year 2026 --max-stations 4500     # build the observation database
mverify score MYMODEL=forecasts/ GFS=gfs/ --out scores.json
