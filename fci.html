US credit conditions · weekly, public Federal Reserve data

Made with Claude Code. This index was devised by Claude, Anthropic's AI model, with some guidance from Shawn Corvec: another just-for-fun project. It is an experiment, not an accurate or official measure of credit conditions, and not financial advice.

How tight is US credit?

One weekly number for how hard it is to borrow in the US, built from corporate bond spreads, commercial paper, bank business lending and the Fed's survey of loan officers, mortgage rates, the fed funds rate, the yield curve, stock-market volatility and drawdowns, and the dollar. Zero is the average since 2001; +1 means one standard deviation tighter than that. Seven sub-indices beside it show where the tightness is: for companies, for home buyers, in rates and the curve, in markets, in money-market liquidity, in private credit, or for owners borrowing against their homes (home-equity lines and refinancing).

Day by day

The headline index recomputed every business day with the same weights: spreads, commercial paper, fed funds, the curve, the VIX and the dollar at each day's value; the loan officer survey, bank lending, the weekly mortgage rate, the monthly stock drawdown and quarterly cash-out refinancing at their latest release. Bars: what each group contributes (above zero tighter). Daily values are noisier than the weekly averages above.

The index since 1990

Weekly, Friday dates. Grey bands: US recessions (NBER). Dashed: the Chicago Fed's National Financial Conditions Index credit subindex for comparison (also 0 = average, positive = tighter; its own scale).

Where the tightness is

Each sub-index is the plain average of its inputs' tightness scores (standard deviations from the 2001–present average, oriented so higher = tighter). They can disagree: companies can borrow cheaply while mortgages are expensive.

What is moving the headline

The headline index split into its inputs' contributions, grouped. Bars above zero push it tighter, below zero looser; the line is the index.

Corporate bond spreads, including high yield

The Federal Reserve Board's Gilchrist–Zakrajšek (GZ) spread averages the spreads of senior unsecured bonds of US nonfinancial companies, high yield included, over matched Treasuries; the excess bond premium is the part of it that expected defaults do not explain, the price investors charge for bearing corporate credit risk. Both are monthly and are plotted on the date each value became public. Moody's Baa spread, in the headline, for comparison.

The ICE BofA high-yield and CCC option-adjusted spreads cannot be shown: FRED marks them "Copyrighted: Pre-Approval Required" and now carries only their last three years. The GZ spread is the free measure that covers high-yield bonds.

Stocks: the equity risk premium and the drawdown

Left: the S&P 500's cyclically adjusted earnings yield (ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings over the price; Shiller) minus the 10-year real Treasury yield. A higher premium means cheaper stocks and a dearer cost of equity, so it counts as tighter. Right: how far the S&P 500's monthly average price sits below its highest monthly average of the past year. Monthly data, plotted when each value became public (prices a month late, earnings six months late).

Long-term rates: the real 10-year yield and the term premium

The 10-year TIPS yield (dotted before : a stated proxy, the 10-year Treasury minus core PCE inflation, shifted by its 2003–07 average gap to TIPS) and the Kim–Wright 10-year term premium, the extra yield investors demand to hold a long bond rather than roll short ones. Both feed the rates sub-index.

Liquidity: reserves, the reverse repo cushion and repo stress

Bank reserves at the Fed and money funds' cash in the Fed's overnight reverse repo facility, both as a share of commercial banks' total assets; and SOFR, the overnight Treasury repo rate, minus the interest the Fed pays on reserves. Smaller cushions and repo trading above the Fed's floor read as tighter (September 2019 is the spike). These histories start in 2009, 2013 and 2018, too late for the headline's 2001 weights, so they form their own sub-index, standardised over their own history.

Home equity and refinancing

How easily households can borrow against their homes. Banks' home-equity lines of credit (HELOCs) and the credit limits on them, what loan officers say about HELOC and mortgage standards and demand, how much refinancing pulls cash out of homes, and the lock-in gap: today's 30-year rate minus the average rate on every outstanding mortgage. When the gap is positive, most owners would refinance into a higher rate, so rate refinancing stops and cash-out refinancing becomes expensive; the cheapest way left to tap home equity is a HELOC.

Private credit

Private lending to companies has no public price. What public data does show is the bank funding behind it: commercial banks' loans to nonbank financial institutions, the credit lines that private-credit funds draw on (Federal Reserve H.8; the January 2025 reclassification jump is linked out of the growth rate). Slower growth reads as tighter. The series starts in 2015, so it forms its own sub-index and is not in the headline.

Who the banks lend to: the Call Report breakdown

Since December 2024 banks with $10 billion or more in assets split their loans to nonbank financial institutions by borrower type (Call Report schedule RC-C, item 9.a). Business credit intermediaries are private-credit funds and business development companies. Summed over every bank that files a Call Report, quarter-end, $ billion; smaller banks report only the total ("not broken out").

How the index is built
  • Inputs (all from FRED, St. Louis Fed): Moody's Baa and Aaa corporate yields minus the 10-year Treasury; 3-month AA nonfinancial and financial commercial paper minus the 3-month bill; year-on-year growth of commercial paper outstanding, of bank commercial & industrial loans (H.8) and of nonfinancial corporate debt (Financial Accounts, Z.1); the Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey's net share of banks tightening business-loan standards (large & middle-market and small firms) and net share reporting stronger demand; the effective fed funds rate and the Freddie Mac 30-year mortgage rate, both real (minus core PCE inflation over the past year); the mortgage rate minus the 10-year Treasury; the 10-year minus 2-year Treasury spread; and the Senior Loan Officer Survey's net share of banks tightening credit-card standards (admitted 2026-10-07, see the test above); and cash-out refinance volume as a share of mortgage debt (admitted in the second round, with a weight near zero).
  • Markets: the VIX (weekly average), the year-on-year change in the Fed's broad trade-weighted dollar (spliced to its predecessor before 2006), and how far the S&P 500's monthly average price sits below its 12-month high (Shiller's data; admitted 2026-10-07). With them the index also registers market-led tightenings that loan officers and spreads see later: late 2018, 2022, April 2025.
  • In the sub-indices only (they failed the admission test): the Fed Board's GZ corporate bond spread and excess bond premium (corporate); the equity risk premium (markets); the real 10-year yield and the Kim–Wright term premium (rates). Liquidity sub-index: reserves and overnight reverse repo as shares of bank assets, and SOFR minus the rate on reserves, each standardised over its own history (from 2009, 2013 and 2018) and averaged once two of the three exist. Context only: commercial real estate standards and business, card and CRE delinquency rates.
  • Home equity and refinancing sub-index: the year-on-year growth of banks' revolving home-equity loans (H.8, from 1987; one-off reclassification jumps linked out) and of home-equity credit limits at all lenders (New York Fed Consumer Credit Panel / Equifax, from 2003); the Senior Loan Officer Survey's net shares tightening HELOC standards and reporting stronger HELOC demand (asked since 2008) and the same for residential mortgages (three wordings spliced end to end: all mortgages to early 2007, prime mortgages to 2014, GSE-eligible mortgages since 2015); cash-out refinance volume, annualised, as a share of home mortgage debt (FHFA National Mortgage Database from 1998 and the Fed's Z.1); and the lock-in gap (from 2013). Slower growth, more banks tightening, weaker demand, less cash-out refinancing and a wider gap count as tighter. Each input is standardised over its own history since 2001; the sub-index averages whichever exist (at least four). The inputs with history from before 2001 also went through a second round of the admission test, added to the current headline (table above).
  • Admission rule for a new input: added to the current inputs and the weights refitted, it must not lower the index's correlation with the NFCI credit subindex by more than 0.005, nor the average episode-peak percentile by more than 0.5 points, nor any single episode's by more than 2 points, and it must carry real weight (a loading of at least 0.05 in the refitted index: an input the index barely uses is not admitted however neutral it is); candidates enter one at a time, best first, while one passes. Only inputs that exist from 2001 can compete: a later start would shorten the window the weights are fitted on.
  • Orientation: every input is signed so that a higher value means tighter credit (slower loan and debt growth, weaker loan demand and a flatter curve count as tighter).
  • Real rates: the fed funds and mortgage rates fell for forty years; in nominal terms that decline would dominate any statistical combination, so both enter net of core inflation.
  • Weights: the headline is the first principal component of the standardised inputs over 2001–present (when every input exists), signed so wider corporate spreads count as tighter and scaled to one standard deviation. It captures the common credit cycle: loan-officer standards, corporate spreads, the stock-market drawdown and volatility and commercial paper carry most of the weight, while the real fed funds rate, loan growth and corporate debt growth barely load and the yield curve loads with the opposite sign (curves steepen in recessions as spreads widen). That is why the sub-indices, which weight their inputs equally, sit beside it. The weights before and after the 2026-10-07 additions are in the table above.
  • No hindsight: each input enters a week only once it would have been published (bank loans about 9 days after the data week, core PCE about two months after the month, the loan officer survey about 50 days after its quarter date, Z.1 about five months after its quarter, the GZ spread 40 days after its month starts, National Mortgage Database originations 275 days and outstanding rates 95 days after the quarter ends, the New York Fed household debt data 50 days after the quarter ends, Shiller's prices a month and his earnings six months after the month, delinquency rates 150 days after the quarter starts), and is carried forward to the next release. Daily series are weekly averages. Before 2001 the index uses the inputs that existed, re-weighted (credit-card standards start in 1996). The GZ spread, the term premium and Shiller's earnings are revised after the fact and have no archived vintages, so their history is the latest estimate; so are the National Mortgage Database aggregates (late-reported loans fill in) and the New York Fed household debt series.
  • Check: the index correlates with the Chicago Fed's NFCI credit subindex since 1990; its peaks in each credit event are in the table above. Both checks are in sample: they are the measures the admission test used to choose the inputs, so they show fit, not independent validation.
