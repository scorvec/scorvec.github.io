MJO with the El Niño signal removed

Since 2014 the standard RMM no longer removes ENSO; in a strong El Niño its standing convection projects onto phases 7–8 and masquerades as a stalled MJO. This is the same AIFS-ENS forecast (wind-only), observed track and IFS overlay with the part tied to the Niño-3.4 change removed. Latest init: 2026-09-28 12Z.

No coherent MJO in the ENSO-removed forecast: ensemble-mean amplitude 0.7 (max 1.0) and a phase speed of +7.0°/day, where a real MJO moves at about +7°/day. NOAA’s OLR-based ROMI on 2026-09-24: amplitude 0.84 (phase 5); over the last 60 days 7 % of days reached amplitude 1. Correction applied: ΔNiño-3.4 = +0.96 K.

How the El Niño signal is removed

Wheeler & Hendon (2004) removed two things before projecting onto the MJO patterns: the part of each day’s anomaly linearly related to ENSO, then the mean of the previous 120 days. The Bureau of Meteorology did both until the end of 2013; since 2014 it removes only the 120-day mean, as this page’s raw index does. While an El Niño is growing, the 120-day mean trails the warming, so the leftover projects onto the MJO phases.

Here the leftover is taken out directly: the wind-only RMM minus ΔN34 × c, where ΔN34 is Niño-3.4 (NOAA OISST, 31-day mean) minus its mean over the same 120-day window, and c is the regression of the 120-day-filtered 850 and 200 hPa equatorial winds on ΔN34, longitude by longitude, projected onto the MJO patterns. It is fitted on ERA5 (ARCO) 2003–2024 with ERSST v6 Niño-3.4 (BoM used its own SST index, SST1). Against BoM’s ENSO-removed RMM (2003–2013, 3,899 days) the cleaned wind-only index correlates 0.96/0.96 (RMM1/RMM2), against 0.94/0.96 before, and its correlation with the Niño-3.4 change falls from -0.21 to -0.01. While an El Niño grows, it lowers the share of “active” days: JAS 2015 0.33 → 0.29; JAS 2023 0.38 → 0.29; JAS 2009 0.42 → 0.49. The forecast holds Niño-3.4 at its latest value, since ENSO changes little in 15 days.

The correction is linear, so it removes part of a growing El Niño’s projection, not all of it. The status line therefore also quotes NOAA’s OLR-based real-time OMI (ROMI), which removes the previous 40 days and so is far less sensitive to slow signals: an independent check on whether a real MJO is present. Wind-only (no precipitation channel), and no 12Z cycle offset correction, unlike the raw diagram above.

How this is built

MJO index. CMIP6: the Wheeler & Hendon (2004) RMM computed from each historical run’s own daily OLR and 850/250 hPa zonal wind (the CMIP6 daily archive has no 200 hPa), 15°S–15°N means, the run’s own seasonal cycle and previous 120-day mean removed, projected on the observed W&H EOFs. Observed, for comparison: the Bureau of Meteorology’s RMM, 1979–2024. Both indices are then band-passed to 20–100 days and renormalised. BoM removes the ENSO signal only up to 2013; after that a strong El Niño’s standing pattern projects on RMM as slow “phase 6–8” days (DJF 2015/16 was “active” on 89 % of days), and the CMIP6 index would carry the same artefact. A linear Niño-3.4 regression removed almost none of it, so the band-pass is used for both datasets alike. Check, DJF share of active days after the filter: strong El Niño winters 0.69 (n 4; 0.70 before), neutral 0.62 (n 13); the difference is not significant (Mann–Whitney p 0.69). Checks: the same machinery on ERA5 winds reproduces BoM’s RMM1/RMM2 at r 0.94/0.96 (wind-only, 1991-2020); substituting 250 for 200 hPa keeps the phase on 90 % of active days (r 0.99/0.99).

Impact fields (2 m temperature, precipitation, 500 hPa height), per grid cell and per dataset, in three steps. (1) A seasonal cycle that is allowed to change: the mean and three annual harmonics, each with a coefficient that varies smoothly in time (a natural cubic spline with knots about every 12 years: 4 over the 36 model years, 5 over ERA5 1979–2024, 3 over GPCP 1997–2024), so, for example, the Arctic’s faster winter warming is not left in the anomalies. (2) The same regression’s time-varying mean is the forced trend, smooth and nonlinear rather than a straight line. A model with two members is fitted on both together, that is on its ensemble mean, so the forced part is the model’s and not one member’s decade of weather; the daily ensemble mean is not subtracted, because with two members it holds half of each member’s own MJO. (3) A high-pass at 120 days: every Fourier component with a longer period is removed from what is left, which takes out ENSO and the rest of the interannual variability the same way the index filter does; the first and last 60 days of each record are dropped. (A 121-day running mean was tried first and left too much: a running mean cuts off gradually and still passes 78 % of a 150-day and 17 % of a one-year oscillation.) Check on 20 test series (central US and Alaska temperature, Colombia and south-east Brazil rain, Arctic 500 hPa height; observed and three models): the share of variance at periods over 120 days falls from 2–23 % to at most 0.1 %, and the trends of the DJF and JJA seasonal means are within about two standard errors of zero (2 of 40 beyond two, largest 2.4, as chance allows).

Composites. A day counts when the MJO amplitude is at least 1. For every such day in the chosen season (DJF, MAM, JJA, SON, or November–March for a larger winter sample; single months have a third of the sample) the anomaly is averaged over days −2 to +2 (“same days”) or +8 to +12 after it, and the composite is the mean over those days.

Model screen (fixed before looking at any composite): east/west power ratio of 10°S–10°N rain (wavenumbers 1–3, 30–96 days, November–April) ≥ 2.0 and eastward propagation (lag-regression pattern against GPCP) r ≥ 0.8; GPCP itself gives E/W 2.5. Used: CNRM-ESM2-1 (E/W 3.2, r 0.85), CNRM-CM6-1 (E/W 3.2, r 0.85), CAMS-CSM1-0 (E/W 2.7, r 0.91), CESM2 (E/W 2.5, r 0.91), MPI-ESM1-2-HR (E/W 2.8, r 0.81), GFDL-CM4 (E/W 2.3, r 0.95), EC-Earth3-CC (E/W 2.3, r 0.95), CESM2-FV2 (E/W 2.2, r 0.93), EC-Earth3 (E/W 2.1, r 0.95), IITM-ESM (E/W 2.3, r 0.84), BCC-ESM1 (E/W 2.2, r 0.82). Tests. CMIP6: each model’s composite (members pooled; at least 30 active days), across-model one-sample t-test with a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate of 10 % over the map, and at least two-thirds of the models agreeing on the sign. Observed: an MJO passage lasts days and daily anomalies are correlated, so the unit is the event (a run of consecutive active days in one phase), resampled with the whole lag window of every one of its days: an event-block bootstrap (2,000 resamples), the same 10 % false-discovery rate, and only phase-seasons with at least 8 events are tested.
