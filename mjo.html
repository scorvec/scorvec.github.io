About this diagram and the ENSO removal

Every model is projected the same way: the 15°S–15°N band means of the 850 and 200 hPa zonal wind, anomalies against the 1991–2020 climatology with the previous 120-day mean removed, projected onto the wind parts of the Wheeler & Hendon (2004) EOFs and normalised to unit variance. OLR is left out because AIFS-ENS does not output it; the GEFS and GEPS pages also carry their full three-field RMM. The GEFS track uses its own analyses for the 120-day filter; the others use the site’s ERA5 filter map. The observed track is ERA5 up to mid-July 2026 and the AIFS-ENS analysis since. ECMWF’s day 0 is the analysis; GEFS and GEPS day 1 is the first forecast day, so their tracks start a day in.

Since 2014 the standard RMM no longer removes ENSO; while an El Niño grows, the 120-day mean trails the warming and the standing convection over the central Pacific projects onto phases 6–8, which reads as a stalled MJO. The ENSO-removed index subtracts ΔN34 × c, where ΔN34 is Niño-3.4 (NOAA OISST, 31-day mean) minus its mean over the same 120-day window, and c is the regression of the 120-day-filtered equatorial winds on ΔN34, longitude by longitude, projected onto the MJO patterns, fitted on ERA5 (ARCO) 2003–2024 with ERSST v6 Niño-3.4. Against BoM’s ENSO-removed RMM (2003–2013, 3,899 days) the cleaned wind-only index correlates 0.96/0.96 (RMM1/RMM2), against 0.94/0.96 before, and its correlation with the Niño-3.4 change falls from -0.21 to -0.01. While an El Niño grows, the correction changes the share of “active” days: JAS 2015 0.33 → 0.29; JAS 2023 0.38 → 0.29; JAS 2009 0.42 → 0.49. The forecast holds Niño-3.4 at its latest value, since ENSO changes little in 15 days. The correction is linear, so it removes part of a growing El Niño’s projection, not all of it; the status line therefore also quotes NOAA’s OLR-based real-time OMI (ROMI), which removes the previous 40 days and so is far less sensitive to slow signals. ECMWF’s 12Z runs are offset-corrected onto the 00Z family as in the successive-runs view.

How this is built

MJO index. CMIP6: the Wheeler & Hendon (2004) RMM computed from each historical run’s own daily OLR and 850/250 hPa zonal wind (the CMIP6 daily archive has no 200 hPa), 15°S–15°N means, the run’s own seasonal cycle and previous 120-day mean removed, projected on the observed W&H EOFs. Observed, for comparison: the Bureau of Meteorology’s RMM, 1979–2024. Both indices are then band-passed to 20–100 days and renormalised, so nothing slower than 100 days enters either one. BoM removes the ENSO signal only up to 2013; after that a strong El Niño’s standing pattern can project on RMM as slow “phase 6–8” days, and the CMIP6 index would carry the same artefact. A linear Niño-3.4 regression removed almost none of it, so the band-pass is used for both datasets alike. Check: the filter changes the DJF share of active days little (strong El Niño winters 0.70 before, 0.69 after, n 4; DJF 2015/16 0.89 before, 0.87 after), so here it is a safeguard rather than a large correction. After it, strong El Niño winters do not differ significantly from neutral ones (0.62, n 13; Mann–Whitney p 0.69). Checks: the same machinery on ERA5 winds reproduces BoM’s RMM1/RMM2 at r 0.94/0.96 (wind-only, 1991-2020); substituting 250 for 200 hPa keeps the phase on 90 % of active days (r 0.99/0.99).

Impact fields (2 m temperature, precipitation, 500 hPa height), per grid cell and per dataset, in three steps. (1) A seasonal cycle that is allowed to change: the mean and three annual harmonics, each with a coefficient that varies smoothly in time (a natural cubic spline with knots about every 12 years: 4 over the 36 model years, 5 over ERA5 1979–2024, 3 over GPCP 1997–2024), so, for example, the Arctic’s faster winter warming is not left in the anomalies. (2) The same regression’s time-varying mean is the forced trend, smooth and nonlinear rather than a straight line. A model with two members is fitted on both together, that is on its ensemble mean, so the forced part is the model’s and not one member’s decade of weather; the daily ensemble mean is not subtracted, because with two members it holds half of each member’s own MJO. (3) A high-pass at 120 days: every Fourier component with a longer period is removed from what is left, which takes out ENSO and the rest of the interannual variability the same way the index filter does; the first and last 60 days of each record are dropped. (A 121-day running mean was tried first and left too much: a running mean cuts off gradually and still passes 78 % of a 150-day and 17 % of a one-year oscillation.) Check on 20 test series (central US and Alaska temperature, Colombia and south-east Brazil rain, Arctic 500 hPa height; observed and three models): the share of variance at periods over 120 days falls from 2–23 % to at most 0.1 %, and the trends of the DJF and JJA seasonal means are within about two standard errors of zero (2 of 40 beyond two, largest 2.4, as chance allows).

Composites. A day counts when the MJO amplitude is at least 1. For every such day in the chosen season (DJF, MAM, JJA, SON, or November–March for a larger winter sample; single months have a third of the sample) the anomaly is averaged over days −2 to +2 (“same days”) or +8 to +12 after it, and the composite is the mean over those days.

Model screen (metrics after Waliser et al. 2009, J. Climate 22, 3006–3030, and Ahn et al. 2017, Clim. Tests. CMIP6: each model’s composite (members pooled; at least 30 active days), across-model one-sample t-test with a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery rate of 10 % over the map, and at least two-thirds of the models agreeing on the sign. Observed: an MJO passage lasts days and daily anomalies are correlated, so the unit is the event (a run of consecutive active days in one phase), resampled with the whole lag window of every one of its days: an event-block bootstrap (2,000 resamples), the same 10 % false-discovery rate, and only phase-seasons with at least 8 events are tested.
