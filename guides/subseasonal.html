The page

ECCC’s GEPS extended ensemble, 21 members to day 35 from the Monday and Thursday runs, read against the 1991–2020 normal with the model’s own drift removed first, and every calibrated panel — teleconnections, tercile maps, regimes, scenarios and the MJO — scored on the 2001–2020 GEPS8 hindcast. The GEFS page uses the same methods on the GEFSv12 reforecast; its methods page lists where the two differ.

How every figure is built

The reference period

Open this figure

What the anomalies are measured against. The 1991–2020 normal, the period everyone quotes. Reaching it takes a step, because the hindcast spans 2001–2020 and the model's 1991–2020 climatology therefore does not exist — it has to be estimated. It is estimated by shifting the model climatology by the observed change between the two periods, so what is computed is

anomaly(L) = [ forecast(L) − model climatology(L) ] − [ ERA5 1991–2020 − ERA5 2001–2020 ]

The first bracket removes GEPS's drift and its mean bias, because both terms are the same model at the same lead — that is what the hindcast archive is for. The second moves the reference period, and nothing else: it is two decades of climate, measured from observations, with no model in it.

The correction is small and physical, which is the point: 1991–2020 is 0.12 K cooler than 2001–2020 and its 500 hPa surface sits 2.7 m lower, so anomalies quoted here are correspondingly warmer and higher. Precipitation moves 0.03 mm/day, outgoing longwave 0.05 W/m², the winds under 0.06 m/s.

What this deliberately avoids. Differencing straight against ERA5 would also be “vs 1991–2020”, and is the obvious way to do it — but it hands the model's mean bias back into the anomaly, measured here at +0.55 mm/day on precipitation and +9.7 W/m² on outgoing longwave. Those are an order of magnitude larger than the base-period change they would be sitting alongside, and they are a property of the model, not of the weather.

The model climatology and its corrections

Open this figure

The climatology. The GEPS8 reforecast (2001–2020, four starts a week, four members, 39 leads) was pulled from the IRI Data Library for nine variables — 2,160 monthly files, 132 GB — and reduced to a lead-dependent model climatology: for each variable, lead and day-of-year, the mean over hindcast starts within ±15 days of that date across all twenty years, roughly 340–540 starts per cell. Days of the year are counted on a 365-day calendar (1 March is day 60 in leap years too, 29 February sits halfway between 28 February and 1 March), so a date pools with the same date in every year, and each start counts once: the monthly files repeat the first start of a month as the last of the month before (154 starts), and those copies are dropped. The ERA5 normals of the re-basing below are built the same way. Anomalies are taken against that, at matching lead, so what the maps show is departure from how GEPS itself normally behaves that far into a forecast.

Two details that matter. The climatology is indexed by the day-of-year of the start, so it is read at the forecast's init day-of-year, the same for every lead; read at each valid date instead, it would compare a day-35 forecast with runs launched 35 days later, which over an autumn continent makes the reference about 8 K too cold and produces a fake warming that grows with lead. And the ensemble mean is weighted by member count: the perturbed file holds 20 members and the control one, so averaging the two files equally would give the control half the weight instead of a twenty-first, leaking one member's small-scale noise into every map.

The anomaly is computed on the climatology's grid, not the forecast's. The hindcast is 1° and the live forecast 0.5°, exactly nested. Interpolating the climatology up to 0.5° to keep forecast detail seems free but is not: the forecast resolves terrain and coastlines the 1° reference cannot represent, so that structure does not cancel and comes out as grid-scale speckle along every mountain range and shoreline — mean state masquerading as anomaly, and worst in precipitation. The forecast is therefore band-limited to the coarse grid first and the difference taken there. An anomaly cannot carry more resolution than its reference.

Outgoing longwave carries a model-version offset, which the maps remove. The live operational GEPS radiates 7.6–8.4 W m−2 less than the GEPS8 reforecast the climatology was built on (global mean, eleven cycles from 27 August to 1 October 2026) — flat across the 35 leads (spread under 1 W m−2 within a cycle), so it is not a forecast signal; a global-mean OLR anomaly that size is not physically possible. Left in, it tinted every map green and put spurious widespread negative anomalies over North America. The per-lead global mean is removed for OLR on the maps and the Hovmöller, as it is for height. The RMM needs no such step: its forecast OLR is filtered with GEPS’s own first-day OLR (below), which carries the same offset, so there it cancels.

Height is treated differently from the rest. Between the 2001–2020 hindcast epoch and today there is a real climate offset in geopotential height — a flat +16 m at every lead, measured — which would otherwise sit over every 500 hPa map as a uniform ridge. For z500 the per-lead global mean is removed so the circulation shows. For temperature and precipitation the raw anomaly is kept, because there the departure from the 2001–2020 climate is part of what you want to see. Each figure says which it is.

Caveats and data

Open this figure

Caveats. The weekly anomaly maps are an ensemble mean, so they understate the range of outcomes everywhere and increasingly so with lead; the range is in the member-based products: the tercile maps, the teleconnection and regime probabilities and the 500 hPa scenarios. Twenty-one members is also not many for precipitation: a single member differs from the member mean by about the field's own magnitude, so roughly a fifth of a single member's noise amplitude (1/√21, about 5 % of its variance) survives into the mean and shows up as coherent-looking blobs at long lead. Those are ensemble sampling noise, not forecast structure. Extended cycles run Monday and Thursday only. The hindcast is a fixed model version while the live forecast is the current operational one, so a slow model change is not captured by the 2001–2020 reference — the outgoing-longwave offset above is exactly that showing up.

The figures

MJO

Open this figure

The views. Phase: the three-field RMM (OLR, 850 and 200 hPa zonal wind), the 21-member plume to day 35; the observed track is the last 30 days, winds from GEPS’s own day-0 analyses and OLR from ERA5 spliced with the offset-corrected GMGSI satellite proxy. ENSO is removed from every track (below), and the footer gives the shift. In the 2001–2020 hindcasts the ensemble-mean amplitude shrinks with lead, to about 0.82 of the observed by day 35 (the Hindcast skill view), so a mean track that keeps growing without moving eastward is a standing pattern in the model, such as drift, not a propagating MJO; the figure says so when the peak is beyond the hindcast’s range. Hovmöller: OLR and 850 hPa zonal wind anomaly, 15°S–15°N, lead against longitude. Hindcast skill: the bivariate RMM correlation, RMSE and amplitude ratio of the 2001–2020 GEPS8 hindcasts against the observed RMM, by lead, for all starts, by season, for a strong initial MJO and for the starts within ±45 days of today; the red marks are ECMWF’s published extended-range values, for reference.

The OLR channel. The observed track’s OLR is ERA5, whose top-of-atmosphere longwave runs about 13 W m−2 above the NOAA interpolated OLR that the RMM reference climatology and EOFs were built on (1979–2001), so ERA5 is moved into that frame before anything is projected, by a fixed offset: ERA5's own 1979–2001 climatology minus NOAA's, by longitude and day of year. Being climatological, it follows the offset’s seasonal cycle and keeps what a mean over the recent ERA5 record would also have removed, part of a long El Niño's or La Niña's convection signal. The GMGSI proxy that covers the final few days is offset-corrected onto ERA5 per longitude over their last 90 overlapping days (over the 220-day overlap available when this was set up: r = 0.80 in the anomaly, spread within 3% of ERA5's) and screened day by day — the mosaic occasionally publishes a day with missing passes, and one such day at the end of the record would otherwise become the observed MJO's latest point.

The forecast’s OLR is filtered with GEPS’s own. Each RMM channel loses the mean of the 120 days before it, and that mean has to come from the same source as the channel. The forecast’s OLR anomaly, put into the reference frame with the reforecast’s first-day climatology, carries GEPS’s own OLR bias: its first day runs 12.6 W m−2 above the observed OLR of the same day in the band mean (13 cycles, 27 August to 15 October 2026). Filtered with the observed 120-day mean, the bias stayed in: the first three forecast days sat 11.6 W m−2 above the observed OLR, 0.13 in RMM amplitude (0.06–0.21 a cycle). So the forecast’s OLR is filtered with the mean of GEPS’s own first-day OLR (each day’s 00 UTC run, its first 24 hours) over the 120 days before the init, the way the hindcasts behind the skill scores below are filtered, and the bias cancels. Tested on the same cycles, the mismatch falls to −0.7 W m−2 and 0.07, the cycle-to-cycle scatter of GEPS’s first-day error, which no filter removes; the forecast track moves by 0.08, nearly all along RMM1 and the same at every lead. The Datamart keeps only about 30 days of runs, so this history began in September 2026. A day it does not hold contributes the observed OLR moved into GEPS’s frame: the forecast’s own frame shift, plus the mean difference between GEPS’s first-day anomaly and the observed one over the days held (at least 20, enough to fix it to 0.02–0.03 in RMM). Once all 120 days are held, the filter is GEPS’s own throughout. The winds need none of this: their observed track is GEPS’s own analysis, which agrees with ERA5’s band-mean winds to within 0.03 in RMM, so ERA5’s 120-day map serves the forecast and the observed track alike.

ENSO is removed from the RMM. The 120-day filter is meant to take out slow signals, but while an El Niño grows its 120-day mean lags the warming, and the standing convection and winds over the central Pacific project onto the index as if they were an MJO. Wheeler and Hendon removed an ENSO component before the filter; the index here, forecast and observed, removes only the 120-day mean, so every track has the part that follows the change of Niño-3.4 over the filter window removed: the RMM minus that change times a fixed vector, fitted by regressing the filtered OLR and wind anomalies on it, longitude by longitude, over 2003–2022 (ERA5 winds, NOAA interpolated OLR, ERSST Niño-3.4) and projecting the result onto the RMM patterns. It is −0.59 on RMM1 per degree of change (95 % interval −0.79 to −0.45, resampling whole years); the RMM2 part is not significantly different from zero, so none is applied. On the observed RMM (the same index computed here from ERA5) over 1980–2002, years the fit did not use, the index’s correlation with the Niño-3.4 change falls from −0.20 to +0.02 (−0.20 to +0.015 over the fit years), and the share of active days barely moves (0.63 to 0.62). The real-time Niño-3.4 is NOAA’s daily OISST, a 31-day running mean; the winds and the OLR each take the change over their own filter window, which end on different days. The forecast holds the latest Niño-3.4 throughout: in the hindcast, even the observed Niño-3.4 of each valid date scored no better. GEPS’s own forecast RMM1 follows the change by −0.53 at day 1 and −0.58 to −0.62 out to day 35, so the same vector takes out the model’s share as well. What it cannot remove is a drift that grows after the start: in the strong El Niño autumn of 2015, 21 of the 55 hindcast starts ran beyond the range that 95 % of starts keep to, their mean drifting along RMM2 to −1.2 by day 35 against the observed −0.6. The phase diagram says so in red whenever a forecast track leaves that range.

MJO skill. Every hindcast start is projected onto the same Wheeler–Hendon EOFs with the same arithmetic as the live RMM — band means, lead-matched de-drift, frame shift, 120-day mean and ENSO removed — and verified on the valid date against the observed RMM, the same index computed here from ERA5 (OLR and 850/200 hPa wind, 1979 onwards) with ENSO removed from it the same way, giving the bivariate correlation, RMSE and amplitude ratio by lead, for all starts, by season, for strong initial MJO, and for the starts within ±45 days of today. The WH04 120-day mean before each start is taken from the hindcast’s own day-1 channels of the preceding 120 days, which is how the live forecast’s OLR is filtered too (above). The one approximation is the winds: the live forecast filters them with ERA5’s map, observations for 2001–2020 are not in this archive, and the term is slowly varying. The ECMWF marks are the published extended-range reforecast values (Vitart 2017 and the ECMWF verification memoranda), not a like-for-like computation — the S2S reforecasts sit behind a research licence and are not on this site, and ECMWF’s open data stops at day 15, so no live comparison beyond that is possible either.

Reading the MJO score honestly. Over all 4,640 starts the bivariate correlation falls through 0.6 at day 16 and 0.5 at day 20; winter (DJF) starts hold on longest (0.6 at day 18) and summer (JJA) starts are the weakest (0.6 at day 15). Removing ENSO from both sides moves these by a third of a day (0.6 at day 16.4 with ENSO left in, 16.1 with it removed), within the noise of the sample. Against the published ECMWF figures of day 27–30 that is eleven to fourteen days of skill less, and the following caveats move it a few days, not two weeks. Ensemble size: the hindcast mean is four members and the operational GEPS runs 21; a larger mean removes unpredictable noise and is typically worth two to three days at the 0.6 level, while ECMWF’s published numbers come from an eleven-member mean, closer to their operational system. Model vintage: GEPS8 is the version this reforecast belongs to, not the current cycle, and ECMWF’s quoted range spans 2016 to the early 2020s. Verification target: the observed RMM from ERA5, which sits close to the hindcast’s first day (bivariate RMSE 0.13 at day 1), where ECMWF scores against its own analysis; a different observed index moves the crossings by about a day. The 120-day mean: the hindcast’s own day-1 channels, as for the live OLR; the live winds use ERA5’s map, a slowly varying difference. What the scores describe: the ensemble mean only — the plume’s spread is not scored here, and an ensemble mean is damped by construction, which is what the amplitude panel shows. Skill and probability maps. The same hindcast that calibrates the indices is scored gridpoint by gridpoint: every 2001–2020 start’s weekly-mean anomaly, de-drifted and re-based as the live maps but, like the indices, leave-one-year-out (precipitation, which the tercile maps take un-re-based, is only de-drifted), against an independent analysis on the same 2.5° grid — NCEP/NCAR R1 2 m temperature, 500 hPa height and sea-level pressure, and the CPC unified gauge analysis for precipitation, which exists over land only — pooled by calendar month. Scored in sample, the week-3 to week-5 correlations of temperature, height and pressure came out 0.02–0.04 higher, and 8–16 % of the temperature and height cells that passed the test below in those weeks (72 % of the week-3 pressure cells) do not pass out of sample; precipitation is unchanged. The anomaly correlation from that marks the skill (the maps, shown everywhere, are dotted where it is significantly above zero: a one-sided 5 % test on the 20 hindcast years, with a 10 % false-discovery rate across the map; cross-hatched where there is no truth), and the gridpoint regression of observed on forecast weekly anomaly, with its residual spread, turns the ensemble mean into calibrated tercile probabilities, below, near and above normal, drawn CPC-style as the most likely category with its probability as intensity. The terciles are those of the 2016–2025 weekly anomaly for that week of the year, not 1991–2020: against the older base the 2026 maps read “above” nearly everywhere before the forecast says anything, which is the warming since the base period, not weather. The calibration works in standardised units: the ensemble mean's departure from the recent-decade mean, divided by the hindcast ensemble mean's own spread, predicts the observed category with the hindcast correlation r as the weight, against the standard normal's terciles — the hindcast slope but not its intercept, which is the 2001–2020 mean state and printed the hindcast-era climate as “below normal” against a recent threshold. With no skill the three probabilities are exactly a third each. It is centred on the recent mean, not the median: weekly precipitation's terciles sit unevenly about its median, so a median-centred form gives “below” about 40% wherever there is no skill. Day 1 is the start date's own UTC day, in the hindcast pairing as in the live forecast. Near-normal is the tercile models forecast worst; its probability rarely clears 40% beyond week 1, and with 21 members its raw fraction is noisy. Precipitation terciles are undefined where the climatological week is dry, so those cells are masked as CPC masks them. The raw member fraction is shown beside the calibrated map: where the two disagree the hindcast is saying the ensemble is over- or under-confident there. Teleconnections

Open this figure

The views. Plumes: every member projected on the loading patterns below, the tail GEPS’s own day-0 analyses on the same projection, the published daily index as crosses, the previous run dashed; units are seasonal standard deviations of the daily index. Probabilities: the upper bar is the member fraction of the weekly-mean index at or beyond ±0.5; the thin lower bar the calibrated probability, with the hindcast skill for that index and week; hatched cells are weeks without significant skill, where the members’ opinion is shown but should not be trusted. The NAO comes in four definitions, with Greenland blocking beside it (high Greenland heights go with a negative NAO). Calibration check: the reliability diagrams and PIT histograms of the cross-validated calibration (flat = calibrated, U-shaped = over-confident, dome = under-confident). Teleconnections, member by member. The map anomalies are ensemble means; the teleconnection indices are built from all 21 members, so that phase can be stated as a probability. The indices are a reproduction of CPC’s, on the reanalysis CPC defines them on (NCEP/NCAR R1, 2.5°), and the reproduction is measured rather than assumed. The AO and AAO are the leading EOF of monthly sea-level pressure poleward of 20° in each hemisphere (Thompson & Wallace 1998 for the AO; the same construction in the south, where Thompson & Wallace 2000 used 850 hPa height), signed so the positive phase has low pressure over the pole — CPC builds its own on 1000 hPa and 700 hPa height respectively, so the AAO in particular is a close relative rather than a copy; projected day by day they track CPC’s daily AO and AAO at r 0.97 and 0.96. The 500 hPa patterns (NAO, PNA, EA, WP, EP/NP, EA/WR, SCA, TNH, POL) are defined by regression: for each pattern and each calendar month, the map of monthly height anomalies 20–90°N — standardized by calendar month and weighted by √cos φ (the area weighting Barnston & Livezey (1987) obtained with an approximately equal-area grid) — regressed on CPC’s published monthly index over the months within one of that calendar month, and the nine maps of a month combined into a least-squares projector so each index is read with the others accounted for. Month-by-month matters: CPC derives its modes separately for every calendar month and the PNA in particular changes shape with season, so a single all-year rotated PCA reproduced CPC’s PNA at only r 0.54, the seasonal regression at 0.74. Fidelity is scored out of sample — patterns fitted on 1991–2005, scored on 2006–2020 against CPC’s monthly indices for every pattern and against its daily NAO and PNA — and those are the correlations in the table; the operational projector is then refitted on all thirty years. A daily index is the daily anomaly projected the same way and divided by the seasonal standard deviation of the daily projection — the spread within ±15 days of that day of year over 1991–2020 — so “+1” means one standard deviation for the time of year. CPC standardizes its daily AO, NAO and PNA by a single annual figure instead; on that convention the indices projected from raw height or pressure (AO, AAO, NAM, EPO, WPO) read as permanently quiet in summer and every ±0.5 threshold is easy in January and hard in July. The published CPC and PSL values drawn on the plumes are rescaled to the seasonal unit, and the correlations quoted against them are computed on the annual convention, like for like. Each GEPS member’s de-drifted, re-based 500 hPa and sea-level-pressure anomaly — the same anomaly the maps show — is projected identically, so the forecast index carries no model drift; the tail is GEPS’s own day-0 analyses of the last weeks, anomalised the same way (against the model climatology at lead 0.5 and re-based), so analysis and forecast sit on one rule — against the NCEP climatology instead, the GEPS-versus-R1 height offset projected onto the monopole-like patterns as a spurious step at the seam. CPC’s own published daily values are drawn beside the tail. Two Pacific indices forecasters use that are not in CPC’s set are added on PSL’s definitions — box differences of 500 hPa height anomaly, EPO (20–35°N minus 55–65°N over 160–125°W) and WPO (25–40°N minus 50–70°N over 140°E–150°W) — and reproduce PSL’s daily series at r 0.97 and 0.99. PSL built those series on NCEP/NCAR R1, which ended in March 2026, so the crosses beside the EPO and WPO tails are the same boxes computed from ERA5 (the 12 UTC analysis against the WeatherBench 2 ERA5 1990–2019 normal), about five days behind. The NAM at 100 hPa, the stratosphere–troposphere coupling index, is the leading EOF of 100 hPa height anomalies poleward of 20°N with the hemispheric mean removed first (the +19 m/decade height trend at that level would otherwise read as a permanently weak vortex), taken from the 21 members’ 100 hPa heights; the GEPS8 hindcast carries no height above 200 hPa, so it is a plain anomaly against the NCEP climatology and gets no calibration. Raw probabilities are member fractions, so they resolve to about 5% and 0% means “no member”, not certainty.

Calibrated on the hindcast. The same projectors are applied to every GEPS8 hindcast start of 2001–2020 — four a week, 39 daily leads, de-drifted and re-based as the live product but leave-one-year-out — and scored against the observed index on the valid date from NCEP/NCAR R1. For each index and week, the starts within ±45 days of the current init day-of-year (about a thousand) give the anomaly correlation of the hindcast ensemble mean (the skill quoted in the table) and a regression of the observed weekly index on the forecast one; today’s ensemble-mean weekly index passed through that regression, with the regression’s predictive distribution (Student t, the residual spread widened for the uncertainty of the fit itself, both counted on the effective number of independent starts), is the calibrated probability. Everything here is out of sample: a live forecast is de-drifted with a climatology and re-based with an ERA5 2001–2020 normal that never saw its own year, so each hindcast start is de-drifted against the climatology without its own season and re-based with the ERA5 normal without that season’s days. The normal is the same for every year, but its day-to-day shape across a season holds a twentieth of each year’s observed weather, which a correlation pooled over the season then credits to the forecast: left in, it raised the week-3 to week-5 correlations by about 0.04 and passed about a quarter of the week-3 and week-4 cells that fail out of sample. The calibration is itself tested by cross-validation: each hindcast year is forecast with the regression refitted on the other nineteen, and the position of every observed weekly index in its predictive distribution (its PIT) should be uniform. The Calibration check view shows the reliability diagrams and PIT histograms, and a cell whose mean or spread of the PIT is significantly off (bootstrap over hindcast years, false discovery rate 10 % over every test of the run) is marked in the table. A week counts as skilful only when its correlation is at least 0.25 and significant (one-sided 5 % on the effective sample size) and also survives a false discovery rate of 10 % over every index and week of the run. Where the model has no skill the regression slope goes to zero and the calibrated probability collapses to climatology, which is the honest statement for a week-5 NAO; where it has skill but too little amplitude, the slope restores it. The hindcast mean is four members against 21 live, so the slope is slightly conservative. A weekly mean of a daily-sd index is a smoother quantity than the daily values, so a weekly mean beyond ±1 is a strong signal.

The NAO’s four definitions. CPC’s rotated-EOF NAO (the default); Hurrell’s station NAO, sea-level pressure at Lisbon minus Stykkishólmur/Reykjavík, each standardised; Hurrell’s principal-component NAO, the leading pattern of Atlantic-sector sea-level pressure (20–80°N, 90°W–40°E), month by month; and Jones’s station NAO, Gibraltar minus south-west Iceland. Beside them the Greenland Blocking Index: mean 500 hPa height over 60–80°N, 80–20°W (Hanna et al.); high values are blocked and go with a negative NAO. Every member is projected the same way for each.

Impacts and The regimes: the composites and the patterns, below.

Weather regimes. The Euro-Atlantic and North American sectors each get four regimes from k-means on 5-day-mean 500 hPa anomalies (NCEP/NCAR R1, 1991–2020, the leading 14 EOFs, 30 restarts), separately for the cold half-year (October–March) and the warm (April–September), because summer regimes are not winter regimes with less amplitude. The cold Euro-Atlantic set comes out as the classic four — NAO+, Scandinavian blocking, Atlantic ridge, NAO−/Greenland blocking — and the North American set as Pacific trough, Alaskan ridge, Pacific ridge and Greenland high, the Lee et al. (2023) quartet (Lee, Tippett & Polvani 2023, J. Climate 36, 7091–7108); the warm-season sets are named by inspection and say so. Each of the 21 members’ de-drifted 500 hPa anomaly is smoothed the same way and assigned to the nearest centroid, or to “no regime” when its pattern correlation with every centroid is below 0.25, as in the ECMWF product; the member fraction is the regime probability. Every valid day is classified with the set of its own season (October–March cold, April–September warm, the halves the sets were built on) — the forecast, the observed tail and the hindcast alike. A soft blend across the boundary was considered and rejected: every training day belonged to exactly one set, so a blend has no counterpart in the regimes’ own definition. What carries across the switch is colour: a cold and a warm regime whose patterns correlate at 0.6 or more are drawn as one “family” (all four Euro-Atlantic pairs, r 0.66–0.89; Pacific trough, Pacific ridge and Greenland high in North America; the cold Alaskan ridge and the warm Hudson Bay ridge have no counterpart and keep colours of their own).

Regime skill. The hit rate beside the forecast is the share of hindcast starts within ±45 days of the date whose ensemble-mean regime matched the observed (NCEP/NCAR R1) one on the valid day, with a 95% interval from a bootstrap over hindcast years (starts in one season share weather, so they are not independent), against two baselines: persistence of the pattern observed over the three days before day 1, and always forecasting the most common regime. A week of the weekly grid is marked skilful only when the bootstrap interval of [hit rate − the better baseline] lies above zero; otherwise it is hatched. The hindcast is scored out of sample like the indices: each start’s 500 hPa anomaly is de-drifted against the climatology, and re-based with the ERA5 normal, without its own season. In sample the hit rates beyond day 10 were 0.01–0.02 higher, enough to pass week 3 at four of the twelve mid-month dates in the Euro-Atlantic sector, where none passes out of sample. The forecast figure gives the number of hindcast starts in the window, and the weekly grid marks the weeks that pass for the current date.

Multi-model view. GEPS (21 members, days 1–35), GEFS (31, days 1–35) and, for days 1–15, AIFS-ENS (26 of its 51 members) and IFS-ENS (50), from 500 hPa fields the site already fetches. GEPS and GEFS are de-drifted with their hindcasts; AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS have no open reforecast, so they are taken against ERA5 1991–2020 and their lead-dependent drift is measured on their own verified runs as they accrue. The combined probability is the equal-weight mean over the models covering each date: skill weighting would need hindcasts of all four, and the two that have them have overlapping 95% intervals at 98% of month × lead × sector combinations.

What each regime brings. ERA5 1991–2020 composites of 2 m temperature and precipitation over the days R1 was in each regime, with each season’s linear trend removed, tested on regime episodes (one mean per run of consecutive days) with a t-test and the false discovery rate held at 10% over the map (Benjamini–Hochberg; Wilks 2016). Only significant areas are coloured.

500 hPa scenarios

Open this figure

Ensemble scenarios. Distinct from the regimes: no fixed centroids. Each week the 21 members’ weekly-mean 500 hPa anomalies over a sector are clustered by k-means with k = 2, 3 and 4, and the number of scenarios is chosen, not fixed: each k is tested against a null in which the members’ deviations are rotated by random orthogonal matrices in member space — the spatial covariance is kept exactly, any grouping is destroyed — and re-clustered a hundred times with the same k-means effort. The k that beats the 95th percentile of its null by the widest margin is drawn as scenario means with member counts; if none does, the ensemble is one scenario with spread and the two-way split is shown faded. This follows the idea of Ferranti and Corti (2011, ECMWF Newsletter 127, 6–11), whose ECMWF scheme tests k = 2–6 against synthetic Gaussian ensembles; the rotation null and the decision rule here are this site’s own. It was calibrated on this ensemble: Gaussian ensembles pass 5–7% of the time, a two-scenario ensemble with groups three standard deviations apart is caught 60% of the time at k = 2 and 95% at four apart, but a forced k = 3 caught those only 25% and 50% of the time, which is why k is chosen rather than fixed at three. Groups closer than about two standard deviations cannot be told from a continuum with 21 members, and the usual verdict is one or two scenarios. Each scenario mean is labelled with its nearest weather regime and the pattern correlation.

The stratosphere

Open this figure

The stratosphere is de-drifted with a second reforecast. Everything else on this page is de-drifted against the GEPS8 hindcast, and that archive cannot serve the vortex: it carries ua/va at 100, 200 and 850 hPa only, zg at 200 and 500 only, and no pressure-level temperature at all — nothing at 10 hPa. So the stratospheric anomalies are taken against ERA5 (the 1991–2020 day-of-year climatology, moved along its significant trend to the valid date), and the model’s drift is removed with a different set of runs: the GEPS reforecasts in the ECMWF S2S archive (ECCC, control + 3 perturbed members, every year 2001–2020, started Mondays and Thursdays, with 10 and 100 hPa fields). For each start date the drift is the reforecasts’ mean minus ERA5 on the same valid dates, by latitude and lead (a zonal mean, 80 runs per lead); start dates within 8 days of the forecast’s day of year are pooled. The correction is not small. At day 1 the reforecasts and ERA5 agree to 0.1 K over the 10 hPa polar cap, and then the model warms the cap by 1.6 K at day 10, 3 K at day 15 and 7 K at day 35 (late-September starts), with u(60°N) at 10 hPa falling 4–6 m/s behind: left in, a raw 10 hPa map would show a warming in weeks 3–5 that is only the model’s drift. The same reforecasts correct u(60°N) and the cap temperature in the vortex diagnostics. Height anomalies additionally have the hemispheric mean removed (the QBO and the warming troposphere move the whole surface, and that is not the vortex). A hemisphere with no fetched start date within 8 days falls back to the uncorrected view, with 10 hPa drawn as absolute fields. The S2S data are licensed for non-commercial research.

ERA5 was also checked against MERRA-2, the reference the site’s existing vortex monitor uses, so the two are not silently disagreeing: over 2,156 matching days they differ by 0.13 m/s in u(60°N) at 10 hPa (r 0.999), 0.11 K in cap temperature (r 0.997) and 9 m in cap height from 2001 onward. An archive-wide sweep of both records found a single corrupt day in 46 years of MERRA-2 — 1991-06-30, where the polar-cap height collapses to 0.80 m — which is screened out; removing it also moved MERRA-2’s own height trend from +18.7 to +18.1 m/decade, against +18.9 measured independently from ERA5.
