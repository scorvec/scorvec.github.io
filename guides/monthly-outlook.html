Methods: the long-range forecast

What each figure shows, how it is made, how it is tested and what it is built from. Open the long-range forecast

The figures
The forecast mapsNiño-3.4

The page

A forecast designed and run by Claude (Anthropic’s AI model), issued unattended on the 15th of every month: the coming month and the three overlapping seasons from it (2 m temperature, precipitation and 500 hPa height over the Northern Hemisphere, as expected anomalies against 1991–2020 and as tercile probabilities), and Niño-3.4 for the next twelve months. Every map is dotted where its own cross-validated hindcast shows significant skill, and every forecast is scored against ERA5 once its target is over.

ERA5 (Copernicus Climate Change Service, ECMWF) via the WeatherBench 2 and ARCO-ERA5 copies on Google Cloud; C3S seasonal forecasts and hindcasts (Copernicus Climate Change Service); GEFS and its reforecast (NOAA); ERSSTv6 (NOAA NCEI via PSL); TAO/TRITON (NOAA PMEL); ORAS5 (ECMWF, via the Copernicus Climate Data Store). Method and code: scripts/monthly/.

The statistical forecast.

Built from ERA5 alone (1959 onward, on 3° cells, as half-month means): the predictors are the ~30 days that end on the 7th of the issue month, which ERA5T always holds by the 15th. Six components, each a map of the target’s anomaly:

  • Trend: at each point, the linear trend of the target’s anomaly over the 30 years before, past years only, after removing the part relative Niño-3.4 explains (a run of La Niña winters at the end is not extrapolated as climate change).
  • RONI, QBO and vortex regression: the detrended anomaly regressed on relative Niño-3.4 (El Niño and La Niña apart: their teleconnections are not mirror images), the equatorial 50 hPa wind and the 57–63N 50 hPa wind of the predictor window. An index beyond every value of the training years is held at the largest one there (from the December 2026 outlook), so a record El Niño is forecast as the strongest on record.
  • Forecast ENSO teleconnection (new in this version): the same regression on relative Niño-3.4 over the target’s own months, trained on the observed values and applied to Claude’s Niño-3.4 forecast (in the hindcast, that model’s cross-validated forecasts). It matters when ENSO changes between the issue and the target, as in the spring after an El Niño; through winter, when ENSO persists, the window’s state already carries it. The forecast value is held within the observed range in the same way.
  • Tropical constructed analogue (Van den Dool 1994): the window’s SST and rainfall anomalies over 20S–20N written as a ridge-regularised combination of every past year’s, and the same weights applied to those years’ target anomalies. Rainfall is the heating that drives the teleconnections; extratropical SST is left out, being mostly the footprint of the circulation.
  • Local persistence of each field, for temperature also of sea ice, snow depth and topsoil moisture.
  • Analogue years: the eight past years closest in ENSO, QBO, vortex, tropical SST and hemispheric 500 hPa patterns.

At each point a non-negative regression of the observed anomaly on the components’ cross-validated hindcasts sets their weights, smoothed over about 2500 km. The regressions train on the target pooled with the targets a month either side (about 200 cases); every hindcast year leaves out itself and its neighbours from every fit. A season is forecast directly as its own three-month mean, not as three months averaged.

The blend with C3S, GEFS and GEPS.

C3S (every target): the multi-system mean of the seven C3S seasonal systems (ECMWF, UK Met Office, Météo-France, DWD, NCEP, and ECCC’s two CanSIPS components counted as one; each centre weighing the same) from the issue published two days before ours, against each system’s own 1993–2016 hindcast and moved to 1991–2020 with ERA5. At each point the issued forecast is a non-negative regression on the statistical forecast and the C3S mean, fitted on the 24 hindcast years 1993–2016 and smoothed like the consolidation. Tested on the September and October issues, the C3S mean beat the statistical forecast at every lead for all three variables (December–February temperature ACC 0.39 against 0.19; precipitation 0.32 against 0.27; 500 hPa height from SEAS5 0.27 against 0.18), and the blend matched or beat C3S alone.

GEFS and GEPS (next month): the 35-day GEFS run of the 15th and GEPS’s newest run (Mondays and Thursdays), which reach the 15th–19th of the month, averaged: their mean over the month’s days they reach and (temperature) over their days before the month, blended on top with weights from their reforecasts (2001–2019 in common). Beside the statistical forecast, GEPS alone beat GEFS alone for all three variables and the two averaged did best: twelve-month mean hindcast ACC 0.18 for 500 hPa height, 0.34 for temperature and 0.18 for precipitation, against GEFS’s 0.15, 0.30 and 0.15. Either model alone stands in when the other has no run.

A stage is issued for a variable only where it beats the forecast before it in its cross-validated hindcast. The Ingredients view shows each input with its weight and its own hindcast score; the headline skill is that of what was issued, on that stage’s years.

Probabilities, skill and updates.

Each point’s terciles are its 1991–2020 33rd and 67th percentiles. The forecast distribution is that point’s own climatology, shifted by the forecast and narrowed by the hindcast’s error relative to the climatological spread, so a skill-less forecast of zero is exactly a third each and precipitation keeps its skew. The maps are dotted where the hindcast has significant skill (one-sided 5 %, false-discovery rate 10 % across the hemisphere). Elsewhere the colours are still the forecast’s lean, but the hindcast cannot tell it from chance, so the issued probabilities there, and the ones scored, are equal chances.

When the stratosphere changes course after the 15th, the month can be re-issued as an update: its first week from the IFS-ENS and AIFS-ENS, the rest from the statistical forecast with their predicted vortex. The issue it updates stays on the record; the update is scored beside it.

Tested and left out.

The MJO (even a perfect 15-day MJO forecast added about +0.01 to the 500 hPa correlation in winter), the 10 hPa vortex in place of 50 hPa (±0.007), a warming-index trend, 90-day windows, extratropical SST in the analogue, an anchored C3S blend (the statistical weight pulled toward 1: worse than the free blend), a linear inverse model for Niño-3.4 (a lead-6 correlation of 0.74 against the regressions’ 0.81), the whole ORAS5 heat-content field in place of the moorings (no better), OISST in place of ERSSTv6 for the last month’s SST (equal to six months, 0.52 against 0.55 at twelve), the OISST window past six months (6 % worse at eleven), GEFS’s MJO forecast alone for Niño-3.4 (5 %, not significant, against GEPS’s 11–14 %) and the MJO forecast in the maps (with GEFS blended in, only December gained; November and January to March moved by ±0.02).

Niño-3.4

Open this figure

Claude’s statistical model. The state at the end of the last complete month is the leading eight patterns of the SST anomaly over 25S–25N (ERSSTv6), the leading three of the 0–300 m temperature anomaly at the eight equatorial TAO/TRITON moorings (165E to 95W), and Niño-3.4 and relative Niño-3.4 themselves. For each lead (1–12 months) and start month, Niño-3.4 and the relative index are regressed on that state (ridge-damped, starts two months either side pooled), trained from 1980. To six months the state also holds the OISST anomaly over the 30 days to the issue (its leading eight patterns, Niño-3.4 and relative Niño-3.4; trained from 1982): two weeks fresher than ERSSTv6, it cut the error variance by 28 % at one month, 11 % at two and 6–8 % at three to five (beyond six it did worse, so the longer leads keep the month state). The heat content trains on ECMWF ORAS5 at the moorings and starts from the moorings themselves (ORAS5 lags real time by about six weeks), less each mooring’s mean TAO-minus-ORAS5 difference. Two more predictors carry the MJO: its mean state over the 35 days from the issue, trained on the observed index and taken from the 35-day GEPS and GEFS runs, half each, GEFS’s halved first because its 35-day MJO runs a third too strong (over 2001–2019 that cut the error variance by 11 % at two months and 12 % at three, as much as GEPS alone but more reliably; a perfect MJO forecast would give 15–16 %). Adding the heat content lifted the 6-month correlation from 0.69 to 0.81 and the 9-month from 0.57 to 0.69, and the eight moorings did as well as the whole ORAS5 field.

Skill. Cross-validated from 1982 (each year and its neighbours left out of everything fitted), the correlation is about 0.98 at one month, 0.81 at six and 0.54 at twelve (by lead, under the chart). Over 1993–2016 it matched the C3S multi-system mean at every lead from 1 to 6 months (RMSE 0.48 against 0.49 °C at six), and it followed the decay of 1982–83, 1997–98 and 2015–16 with those events left out.

Claude’s forecast averages the statistical model and the C3S mean of the five systems the ENSO forecasts page keeps, half each, where the models reach; beyond their last month it is the statistical model plus half their last difference, decaying 20 % a month (over 1993–2016 this beat the statistical model alone at leads 7–9 and was no worse after). Its ranges are the hindcast’s errors by start month and lead. RONI is CPC’s official index since February 2026: relative Niño-3.4 rescaled to Niño-3.4’s variance; the season odds use ±0.5 °C on the three-month mean.
