The page

How the atmosphere moves air, heat and momentum around the globe, from today’s analysis to two weeks ahead: the mid-latitude jets and Rossby waves, the tropical overturning (Walker and Hadley cells, velocity potential, rain-forced circulation), and angular momentum with the torques that change it. The forecasts are ECMWF’s two ensembles (AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS), the AIFS single run, the IFS control (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026) and Canada’s GDPS, most of them refreshed with each 00 and 12 UTC cycle (the rain forecast also uses NOAA’s GEFS and Canada’s GEPS ensembles); most anomalies are against ERA5 1991–2020. The ocean side of El Niño is on the El Niño monitor, the polar vortex on the stratosphere page.

Mid-latitude weather

Dynamic tropopause

Open this figure

The explainer: what the dynamic tropopause and potential vorticity measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

The upper-level flow as PV thinkers read it. Ertel potential vorticity is computed from the temperature and wind of the IFS control (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026) on nine levels from 700 to 100 hPa — a single run, because PV does not survive ensemble averaging: a mean of fifty folds is a smear. The dynamic tropopause is the 2-PVU surface, found by searching upward from 700 hPa; its potential temperature is shaded (low θ, blue, is stratospheric or polar air reaching down; high θ, red, is a tropical tropopause), its pressure contoured, and the wind on it drawn. Troughs, cut-off lows and ridges are the θ pattern itself; a tight gradient is the jet; a tongue of low θ wrapping up is a Rossby wave breaking. The other two loops give PV on the 330 and 350 K isentropes with the 2-PVU line — 330 K sits near the winter jet, 350 K near the summer and subtropical one. 6-hourly to day 10, on a hemispheric view and six regional ones (North Pacific, North America, North Atlantic & Europe, East Asia, South America and the tropical Atlantic; in the Southern Hemisphere the dynamic tropopause is the −2-PVU surface and the isentropic maps show −PV, and the tropical Atlantic view, which spans the equator, draws each hemisphere in its own sign). θ runs purple and blue (cold, low tropopause) through green and yellow to red (warm, high tropopause) in 5 K steps; PV is yellow to orange below 2 PVU (tropospheric air) and blue to purple above it (stratospheric air), with the 2-PVU line in black. Where PV is still below 2 PVU at 100 hPa (the deep tropics) the 2-PVU surface lies above the model levels, and the maps show the 100 hPa values there.

North Pacific jet: Jet maps

Open this figure

What this shows. The jet stream over the North Pacific at 250 hPa (about 10 km up), from 100°E to 60°W so the downstream half over North America is in view, for the next two weeks. Jet maps: the week-1 and week-2 ensemble-mean wind speed with isotachs, the jet axis (the latitude of the fastest wind at each longitude, where it reaches 30 m/s) against the ERA5 1991–2020 normal axis, and the share of member-days with a jet core of 50 m/s or more against the line where such winds occur on 20% of days normally; the loop steps the same two panels day by day. Jet axis: every member’s axis on days 3, 7, 10 and 14 against the normal axis and its usual (10–90%) range — where the lines fan out, the forecast does not know where the jet, and the storm track beneath it, will be. Members: AIFS-ENS control + 50 perturbed members, IFS-ENS all 50 perturbed members (open data has no IFS control on pressure levels), all on the same 0.5° grid.

Jet phases (Winters et al. 2019). Day to day the Pacific jet varies mostly in two ways: it extends east across the basin or retracts toward Asia (the first pattern of the cold-season 250 hPa wind, 13.6% of the variance), and it shifts poleward or equatorward (the second, 10.2%). A day is a point in the plane of these two indices; outside the unit circle its phase is the nearest axis. The patterns are defined on November–March ERA5, and the scale is seasonal: each month’s indices are standardised over that month ± one month, so 1σ in October is an October-sized departure. The diagram is shown only for forecasts centred in October–May, the months where the two cold-season patterns still capture ≥ 80% of what the month’s own two leading patterns capture (October 82%, September only 70%); in summer the figure says so instead of printing misleading σ values. In season, a day is extended 16.4% of the time, poleward 15.7%, retracted 15.1%, equatorward 16.1% and neutral 36.6%.

What each phase brings (ERA5 1991–2020, October–May, 282–318 episodes per phase; colour only where significant: two-sided t-test on episode means, false discovery rate 10% over the map, Wilks 2016). On the same days: an extended jet sits under a deep Aleutian/Gulf of Alaska trough (up to −145 m at 500 hPa) with a mild Alaska and western Canada; a poleward-shifted jet brings a trough across the Gulf of Alaska and warmth over central and eastern Canada and the northern United States; a retracted jet puts a ridge south of the Aleutians (+140 m); an equatorward-shifted jet goes with a ridge over Alaska and cold air over western and central Canada and the northern Plains (to −3 °C), a dry British Columbia coast and a wetter subtropical Pacific. Three to seven days later the height signal is weaker (±45–60 m) but still significant over 54–72% of the map, and the temperature signal over North America holds (53–63% of the map).

What the forecast implies. The forecast jet indices turned into weekly maps over North America. In ERA5 1991–2020, November–March, the 7-day mean anomaly of 2 m temperature, precipitation and 500 hPa height is regressed on the two jet indices averaged over five days: for the week centred on those days (forecast days 9–15 when the indices are those of days 10–14) and for the week after (days 16–22, past the end of the forecast). Each season is predicted from a fit on the other seasons, and a point is drawn only where that out-of-sample correlation is significant (whole seasons resampled, false discovery rate 10% over the map) and at least 0.15. Median correlation over the map, and the share of it drawn: height 0.26 and 72% in the first week, 0.16 and 53% in the second; temperature 0.21 and 62%, 0.15 and 50%; precipitation 0.09 and 38% in the first week, and its second week (0.03, 13%) is not shown. The slopes were fitted on analysed indices; the forecast index is an ensemble mean, smaller than what verifies, so the implied anomalies err towards zero.

Drift. Neither model has an open reforecast, but the Google Cloud mirror keeps the real-time runs, so the drift was measured on the models’ own archived forecasts: ten members of every other 00Z run from July 2025 to September 2026 (220 runs each), against the ECMWF operational analysis. The sector-mean 250 hPa wind drifts by less than 0.2 m/s at every lead in both models. AIFS-ENS phase-index biases are small: in season the 90% intervals include zero at every lead except the shift index on days 1–3 and extension on day 2, all under 0.02σ (day 15 extension +0.08σ, −0.04 to +0.25); over the whole year extension runs +0.10 to +0.14σ on days 10, 12, 14 and 15 with intervals excluding zero (15 leads and two indices, each tested on its own). A cross-validated correction did not reduce the in-season error, so none is applied. IFS-ENS drifts poleward late in the run (shift index +0.24σ [+0.01, +0.44] at day 15; extension +0.15σ [+0.02, +0.32]); its smoothed, noise-shrunk drift field is subtracted, which lowered the cross-validated error of the indices by 1.8% in season and 5.8% over the year. The figures say which applies. (The ensemble mean still weakens with lead as members spread: that is uncertainty, not drift, which is why the maps show probabilities and the axes show every member.)

Skill of the ensemble-mean phase indices against the analysis, 122 runs in season (October–May starts, 90% block-bootstrap intervals). AIFS-ENS: extension r = 0.89 [0.84, 0.93] at day 7, 0.73 [0.61, 0.83] at day 10, 0.49 at day 14; shift 0.94 [0.92, 0.96], 0.84 [0.76, 0.89], 0.56. IFS-ENS: extension 0.86 [0.79, 0.91], 0.64 [0.50, 0.76], 0.47; shift 0.93, 0.81 [0.73, 0.87], 0.53. Persisting today’s analysis scores 0.55–0.63 at day 3 and 0.23–0.30 at day 7; for both models and both indices the lower end of the forecast correlation’s 90% interval stays above persistence’s correlation at every lead to day 15 (a comparison with persistence’s value, not a paired test). One cold season is in the sample; the numbers will firm up as the archive grows.

Torque (research). A burst of mountain torque over the Himalaya and Tibet changes the atmosphere’s angular momentum and has been linked to the jet downstream. The research view regresses each jet index on the Himalaya/Tibet torque at lags from −10 to +20 days, torque days in the forecast month ± one month, with 90% moving-block bootstrap intervals (30-day blocks, which respect both series’ memory; about 600 to 850 effective days of 2,700) and the false discovery rate held at 10% over lags and indices. The torque is the one the torque budget forecasts (its “By range” view): the day-mean mountain torque over 70–105°E 25–45°N on 5° boxes, computed the same way from ERA5 1991–2020, and the forecast panel standardises the IFS-ENS forecast of it by that season window’s ERA5 mean and σ, so forecast and regression are one quantity on one scale. Within each season window (lags and indices under one false discovery rate) the link is significant in every window, though neighbouring windows share two of their three months and are not independent tests; it is small: extension follows the torque at +6 to +7 days with a slope of +0.20 to +0.26σ per σ (e.g. September–November torque days: +0.20 [+0.15, +0.25] at +7 days, 4% of the variance), and the poleward shift at +3 to +5 days (+0.12 to +0.27). The jet also leads the torque: extension is followed by a negative torque anomaly (about −0.1, significant in seven of the eight windows). The forecast panel draws what the torque alone would imply only at lags where the regression is significant, with the largest significant slope, which picking the largest of several estimates overstates somewhat; it is a nudge of a few tenths of a σ, never the forecast. Until October 2026 the regression used a 1.5° WeatherBench 2 version of the torque (correlation 0.93–0.94 with this one in every season window) and the forecast was scaled by the σ of single 00 and 12 UTC values over the whole year; the slopes barely moved, the standardised forecast by up to 12%.

North Pacific jet: Extension watch

Open this figure

What this shows. Whether the jet is about to extend across the Pacific, what over the tropics and Asia is pushing for or against it, and what came before past extensions. It runs for forecasts started from November to March. An onset is the first of at least three days in a row in the extension phase (the extension index leading, at 1σ or more: see Jet phases above) after seven days without one. ERA5 1991–2020 has 58 onsets in those months (25 in El Niño winters, 19 in neutral ones, 14 in La Niña winters), and 18% of the days outside the phase are followed by one within 13 days: the base rate the ensemble’s count is set against.

Tracker. Left: every member’s extension index with the ensemble mean and its 10–90% range after the last 20 days of analyses, and the course of ERA5’s 58 onsets laid over the members’ median onset day (median and middle half); below, the share of members in the extension phase day by day and the share whose extension has begun. A member’s onset must begin by day 13, the last day on which a three-day run can still be seen. Right: how many days the members’ episodes last and how high the index peaks, against ERA5’s (half of ERA5’s episodes last 4–6 days and peak at 1.5–2.0σ; medians 5 days and 1.75σ). An episode still going on the last forecast day is counted at its length so far, so the members’ lengths are a lower bound. The shaded days, from day 12, are those where the forecast index’s correlation with the analysis is below 0.6 (see Skill above). When most members are already in the extension phase on day 0 the episode is under way, and the figure follows each member to the day it leaves the phase.

What favours it. One row per ingredient: its value now and, by days ahead, how the chance of an onset changed in ERA5 when the ingredient was in that state. The number is the chance that an onset begins in the lead window (days 1–5, 4–10, 6–12, 8–14) after a day in the state, over the chance after any day (6.5% for days 1–5, 9% for the others). It is tested by shuffling whole seasons of the state against the onsets (2000 draws, two-sided, false discovery rate 20% over every state and lead, so up to one change in five shown may be chance); a dash means no significant change. A state begins at ±1σ (for ENSO, an ONI of ±0.5), and an ingredient between the thresholds, or one whose input is missing or stale, has no row.

The ingredients: the MJO phase on the cleaned wind-only RMM of the MJO page (no MJO below amplitude 1; phases 4–5, convection over the Maritime Continent, make an onset 1.6 times as likely 4–14 days later, phases 2–3 a quarter to 0.4 times as likely in the next 10 days, phases 6–1 a third to half as likely a week or two later); the Siberian High, sea-level pressure over 45–60°N 60–100°E (strong: 1.5–1.6 times, 6–14 days later); the East Asian trough, 500 hPa height over 35–50°N 115–145°E less the mean north of 20°N (deep: 1.4–1.6 times in the next 10 days; shallow: 0.55 times in the next 5); the jet itself (shifted equatorward: 1.4–1.6 times at 4–14 days; retracted: almost never an onset within 5 days); and the Himalayan mountain torque (high: 1.6 times in the next 5 days; low: 0.45 times). The Siberian High, the trough and the torque are often three views of one chain: a cold surge off Asia raises the pressure against the mountains, the positive torque and the deepened trough speed the jet off Japan, and the streak then runs east. The two box indices come from each ensemble’s newest run, on the same 2.5° grid as the reference. A Forecast line under a value gives the ensemble mean’s largest departure of the next seven days, when that is a different state and one that changed the odds.

ENSO is the background, not a trigger. The jet spends 23% of an El Niño winter’s days in the extension phase, 15% of a neutral winter’s and 11% of a La Niña winter’s (the season’s share against its ONI: r = 0.55, p = 0.002, 29 winters), which is what its row says. Day by day, onsets were 1.3 times as likely in El Niño (days 4–14) and 0.7 times in La Niña, which the row adds as one number: a smaller change than in the days themselves, since El Niño lengthens and repeats the extensions more than it times them. The ingredients are not independent, so nothing is multiplied together; the line above the table only lists which favour an onset and which do not. The forecast is the ensemble’s own share of members with an onset, against the 18% base rate.

Before an onset (ERA5). Means over the 58 onsets for four windows, 12–10, 9–7, 6–4 and 3–1 days before: south of 20°N the 200 hPa velocity potential (green: more rising air and rain), north of it sea-level pressure in colour and 500 hPa height in lines. Each field is the anomaly from its day-of-year normal less its own mean over the 120 days before, so the season’s standing pattern (El Niño, the trend) is out and what is left is what changed. Colour and lines only where the mean differs from zero (one-sample t-test across onsets, false discovery rate 20% over each map). The sequence: more convection over the Maritime Continent and the western Pacific from 12 to 4 days before; higher pressure building over eastern Asia with a deepening trough near Japan in the last six days; then, in the last three, the North Pacific low deepening under the jet as it extends. El Niño’s onsets differ from the others over at most 3% of any map.

North Pacific jet: Jet branches

Open this figure

What this shows. The two jet streams of the cold season told apart, and where they stack: a polar jet, a subtropical jet, and a superposition, where both sit in one column over a single steep tropopause wall from the polar to the tropical tropopause, with unusually strong winds and the vertical circulations of high-impact winter storms (Winters & Martin 2014). The forecast is IFS-ENS’s 50 members at 12-hour steps to day 10, each member classified on its own: potential vorticity does not survive averaging, so the ensemble mean is never classified.

How a jet is found (Winters & Martin 2014; Christenson et al. 2017; Winters et al. 2020). On a 0.5° grid, a column holds a polar (subtropical) jet when the pressure-weighted 400–100 hPa wind exceeds 30 m/s and the horizontal gradient of the 315–330 K (340–355 K) layer’s PV, where that PV lies between 1 and 3 PVU, reaches 1.4 (0.9) × 10−5 PVU/m. On ERA5 1991–2020 these thresholds give the published North American event threshold (18 columns) and close to the published event rate; on the 2.5° grid of the original climatology the method puts the maxima where it does, east of Japan, across southern North America and over North Africa.

Maps. A 0.5° column mask is a ribbon one to three columns wide, so the maps show the neighbourhood: the chance of the branch within 300 km (the members’ share, recalibrated as below), against how often ERA5 had it within 300 km on the same date (1991–2020, a 31-day window centred on the date).

Cross-section. The run cut along a line chosen on the map of its 250 hPa wind: a tap puts the section along that longitude from 10 to 80°N, and Draw a line takes any two points. From 700 to 100 hPa it shows the members’ mean wind speed in colours and their mean potential temperature as lines every 10 K, with the isentropes that bound the polar jet’s layer (315 and 330 K) and the subtropical jet’s (340 and 355 K) in the jets’ colours, and the 2 PVU surface of the members’ median PV, the tropopause, as the thick black line. A jet of either kind sits where the tropopause steps down steeply through its layer; a superposition is one tall wall through both. These averaged fields, on columns 1.5° of longitude wide, are there to read the structure: the chances in the strip beneath come from each member classified on its own, as on the maps, against the ERA5 normal (dotted), and a branch is left out at a lead where the maps do not show it. Touching the section reads the values at that point.

Events. In the western North Pacific box (30–40°N, 135–175°E; Handlos & Martin 2016) and over North America (10–80°N, 140–50°W; Winters et al. 2020), a superposition event is a group of superposed columns, each within 1000 km of another, at least as large as the region’s top-10% count in ERA5 (25 and 18 columns). The chart gives the share of members with one at each lead against ERA5’s rate on the date.

Verification (151 archived IFS-ENS runs, every 00 UTC run from November 2025 to March 2026, ten members, against ERA5 identified the same way; Brier skill against the ERA5 normal for the date, cos(latitude)-weighted over 10–80°N, 90% intervals from a bootstrap over the 22 weeks of start dates, significance held to a 10% false discovery rate over all products and leads). The raw shares run too high at short leads: at day 0 the members find about 30% more polar and subtropical jet and 46% more superposition than ERA5, because the forecast starts sharper than ERA5 and its PV walls cross the thresholds more often; from day 2 the excess is under 15%. The shares are therefore recalibrated by the observed frequency at each member count, fitted with each month left out in turn and kept only where it beat the raw shares significantly (at every lead). Skill of the maps at days 0, 4 and 8: polar jet 0.71, 0.43 and 0.12; subtropical jet 0.72, 0.51 and 0.25; superposition 0.49, 0.16 and 0.02 (0.07 at day 6). Event odds: western North Pacific 0.42 for days 0–2, 0.26 for days 3–5 and 0.05 for days 6–10; North America 0.58 and 0.31, with no significant skill after day 5. Only the leads whose skill is significant and at least 0.05 are drawn: the polar jet maps to day 9, the subtropical jet maps to day 10, the superposition maps to day 6, the western North Pacific odds to day 10 and the North American odds to day 5. One season of runs is a short record.

Season. The layers are the cold season’s; in the warm season the two jets become one on other isentropes, so runs are shown from 1 November to 31 March.

Wave activity flux

Open this figure

The explainer: what the Takaya–Nakamura wave activity flux measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

The Takaya–Nakamura (2001) wave activity flux is a phase-independent vector diagnostic of quasi-stationary Rossby wave propagation: unlike watching ridges and troughs move, the flux W points along the wave packet's group velocity — where the wave energy itself is travelling, ducted along the jet-stream waveguides — and its convergence marks where the downstream flow will amplify days later. That makes it a practical forecast tool for downstream development, blocking onset, and teleconnection excitation: watch packets radiate out of the tropical Pacific convection (the El Niño–PNA pathway) or off a mountain-torque event, arc along the subtropical jet, and pile into an amplifying ridge.

$$\mathbf{W}=\frac{p\cos\phi}{2\,|\mathbf{U}|}\begin{pmatrix}U(\psi_x'^2-\psi'\psi_{xx}')+V(\psi_x'\psi_y'-\psi'\psi_{xy}')\\[2pt]U(\psi_x'\psi_y'-\psi'\psi_{xy}')+V(\psi_y'^2-\psi'\psi_{yy}')\end{pmatrix}$$

Computed at 250 hPa — the level of the mid-latitude jet core and the stationary-wave maximum — for each of 25 of the 50 AIFS-ENS perturbed members (a subset, to keep the download small) at every daily lead to day 15, then averaged: the flux is quadratic in $\psi'$, so the flux of the ensemble mean fades with lead as members decorrelate, whereas the mean of the members' fluxes keeps the packets that are actually forecast. Each member's streamfunction anomaly $\psi'$ (spherical-harmonic inversion of the vorticity, against the ERA5 1991–2020 day-of-year climatology) is smoothed with a 5-day running mean along the lead before the flux is formed, because the theory is for quasi-stationary waves and fast synoptic packets enter the phase-independent form with error; the basic state $(U,V)$ is the ERA5 day-of-year climatology plus the 30-day mean anomaly of the AIFS 0-h analyses, and $\psi'$ is taken against that same low-passed flow — so in a year with a displaced jet the packets are steered by the waveguide that is actually there, and perturbation and basic state are consistent. Shading is the flux convergence $-\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{W}$ — red marks where wave activity is piling up and the downstream flow amplifies over the following days (blue marks emission regions) — hatched where fewer than 60% of members agree on its sign; grey contours are $\psi'$ (the anomalous ridges/troughs, dashed negative), arrows the member-mean wave activity flux, green contours the ensemble-mean 250 hPa jet at that lead (25/35/45 m/s) — the actual waveguide the packets follow. Fluxes are masked equatorward of 20° and outside westerlies, where the quasi-stationary linear theory does not apply; the diagnostic is most meaningful for 3–10-day downstream development, not fast transients. Both hemispheres are shown: in austral winter the Southern Hemisphere waveguide is the active one.

Filtering. The convergence shading is truncated at planetary scale (spherical-harmonic degree 15), a filter over the whole globe, so the way the masked cells are handled reaches into the map. The flux is set to zero under the mask, which leaves a step at its edge; the divergence in the masked cells is set to zero before filtering, so the half of that step inside the mask stays out of the map. Tested against a known answer (the live 250 hPa flux, masked as on this map, at 11 forecast days), the rms error of the shading is 16% of the signal and the spurious convergence and divergence a divergence-free flux produces at the mask edge 6%; a smooth taper of the flux into the mask did worse on both counts. Teleconnections, days 1–15

Open this figure

What this shows. Twelve patterns of the Northern Hemisphere flow, from every member of the newest IFS-ENS (50 members), AIFS-ENS (51), GEFS (31) and GEPS (21) runs to day 15: the Arctic Oscillation (AO) from sea-level pressure and, at 500 hPa, the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), the Pacific–North American pattern (PNA), the Eastern and Western Pacific Oscillations (EPO, WPO) and CPC’s East Atlantic (EA), West Pacific (WP), East Pacific–North Pacific (EP/NP), East Atlantic/West Russia (EA/WR), Scandinavia (SCA), Tropical/Northern Hemisphere (TNH) and Polar/Eurasia (POL) patterns, signed as CPC and PSL sign them. A positive AO has low pressure over the Arctic and higher pressure in a ring around 45°N, the westerlies strong and the cold held in the north; a negative AO lets it out. A positive NAO has low heights near Iceland and high heights near the Azores; a positive PNA has higher heights near Hawaii and over western North America and lower heights south of the Aleutians and over the southeastern United States; the EPO and WPO compare a subtropical box with a high-latitude box over the eastern and the western North Pacific and are negative when the high latitudes are high, as under a ridge over Alaska (EPO) or the Bering Sea and Kamchatka (WPO).

The indices are the GEFS and GEPS pages’ own, so they agree with theirs. CPC’s nine patterns project the 500 hPa height anomaly over 20–90°N, divided by its standard deviation for the calendar month and weighted by √cos(latitude), on that month’s least-squares projector from the regression of NCEP/NCAR reanalysis heights on CPC’s monthly indices, all nine together (CPC’s method, after Barnston & Livezey 1987). Out of sample they follow CPC’s monthly indices with r = 0.66 (EP/NP) to 0.86 (NAO) and CPC’s daily NAO and PNA with r = 0.80 and 0.70. The EPO and WPO are PSL’s box differences in metres: EPO = 20–35°N minus 55–65°N, both 160–125°W; WPO = 25–40°N minus 50–70°N, both 140°E–150°W. The AO is the leading pattern (the first empirical orthogonal function) of daily sea-level pressure anomalies north of 20°N over 1991–2020, weighted by √cos(latitude), applied to the anomaly in hPa; it follows CPC’s daily AO with r = 0.97. A pattern exists only in the months CPC defines it: EP/NP not in December, TNH in December to February only; outside them the index has no value and the views say so. Each index is in seasonal standard deviations: the standard deviation of the daily index within 15 days of the date over 1991–2020, so ±1 means as much in July as in January.

The anomalies. Each member’s 500 hPa height (for the AO, its sea-level pressure) at 00 UTC of each day of a 00Z run (12 UTC of a 12Z run), not a daily mean, averaged conservatively onto the patterns’ 2.5° grid, less the ERA5 1991–2020 normal for that day at that hour (for 500 hPa the 00 UTC normal and at 12 UTC the same plus the mean difference between the two hours in ERA5’s climatology by hour of day, the 500 hPa tide; for sea-level pressure ERA5’s own normals at 00 and 12 UTC). The ensemble mean is the mean of the members’ indices.

Calibration. Each ensemble is calibrated against its own past runs, scored the same way: every archived 00Z run in the verification database, from March 2024 for IFS-ENS and GEFS, July 2025 for AIFS-ENS and 2026 for GEPS (its open archive keeps only about 50 days), against ERA5 at 00 UTC with the same normal. The AO is calibrated from each past run’s ensemble-mean sea-level pressure, kept from March 2024 for IFS-ENS and GEFS and from 21 September 2026 for AIFS-ENS and GEPS, so those two show their AO without demonstrated skill until about 30 of their runs near the date have been checked against ERA5. From the runs started within 45 days of today’s date in any year, a regression of ERA5’s index on the ensemble-mean index at each lead gives the calibrated forecast and its error; the chance of the positive phase (≥ +0.5 sd) or the negative phase (≤ −0.5 sd) comes from that regression’s Student t predictive distribution, widened for the uncertainty of the fit itself (the GEFS page’s method). A 12Z run takes the 00Z runs’ fit at the same lead in hours.

Skill. A lead counts only when the hindcast correlation is at least 0.25, significant at 5% (one-sided, on the effective number of runs, which counts consecutive daily starts as largely the same case: n(1 − r1r2)/(1 + r1r2), with r1 and r2 the day-to-day autocorrelations of the forecast and observed series; Bretherton et al. 1999), and still significant when the false discovery rate is held at 10% over every ensemble, index and lead of the update (Benjamini & Hochberg 1995). Elsewhere the figures hatch the lead and give no probability. A lead with fewer than 30 runs is not tested. The skill view gives each correlation and the number of runs behind it.

The blend. The ensembles’ mean indices are also combined, index by index and lead by lead, from the newest start every model in the blend has run. A blend needs the models’ past runs on the same days in at least two seasons, so that its weights can be tested on a season they were not fitted on, and at every lead to day 15 enough runs outside each season to fit on. This autumn’s runs are few at the long leads (ERA5 is a week behind), so a model needs two seasons besides the current one: the blend takes as many of the four as that allows and names them (IFS-ENS and GEFS now; AIFS-ENS, whose runs began in July 2025, from mid-2027; GEPS once its open archive is that long). Two blends are compared out of sample, each season predicted from fits on the others: equal weights, the mean of the models’ indices, and skill weights, the regression of ERA5’s index on theirs fitted on the lead and the next ones either side. Each is calibrated as a single model is, and scored on the same starts as each model alone by its correlation and by the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) of its calibrated Student t. Skill weights are used only where their CRPS beats the equal weights’ in a one-sided paired test at 5% on the effective number of starts; elsewhere the weights are equal, as in short samples they usually should be (DelSole 2007). The blend’s skill is its out-of-sample correlation and passes through the same gate, in the same false-discovery family as the models; the skill view gives it with its runs, marks SW where skill weights won, and below it shows how much its CRPS differs from the best single model’s and how much skill weights changed it, in bold where the paired test finds the difference.

Run to run. Each run’s indices are kept as it arrives (every member, their mean and their 10–90% range at each lead, the last ten days of 00Z and 12Z runs), and the run-to-run view lays the last eight runs’ ensemble means over one another by the date they are for, so a forecast that keeps moving one way, or that swings back and forth, shows as such. The record began on 29 October 2026; until a model holds eight runs its panel says when its record began. The hatching is the newest run’s: a lead without demonstrated skill is hatched whichever run is read there.

Limits. The record mixes model versions: IFS-ENS cycles 48r1, 49r1 (from 12 November 2024) and 50r1 (from 13 May 2026), AIFS-ENS versions 1 and 2 (from 13 May 2026); GEFS is version 12 throughout. Two or three autumns are a short record, so the calibration and the skill will firm up as runs accumulate, and the correlations of neighbouring leads and indices are not independent. ERA5 reaches the database about a week behind real time, hence the gap before each run.

Turnover. Each day at 00 UTC, parcels released on a 3° grid over 15–33° of both hemispheres at 500 hPa are traced back 30 days through the 6-hourly winds (a second-order Runge–Kutta step of one hour, the winds linear in time and space). A parcel’s transit time is how long before its release it last left the tropical outflow layer (equatorward of 20°, within 150 hPa below the dynamic tropopause). The panels give, for each sector and the week’s seven releases, the median transit time of the parcels traced to the outflow layer and, in percent, the share of the air that was (area-weighted: the rest had not been in the layer within the 30 days); ERA5’s numbers come from the same code on ERA5’s own 6-hourly winds, every day of 1991–2020, so the 30-day horizon and the grid treat both alike. A shorter transit is a faster-turning cell. Lower down, at 700 hPa, most of the air is not traced to the outflow within 30 days and the median of the rest is set by the horizon, so the tracker stays at 500 hPa. Transit-time map: in cells of two neighbouring release points, the median transit time of their fourteen parcels of the week, grey where fewer than four were traced, with a sample of the latest release’s parcels drawn back to where each left the layer.

Drop a particle. The other way round: a particle dropped anywhere between 20°S and 20°N, at 150, 250 or 400 hPa, is carried forward on the same corrected winds, from one of four releases a week apart, for up to 30 days or until the last analysis — or, from the newest 00 or 12 UTC IFS control run, ten days into its forecast (as forecast: the model’s drift is measured for the ensembles only, so it is not removed). The click takes the nearest point of a 2.5° × 3° grid whose paths are worked out in advance each day; the map draws the path with a dot every five days, the panels below its pressure and latitude, and the list says when it left the deep tropics and when it sank through 500 hPa. A click on a path (or its line in the list) picks that particle for a time–height plot, coloured by its distance from the equator, so the poleward drift and the sinking read together; the heights are the standard atmosphere’s for each pressure, since the paths are kept in pressure. One path is one parcel on one set of analyses, an illustration of the circulation rather than a forecast: neighbouring parcels can part company.

The analysis is first corrected by its mean offset from ERA5 at the same season: on every fifth day since January 2025 that ERA5 has published, the IFS control less ERA5 at the four times, fitted with an annual and a semiannual harmonic — for the mapped fields, and for the 3-D winds and temperatures the trajectories and their tropopause use. Nothing is drawn while fewer than 60 such days are paired.

Rain-forced circulation

Open this figure

The question. Rain is the tropical atmosphere's main heat source: every millimetre per day releases about 29 W m−2 of latent heat into the air column. Where it rains more than usual the air rises, spreads out near the tropopause and sinks somewhere else. That overturning is what the 200 hPa velocity potential χ200 maps (green = air diverging aloft over rising motion, orange = converging aloft over sinking). This figure asks, week by week, how much of the observed χ200 anomaly the rain anomaly alone accounts for — and, for the next two weeks, whether the AIFS ensemble's forecast rain and its forecast circulation tell the same story.

The Gill model. Gill (1980) is the simplest dynamical model of how the tropics respond to a heat source. It is a linear shallow-water model on an equatorial β-plane (the Coriolis parameter growing in proportion to the distance from the equator), for the troposphere's first baroclinic mode — winds aloft opposite to the winds below, heating peaking in the middle troposphere — solved for the steady state, with the same damping ε on the winds and the temperature:

$$\varepsilon u - y v = -\partial_x p,\qquad \varepsilon v + y u = -\partial_y p,\qquad \varepsilon p + \partial_x u + \partial_y v = -Q$$

A heat source on the equator drives a Kelvin wave to its east (low-level easterlies flowing into the heating) and a pair of Rossby gyres straddling the equator to its west (low-level westerlies flowing in), with rising air over the heating and outflow aloft. Here $Q$ is the rainfall anomaly converted to latent heating within 20° of the equator (tapered to zero from 18° to 22°: poleward of that, rain is mostly frontal and the equatorial β-plane does not apply). The equations are solved directly on a 2° grid with a wave speed $c=30$ m s−1; the upper-level wind is minus the lower-level wind, and χ200 comes from it by the same spherical-harmonic inversion as the observed χ200, so the two are compared like for like.

The assumptions are strong on purpose: steady state (the circulation keeps up with the heating within the week), linear (anomalies add and the background flow plays no part), one vertical mode (every column heats with the same vertical profile), rain as the only heat source (no radiative or sensible heating), and nothing from outside the tropics. The model has no mean flow, no land and no boundary layer.

Calibration, done once. Three numbers are fitted and then held fixed: a heating scale for χ200 (how column heating projects on the model's single mode), a separate scale for the 850 hPa wind, and the damping. They were fitted to the 1,043 weeks of 2001–2020, IMERG Late rain anomalies against ERA5 χ200 and 850 hPa wind anomalies (the same conventions as the live panels), over 20°S–20°N. The damping was chosen by out-of-sample share (heating scale fitted on the odd years and scored on the even years, and the reverse): ε = 0.04, an 11-day damping time. The choice is not sharp — anything from ε = 0.03 to 0.075 (15 to 6 days) explains 61% to within half a percentage point, while Gill's often-quoted 0.1 gives 60% and 0.5 only 35%. Confidence intervals below are 95%, from resampling whole years (2,000 draws).

Weekly χ200: the Gill response explains 61% of the anomaly variance (59–64%), 61% out of sample; median weekly pattern correlation 0.82 (0.80–0.83), half of all weeks between 0.68 and 0.89. Heating scale α = 0.0166 (0.0161–0.0171) per model unit.

Significance: pairing each week's circulation with rain from the same time of year in another year (500 shuffles of the years) explains at most 0.3% (95th percentile); none of the 500 reached 61%, so p < 0.002, the smallest this test can give.

Four-week means: 62% (58–67%), median r 0.82 (0.79–0.84).

By season (weekly share): DJF 57%, MAM 66%, JJA 58%, SON 65% — every season far above the null; the differences between seasons were not tested and are not claimed.

850 hPa wind: a much weaker fit, 15% of the vector wind variance (14.5–16.0%), median vector correlation 0.38, scale β = 0.00240 (0.00234–0.00245). The low-level flow feels friction, the boundary layer, land and the mean flow far more than the model allows, so the arrows (6° × 12° box means) show the direction of the inflow the rain implies, not a wind forecast.

An independent check on a different data set and time scale: GPCP monthly rain against ERA5 monthly χ200, 1991–2020, explains 58% (54–61%) with its own scale and 57% with the weekly one; IMERG monthly means, 2001–2020, 60% (56–64%).

Reading the rows. The first four rows are the last four weeks, the fifth their 28-day mean: the rain anomaly (IMERG V07 Late, against its own 2001–2025 climatology), the Gill response with its 850 hPa wind, the observed χ200 anomaly (the AIFS-ENS 0-h analyses at 00 and 12 UTC, against ERA5 1991–2020 with the 1991–2020 trend removed, as in the Walker figures), and the difference. Under each Gill panel, r is the pattern correlation with the observed map over 20°S–20°N (dotted lines) and "explains" is the share of its variance, $1-\sum(\mathrm{obs}-\mathrm{Gill})^2/\sum\mathrm{obs}^2$ (negative when the Gill map is further from the observation than a blank map). A week is marked significant when its r beats what 95% of the mismatched weeks reach: 0.74 for a week, 0.72 for four weeks. The bar is high because χ200 is a smooth, planetary-scale field, and two such maps correlate easily by chance; in 2001–2020, 66% of weeks and 70% of four-week means cleared it. A week marked "not significant" is one the rain does not explain beyond chance — not evidence that something else is at work.

The residual (last column) is the part of the observed χ200 anomaly that latent heating in the deep tropics, on the first mode, does not account for. That lumps together extratropical wave forcing, the ocean's direct influence on the boundary layer, higher vertical modes, the nonlinear and mean-flow effects the model leaves out, and errors in the rain and in the analyses. It is shown, not attributed: the figure cannot say which of these is responsible, and a large residual in one week is not by itself a finding. Note also that a least-squares scale makes the Gill maps weaker than the observed ones on average, so part of every residual is a muted copy of the observed pattern.

Weeks 1 and 2 of the latest AIFS-ENS cycle: the 51-member mean rain anomaly, with the model's mean rain bias at that lead removed (AIFS control against IMERG Late, 438 00 UTC cycles from July 2025, a seasonally varying field); the Gill response to it; the ensemble-mean forecast χ200 anomaly, with its mean drift against the AIFS analyses at the same lead removed; and forecast minus Gill. The bias correction. Every model rains too much in some places and too little in others (over the tropical Americas both ECMWF ensembles run too wet, IFS-ENS more than AIFS-ENS). For every 2° box, model and lead week we compare the ensemble mean of the model version running now with IMERG satellite rain over the same seven days (IMERG Late, scaled to the gauge-adjusted IMERG Final by season). Runs from earlier model versions are never mixed in, and runs from the same time of year count most. A correction is applied only where the bias is statistically significant, and only as strongly as its uncertainty allows; each member's week total is then multiplied by it. The runs behind the corrections: IFS-ENS (cycle 50r1) and AIFS-ENS (v2), every run since 13 May 2026; GEFS v12, every run since 1 October 2024 (two annual cycles); GEPS 8.1.0, the runs since mid-September 2026, when this archive began keeping them (the MSC Datamart holds only the last month). A model's bias is estimated only once it has six 21-day blocks of verified runs, so GEPS goes uncorrected until early 2027 at the soonest, and its figures say so. The “Bias removed” view shows the correction in force and the runs and dates it rests on.

The blend. The verified ensembles are pooled member by member, each model's members sharing that model's weight. The weights are fitted to the ranked probability score of the week's tercile forecast against IMERG over the archive (in its fair form, which removes the sampling noise of the six members the archive keeps per run), each run corrected with a bias estimated without its own 21-day block. They are tested out of sample: leave one 21-day block out, fit on the rest (minus the two weeks either side), score the block, and compare with equal weights on the same runs; a 90 % interval comes from resampling whole blocks. Fitted weights are used only when that interval lies entirely above zero (a one-sided 5 % test per lead week); otherwise the blend keeps equal weights. The blend figures give the weights in use and the test result. A model joins the blend once the archive it shares with the others spans seven 21-day blocks (about five months); until then it stays out of the blend and is shown only on its own (GEPS for now).

The normal week. GPCP V3.2 daily rain, 2001–2020: the mean and terciles of seven-day totals starting within ten days of the same date (GPCP is built on IMERG Final, so the corrected forecast and the normal use the same yardstick). A week is “above normal” when it falls in the wettest third of normal weeks.

When colour appears. In the anomaly and tercile views a box is coloured only where the share of members in the wet or dry third differs from one in three by more than sampling noise (binomial test on the effective number of members, false discovery rate 10 % over the map). Grey marks places where a normal week is nearly dry, so no tercile can be tested. In the blend each member counts with its model's weight.

Change since yesterday. Today's week 1 and week 2 are the previous day's days 2–8 and 9–15. For those same dates the view shows today's bias-adjusted ensemble-mean rain minus yesterday's, both corrected with this week's factors, so only the forecast change shows. A box is coloured only where the change exceeds what the members' spread allows by chance (Welch's t-test on the members of the two runs, the blend's models combined with their weights; false discovery rate 10 % over the map). The figure names the two runs and the dates.

Limits. The correction scales the rain; it does not fix how spread out the members are, so the chances can still be over- or under-confident. The strongest corrections are in the dry subtropics, where the models drizzle; part of that gap is drizzle the satellites miss (IMERG and GPCP under-detect light warm rain over the subtropical oceans), and the normal shares that blind spot. The ECMWF corrections and the blend weights rest on runs since May 2026, so they improve as the archive grows.

Convergence and monsoon troughs

What it shows. Where the low-level wind of the tropics flows together and where the monsoon troughs lie, for each day of the latest 00Z IFS-ENS run (ECMWF's physics-based ensemble, 50 members) out to day 14, in four sectors between 35°S and 35°N. Green is convergence at 850 hPa, about 1.5 km above the sea: the inflow under the ITCZ, the South Pacific and South Atlantic convergence zones and the monsoon lows. Brown is divergence, air spreading out under the subtropical highs. The thin arrowed lines follow the members' mean 850 hPa wind, heavier where it blows harder. The heavy black lines are monsoon trough axes.

How a day is made. Each member's wind is averaged onto 1° boxes, and each day is the mean of its 00 and 12 UTC states: half a day apart, they cancel most of the daily cycle of the low-level wind (sea breezes, mountain winds, nocturnal jets). Convergence and vorticity are computed member by member and smoothed over about 3.5° (a Gaussian of 1.5° standard deviation), because at the grid scale convergence is the noise of single showers. The map shows the members' mean.

When colour appears. A box is coloured only where the members agree on the sign of the convergence by more than chance: a two-sided sign test on how many members converge, the false discovery rate held at 5 % over the map. With 50 members that comes to about 33 on one side; the line under each map gives the day's count. Convergence weaker than 1.5 × 10−6 s−1 is left white. Late in the run the members put their lows and troughs in different places, and the colour thins to what every member has: the ITCZ, the coasts and the flanks of the mountains.

The trough axis. A monsoon trough has westerlies on its equatorward side and easterly trades on its poleward side, so its axis is where the 850 hPa zonal wind changes sign that way. The line is drawn where the members' mean zonal wind is zero, between 3° and 30° of latitude, where the wind falls by at least 0.5 m s−1 per degree of latitude towards the pole and the members agree (the same test) that the flow turns cyclonically. Pieces shorter than 8° are left out. An ITCZ where the trades of the two hemispheres meet with no westerlies between them, as over the central Pacific and much of the Atlantic, gets no axis: there the green band is the convergence zone.

Grey. Ground that stands above the 850 hPa surface (surface pressure within 10 hPa of the level, or lower, in the run's first state). The wind reported there is extrapolated under the ground, so nothing is drawn.

Limits. The map is the forecast as it stands, not a departure from normal: the ITCZ is green every day, no model bias is removed and no skill is measured. The level clears most of the land of the tropics, but the trade-wind inflow under the ITCZ over the oceans is shallower than that and shows weaker here than it is near the surface. Low-level convergence is not rain (the rain forecast is the figure for that). Two states a day remove the daily cycle only roughly. Along steep coasts and mountain flanks the colour is set by the terrain every day and says little about the weather.

Atmospheric angular momentum: Series and forecast

Open this figure

The explainer: what atmospheric angular momentum measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

Relative AAM is the angular momentum carried by the winds: $M_r=(a^3/g)\iiint u\cos^2\!\phi\,d\lambda\,d\phi\,dp$, the atmosphere's total westerly momentum about the rotation axis. It is dominated by the upper-tropospheric subtropical jets: the $\cos^2\phi$ weight peaks at the equator, but the deep tropics carry weak or easterly mass-weighted flow, so they contribute little to the total. The variability is carried mostly by zonal-wind changes aloft in the tropics and subtropics, where the weight is large; the shallow trade-wind layer holds little mass, so the trades act through the friction torque rather than as a store of momentum. It is shown here for the globe and each hemisphere from the winds of the 50 IFS-ENS members on 14 pressure levels (10–1000 hPa — effectively the full column; the same integral on ERA5's 37 levels is the reference check). IFS-ENS is ECMWF's physics ensemble: its change of angular momentum closes against its own torques, which AIFS-ENS’s does not (the torque budget below). The top panel is this year's observed AAM (black: ERA5 at 12 UTC every third day; ERA5 is published several days behind real time, so the line ends one to two weeks before the forecast starts) and the 15-day forecast (red; the shaded band is the members’ 10–90% range, the raw ensemble spread, not a calibrated interval) on the climatological annual cycle; the hemispheres are strongly out of phase (each peaks in its own winter). The bottom panel is the anomaly (departure from the 1991–2020 normal), which removes the seasonal cycle; its right-hand axis restates the anomaly as a percentage of the normal averaged over the forecast days, so it reads the forecast, not the months before it. El Niño strengthens the subtropical jets and weakens the trades, so the atmosphere holds more westerly momentum; conservation then requires the solid Earth to slow, so AAM ties directly to the measured length of day. The green trace is GFZ's ESMGFZ wind-term series (computed from ECMWF's operational analyses for the Earth-rotation community, its published anomaly rescaled onto absolute momentum) — an external check on the pipeline's day-to-day variability.

Surface torques

Open this figure

The explainer: what mountain and friction torque measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

Atmospheric angular momentum changes only through torques the Earth exerts on the atmosphere (measured here in Hadley: 1 Hadley = 1018 N m). Three torques act: friction, the surface wind stress; mountain torque, pressure pushing on resolved topography; and the gravity-wave drag the model exerts on sub-gridscale terrain. The first two are resolved live — the open-data forecast stream carries no gravity-wave stress, so that term is left in the residual. The budget applies to total AAM — the wind (relative) part plus the mass term the rotating Earth carries in its surface-pressure field. Only the wind term is computed here; it tracks the total closely on subseasonal timescales but is not identical to it.

$$\frac{dM}{dt}=T_{\text{fric}}+T_{\text{mtn}}\;(+\,T_{\text{gw}}),\qquad T_{\text{mtn}}=a^2\!\iint h_s\,\frac{\partial p_s}{\partial\lambda}\,\cos\phi\;d\lambda\,d\phi$$

Everything here is an anomaly against the ERA5 1991–2020 climatology at the same time of year, and that is deliberate. The absolute budget cannot be made to close: ERA5’s own three terms sum to −13.1 Hadley in the 1979–2025 mean against a required zero (the El Niño and mountain torque study) — the reanalysis adds westerly angular momentum at every assimilation cycle, and the resolved mountain torque’s mean depends on the grid it is computed on. Every one of those errors sits in the mean and cancels in an anomaly.

Maps: friction (top) and mountain (bottom) torque-density anomaly, days 1–15, each the mean of its 24 hours — red adds westerly momentum, blue removes it. Grey contours are the standardized surface-pressure anomaly the mountain torque acts on, so colour and contour read together: high to the west of a range and low to its east is a braking event. Series: the same anomalies integrated over the globe and each hemisphere; the gap between net surface torque and $dM/dt$ is the gravity-wave-drag torque (the sub-gridscale companion of the mountain term, which the model applies but ECMWF’s open data does not publish) plus, hemisphere by hemisphere, cross-equatorial transport — an internal flux that cancels in the global integral. By range: which barriers are doing the work.

How much is this worth? In ERA5’s monthly means, 1979–2025, the net surface torque anomaly correlates 0.87 with the month’s change in angular momentum (p < 0.001 even counting one independent value per year), with a slope of 0.74: the torques account for most of the month-to-month swing, and a slope below 1 is what a noisy predictor gives. Read them as a driver of week-to-month swings, never as something to accumulate.

Why IFS-ENS. A test of the budget inside the forecasts themselves (winter 2025–26) found that AIFS-ENS’s own torques stop accounting for its change in angular momentum after about day 10, while the physics-based IFS ensemble stays consistent at every lead, and more so with its own surface stress: does an AI weather model conserve angular momentum? So the budget here is IFS-ENS’s, from its 50 members.

Study: Why the atmosphere spins up in El Niño. In ERA5 and 41 CMIP6 models, the El Niño rise in angular momentum comes in through mountain torque, mostly a standing shift in the pressure difference across the Andes rather than more torque events; ERA5’s budget closes only with its analysis increments, which add westerly momentum the model loses, mostly over the oceans.

Friction is the model’s own surface stress: the eastward turbulent stress IFS computes (its turbulent orographic form drag included), accumulated in the open data and differenced over each day, so it is that day’s mean. The mountain torque is the trapezoid of the ensemble-mean surface pressure’s values at 00 and 12 UTC over the day, on ERA5’s terrain, the sampling and terrain of the climatology. Its climatology is the same quantity in ERA5: ERA5’s eastward turbulent surface stress, the mean of 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC each day, 1991–2020. Open data carry no IFS-ENS reforecasts, so any standing difference between today’s IFS and the version of it behind ERA5 is not removed and stays in the anomalies.
