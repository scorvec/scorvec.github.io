The page

Whether the winter polar vortex is about to weaken, and what that has meant at the surface before, in one place, opening on the next two weeks. The vortex, days 1–15: its strength and the chance of a sudden warming in every model, its shape member by member (split or displaced), the 10 hPa temperature, the vortex winds and the 100 hPa height, the dripping paint, the upper stratosphere to 1 hPa, and how the recent runs verified (every model’s maps and potential vorticity, GEOS FP’s among them, are on the stratosphere model maps). The waves driving it: the 100 hPa eddy heat flux with the SSW warning meter, planetary waves 1 and 2, the wave compass (why some wave patterns feed the vortex), the E–P flux and refractive index, and the regional reflection index. Down to the surface: the downward-propagation monitor, the polar cap at sea level and 500 hPa, and the AO and NAO, from AIFS-ENS, IFS-ENS, GEFS and GEPS member by member, and the descent over the polar cap by sector. Weeks 3–5: the GEPS and GEFS extended runs, drift-corrected. The season ahead: the December vortex from the seven C3S seasonal systems together, calibrated on their hindcast (the one seasonal vortex forecast here with demonstrated skill), and SEAS5’s vortex, QBO and sudden-warming risk against its own hindcast. The QBO and today’s analyses: the QBO from radiosondes and its zero-wind line forecast, the momentum budget, the Brewer–Dobson circulation and the water-vapour tape recorder. History and studies: every winter and every major sudden warming since 1980 from MERRA-2 (this winter from GEOS FP analyses, daily), with only the statistically significant results shown. Where several models answer the same question they share one figure slot with a model switch. Background on each diagnostic is in the sudden stratospheric warming, E–P flux and eddy heat flux explainers. Every pressure level from 100 to 0.4 hPa and the PV on seven isentropes, from the GFS to day 16, are on the interactive stratosphere model maps. The tropospheric circulation is on Global circulation and jets.

The vortex, days 1–15

Vortex strength and sudden-warming odds

Open this figure

The explainer: what sudden stratospheric warmings measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

The stratospheric vortex is the slow variable that sets the odds for the surface weeks later, so it is worth watching before it moves. Top: the zonal-mean zonal wind at 10 hPa, 60°N — the standard sudden-stratospheric-warming diagnostic. A reversal to easterly here in winter is a major SSW by the wind criterion (Charlton and Polvani 2007; the original WMO definition also asks for the temperature to rise from 60°N to the pole, which the Temperature view follows), which is why the zero line is drawn heavy and the member fraction crossing it is called out. Middle: the same wind at 100 hPa. Anomalies that reach this far down are the ones with a path to the troposphere — a 10 hPa event that never appears at 100 hPa usually never reaches the surface. Bottom: the 100 hPa polar-cap (65–90°N) height anomaly, positive for a weak or displaced vortex; this is the field that actually leads the AO/NAO response. Each model’s band is its members’ 10th–90th percentile and the solid line their mean; dashed lines are deterministic runs (GDPS, and NASA’s GEOS FP, whose 00Z run reaches day 10; GEOS FP is the operational sibling of MERRA-2, the reanalysis the climatology bands come from). The black line up to the start is the GEOS FP analysis (daily mean of its 00/06/12/18Z analyses), its polar-cap height moved onto the MERRA-2 scale by their measured offset (−14 m at 100 hPa). The AIFS-ENS polar-cap height uses the same 50 perturbed members plus the control as the winds. GEFS (30 members + control) and GEPS (20 + control) run to day 16. IFS-ENS is drawn with all 50 of its perturbed members (its ensemble files carry no control at these levels), and the IFS control (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), from its own open-data files, dashed to day 15, as is AIFS single (ECMWF’s deterministic AIFS run, its own model, not a member of AIFS-ENS). The chart is drawn about 90 minutes after the AIFS products (around 09:30 and 21:30 UTC), once IFS-ENS day 15 is on the mirror.

Temperature. A sudden warming is first of all a warming of the polar cap, so the second figure follows the 10 hPa temperature in the same models against the same MERRA-2 record. Top: the polar-cap temperature, the cos-weighted 60–90°N mean of the zonal-mean temperature (NASA Ozone Watch’s polar cap), as an anomaly against the MERRA-2 day-of-year median. At this level the cap has cooled by 0.7 K a decade since 1980, over 3 K across the record and more than November’s standard deviation, so the trend is removed and the band referenced to the current year, as for the 100 hPa height; without that every recent day would read about 1.7 K cold. Bottom: the gradient, the cos-weighted 85–90°N mean minus the zonal-mean temperature at 60°N, as it is (its trend, −0.2 K a decade, is not significant and is left in). From October to January the pole is normally 10–12 K colder than 60°N (the MERRA-2 median); above zero the gradient is reversed. A member easterly at 60°N with the gradient reversed on the same day meets the original WMO definition of a major warming (McInturff 1978), and the share of a model’s members that do so on some day to day 15 is called out under the zero line, as the wind-only share is on the first figure. The black line is the GEOS FP analysis drawn as it is: its offset from MERRA-2 at 10 hPa has not been measured (at 1 and 3 hPa it is about a kelvin). Each model’s temperature comes with its winds: the shared IFS-ENS 10 hPa file, the GEFS and GEPS fetch, NASA’s OPeNDAP server for GEOS FP and ECCC’s Datamart for GDPS, and for AIFS-ENS, AIFS single and the IFS control only the 90–60°N rows of each 10 hPa field. MERRA-2’s 2004 step, when it began assimilating Aura MLS temperatures, is near 1 hPa, not at 10, so the bands use all of 1980–2026.

Members. One ensemble’s newest run member by member: every member’s wind at 60°N at 10 and 100 hPa (thin), the members’ mean (bold) and the control (dashed; for IFS-ENS the IFS control), over the same MERRA-2 band and GEOS FP analyses. The members whose 10 hPa wind turns easterly on some day to day 15, a major warming by the wind criterion, are drawn red in both panels, so you can see whether the members that reverse at 10 hPa are also the ones whose 100 hPa wind weakens most, the coupling level a warming has to reach to matter for the weather. Each figure is drawn by the job that records that ensemble, as soon as its run is in.

Run to run. How the forecast has moved: the ensemble mean of the last eight runs of each ensemble (AIFS-ENS, IFS-ENS, GEFS, GEPS; four days of 00 and 12 UTC runs, two days of the four a day of AIFS-ENS and GEFS) for the 10 hPa wind and the 100 hPa cap height, older runs fainter, the newest with its 10–90 % member band, and the change of the newest run against the run a day earlier over its days 6–15. Below, every run of the last 30 days and its chance of a sudden warming: the share of members easterly at 10 hPa, 60°N on at least one day to day 15. A run-to-run swing is how the models’ view is changing, not a forecast of its own.

Model by model. Each model is drawn from its newest run as soon as that run is in (GEPS about 5 h after the start, AIFS single about 7 h, AIFS-ENS about 8 h, IFS-ENS about 9 h), so for a few hours of each cycle the models stand on different starts; the legend gives each one’s.

Split or displaced

Open this figure

What it shows. The shape of the polar vortex at 10 hPa in every member of every ensemble, each day to day 15: split in two (two separate low centres), displaced off the pole (one centre, its middle south of 66°N), centred, or gone (no part of the polar cap below the winter vortex edge). Members gives a row per member, sorted by the day its vortex first splits, with the ensemble’s control run (AIFS-ENS) or the IFS control (IFS-ENS) on a row of its own above, and the day’s shares beneath; All models the share of each ensemble’s members split and displaced, day by day; Run to run how the last runs have moved those shares and the latitude of the vortex’s centre. The maps of the same members are under Polar temperature and the vortex, 10 hPa.

The test. The same geometry as the split/displacement typing of the warming catalogue in the history items: the vortex is the part of the cap poleward of 30°N whose 10 hPa height is below the edge, the December–March mean height at 60°N; a split needs two separate regions more than 150 m below the edge, the smaller at least a quarter the size of the larger (the catalogue’s test, which gives the literature’s type for all 20 of its warmings that have one, though it was tuned on them); the centre is the area-weighted centroid of the region below the edge, and a centre south of 66°N is a displaced vortex (the moment method of Seviour et al. 2013). The catalogue measures NCEP R1 against R1’s own edge, 30 221 m. R1 runs 76 m above MERRA-2 at 60°N on the same 5541 winter days, and the forecasts start from modern analyses, so they use MERRA-2’s edge, 30 145 m (1980–2026). Each member’s height is first averaged onto the same 1.5° cells as the polar-cap normals.

Limits. The edge is a winter value, and early in the season the vortex is smaller than in midwinter: in late October the climatological vortex reaches the edge only north of about 67°N, so a young vortex is more easily called gone or split than a January one. A split or displaced vortex is not in itself a sudden warming (the polar vortex item’s share of members easterly at 60°N is that), and in the catalogue every warming that is not a split counts as a displacement, while here a vortex is displaced only when its centre is south of 66°N. Raw model output; no drift is removed.

Polar temperature and the vortex, 10 hPa

Open this figure

Polar temperature at 10 hPa. A sudden warming is, first of all, a warming: air descending over the pole as the vortex is displaced or split warms by tens of degrees in a few days, and the westerlies at 60°N then weaken or reverse. These maps show the 10 hPa temperature as its departure from the ERA5 1991–2020 normal for that day of the year, and the 10 hPa geopotential height, whose contours outline the vortex (the flow runs along them): a warm patch moving over the pole and the height lines crowding off to one side or pinching in two is the warming in progress.

The ensembles. The ensemble mean of AIFS-ENS (control and 50 members), IFS-ENS (50 members), GEFS (control and 30 members) and GEPS (control and 20 members), each day to day 15 on the same 1.5° cells as the polar-cap maps, the anomaly shaded only where enough members agree on its sign for a sign test at 5% (16 of 21, 22 of 31, 33 of 50 or 51); elsewhere grey. A mean of members whose vortices are in different places is weaker and rounder than any one of them, so the right-hand panel draws every member’s vortex: its height line 150 m below the winter vortex edge (29 995 m), coloured by whether that member’s vortex is split, displaced or centred that day (the test is under Vortex shape: split or displaced). The AIFS-ENS control and AIFS single (ECMWF’s deterministic AIFS run) are each drawn as one run.

The IFS control. ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run (called HRES until May 2026), here at 0.25°, every 12 hours to day 15 and in both hemispheres. It is a single realisation: by the second week a warming in it is one outcome among many.

The normal. ERA5 monthly-mean 10 hPa temperature, 1991–2020, each month averaged over the 30 years, with a smooth daily curve fitted at every grid point so that its month averages match (the mean and four annual harmonics), evaluated at the map’s day. Raw model output: no model’s drift is removed.

Vortex winds

Open this figure

The vortex as it actually looks, rather than as an index. Wind speed is shaded and contoured with wind barbs over it (in m s−1: half barb 5, full barb 10, pennant 50) and the geopotential height in black (dam, every 24 dam at 10 hPa and 16 at 100): the wind runs along the height lines, so the vortex’s centre and shape read from them and the shading says how fast. The pole itself is left blank poleward of 86°, where a latitude–longitude grid has a seam and nothing about the vortex is read. One hemisphere at a time, at 10 hPa. This is where the vortex lives and where an SSW is declared — the level the WMO diagnostic is evaluated on. The red contour is u = 0: where the westerlies end in the zonal sense (not the PV-gradient vortex edge), and the outer bound of the corridor $0<\bar{u}<U_c$ in which planetary waves can propagate vertically; outside it they cannot. The dashed ring is 60°, the latitude the SSW diagnostic is evaluated at. Speeds below the first contour are left white, which is why the summer hemisphere is largely blank — there is no vortex there to draw. Both hemispheres share the colour scale, so they are directly comparable.

Every forecast frame is the ensemble mean of 50 AIFS-ENS members (the analysis frame is the control, which is the analysis). The mean shows the part of the flow the members agree on. It is also smoother and weaker than any one member wherever they disagree, so a split that only a few members carry appears here as a stretched, slackened vortex rather than two centres. The eddy heat flux chart shows how far the members spread in wave-2, the component that splits the vortex.

The IFS-ENS version is the mean of all 50 of its perturbed members, on the same fixed colour scales. Its ensemble files carry no control member at these levels, so its analysis frame comes from the IFS control’s own files (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), the state the ensemble is started from. The IFS control version is that one run on its own, analysis to day 15: by the second week its vortex shape is largely its own, so a split or displacement in it is one realisation of many. The AIFS-ENS control and AIFS single (ECMWF’s deterministic AIFS run) versions are single runs in the same way. The GEFS and GEPS versions are the means of all their members (control + 30 and control + 20), their analysis frames the controls, on the cycle the IFS-ENS version is on.

100 hPa height: anomaly and 24 h change

Open this figure

What it shows. The vortex at the bottom of the stratosphere, day by day to day 15: the 100 hPa height (black, every 16 dam), its anomaly from the ERA5 1991–2020 normal of that day and hour, and its change over the last 24 hours. The change panel is where a lobe of the vortex is filling (red over it) or deepening (blue), which the height lines alone show only slowly; it is also where a warming coming down from 10 hPa first reaches the level that couples to the troposphere.

Models. The ensemble mean of AIFS-ENS (control and 50 members), IFS-ENS (50 members), GEFS (control and 30 members) and GEPS (control and 20 members), with the anomaly and the change shaded only where enough members agree on their sign for a sign test at 5% (16 of 21, 22 of 31, 33 of 50 or 51); elsewhere grey. The IFS control, the AIFS-ENS control and AIFS single (ECMWF’s deterministic AIFS run) are single runs and are drawn as they are: their lobes are sharper than any ensemble mean, and by the second week they are one outcome among many. Every model is averaged onto the same 1.5° cells as the normal.

Limits. Raw model output: no model’s drift is removed. The normal is a smooth annual curve (mean and three harmonics) through ERA5’s 1991–2020 monthly means of daily-mean height; the 100 hPa height’s daily cycle is a few metres, far below the scale shown.

Dripping paint, ground to 10 hPa

Open this figure

The dripping-paint view of the forecasts: the polar-cap (65–90°N) geopotential height at eleven levels from 1000 to 10 hPa (GEFS sixteen, to 1 hPa: the other models’ open data stop at 10 hPa), standardised exactly as in the MERRA-2 history drips (anomaly from a 4-harmonic seasonal cycle and linear trend, divided by the seasonal standard deviation), so a forecast can be read against the historical composites. Red is a high cap: a warm, weak vortex, and near the ground a negative Arctic Oscillation. Left of the line are the last 60 days observed in GEOS FP analyses; right of it the ensemble mean of the forecast.

Drift is removed before anything is drawn. Extended-range models slide towards their own climate with lead, and in the stratosphere that slide is large: in its reforecasts GEPS raises the 10 hPa cap by about 200 m by day 35, and GEFS by about 170 m. Left in, that drift would paint a warming that is only the model. So each model's anomaly is taken after subtracting its measured systematic difference from the MERRA-2 reference at that lead:

  • GEPS: from the GEPS reforecasts in the ECMWF S2S archive (2001–2020, control + 3 members, start dates within 8 days of the forecast's day of year). 10 and 100 hPa are corrected; the other levels are added as their reforecasts arrive from the archive.
  • GEFS: from the GEFSv12 reforecast daily climatology (2000–2019), which holds 10, 100 and 500 hPa; its five levels above 10 hPa (7, 5, 3, 2 and 1 hPa, from its pgrb2b files) are marked. Their MERRA-2 scale includes the step at August 2004, when MERRA-2 starts assimilating Aura MLS, as the history drips’ does at 5 hPa and above.
  • AIFS-ENS: no reforecast exists, so its control's cap forecasts from 25 recent cycles are verified against the GEOS FP analyses and the mean error at each level and lead is removed where it is significant (more than twice its standard error). It is small for the first week and rises to +15 to +45 m by days 10–15.
  • IFS-ENS (all 50 perturbed members): no open reforecast either, and no control at these levels on open data, so the mean of the 50 members on the same 25 cycles is verified the same way and removed where significant (at every level). In the stratosphere it drifts the other way from AIFS-ENS: the 10 hPa cap falls from 23 m above the analysis at the start to 53 ± 2 m below by day 7 and 104 ± 4 m below by day 15 (50 hPa −33 m, 100 hPa −14 m), a vortex that grows too cold and too strong; from 250 to 925 hPa it rises by 13–23 m by day 15, as AIFS-ENS does. Both drifts come from 00Z cycles between 30 June and 10 September 2026; a winter drift may differ.
  • IFS control (one run, from its own open-data files, day 15): the IFS-ENS drift above is removed — the same model at the same resolution, whose mean error over many cycles is the ensemble’s. Its lower panel has no spread and no member share.
  • AIFS single (ECMWF’s deterministic AIFS run, one run, day 15): a different model from AIFS-ENS, so AIFS-ENS’s drift is not used for it. Its own cap forecasts from 25 00Z cycles (27 July to 7 October 2026) are verified against the GEOS FP analyses the same way and the mean error removed where it is significant (at every level). At 10 hPa it starts 25 m above the analysis and is 46 ± 11 m above by day 15; from 925 to 200 hPa it rises by 19–25 m by day 15, and at 1000 hPa it reads about 20 m low from the start.

The days 1–15 item shows GEFS (control + 30) and GEPS (control + 20) as well, on the cycle the IFS-ENS chart is on, with the same reforecast drift corrections as their 35-day versions.

Levels where a model's drift has not been measured are marked by a grey triangle on the chart's right edge: there only the model's offset from the GEOS FP analysis over its first day is removed, so read them with caution, especially at long lead. The 1000 hPa cap needs that offset most: models extrapolate heights below the ground, while GEOS FP and MERRA-2 leave those points out (and on some summer days the observed value is undefined), so GEPS and GEFS read about 30–40 m low at the start (AIFS-ENS 11 m) by definition alone. The lower panel shows the 1000 hPa cap (a proxy for the Arctic Oscillation; high = negative AO) and the share of members with the 100 hPa cap at least one standard deviation high, the level that couples to the troposphere.

Change since a day earlier (View: Change). The newest run’s drip minus the same model’s run 24 hours earlier (12 hours when that one is missing), at the valid dates and levels both cover, in the same standard deviations: red is a cap now forecast higher, a vortex weaker than the earlier run said. The newest run is contoured over it, and the lower panel follows the change at 1000, 100, 10 and (GEFS) 1 hPa. Both runs have the same drift removed, so the change is the forecast’s own movement. Ensemble means move less from run to run than single runs do.

Run to run (View: Run to run). The 10 and 100 hPa rows of the drip, the ensemble mean of each of the model’s last eight runs over one another (the newest with ± one member standard deviation), the change of the newest run against the run a day earlier over its days 6–15, and every run of the last 30 days with its share of members whose 100 hPa cap is at +1 sd or more on the last day.

Upper stratosphere

Open this figure

The stratosphere does not stop at 10 hPa. Its top, the stratopause near 1 hPa (about 48 km), is where the polar vortex usually changes first: a sudden warming generally shows up there as a warming and a slackening of the westerlies days before it reaches 10 hPa, and it then works its way down, the way the dripping-paint charts show lower down. The spring final warming that ends the southern vortex (September–November) also comes down from the top. Most forecast products on this site stop at 10 hPa; four models publish higher, and this item puts them side by side. ECMWF’s AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS cannot join them: their open data stops at 10 hPa. Nor can GEPS: ECCC’s Datamart publishes its pressure levels only up to 10 hPa.

Time series: the zonal-mean zonal wind at 60° (the latitude of the sudden-warming criterion, reversal to easterly at 10 hPa) and the polar-cap temperature (the cos-weighted mean of the zonal-mean temperature over 60–90°) at 1, 5 and 10 hPa. GEFS shows its 31 members as a 10–90 % band with the ensemble mean and the control; the other three are single deterministic runs. Each model runs to its own range (GEFS 35 days from its 00Z run and 16 from 12Z, GFS 16, GEOS FP and GDPS 10) on a common axis, so switching models keeps the dates in place. Height–time: the same two quantities from 100 up to 1 hPa against time, the wind raw with its zero line in black and the temperature as an anomaly, with the last 30 days of analyses to the left of the black line; the colour scales are fixed, identical for every model. Polar maps: geopotential height (contours, dam) over temperature at 5 and 1 hPa, analysis to day 10, one colour scale per run shared by every model and frame. “Compare all” overlays the four models on the time series and tiles them on the sections and maps.

Model tops, and where the plots stop. Nothing above 1 hPa is drawn for any model. GEFS and GDPS publish nothing higher, and the top layers of every model are a damping (“sponge”) layer where the winds are relaxed artificially, so the levels nearest a model’s lid are its least trustworthy. GEFS (version 12, 64 layers) is the one to read with the most caution at 1 hPa: that is its top published level and its lid is not far above it. GDPS (the GEM model) has its lid at 0.1 hPa but ECCC publishes only 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50 and 100 hPa in the stratosphere, so its section is interpolated across wider gaps (the ticks on the right edge of each section mark the levels a model actually provides). GFS (127 layers) reaches about 80 km and GEOS FP (72 layers) 0.01 hPa, so 1 hPa is well inside both.

Raw output, not drift-corrected. Models drift toward their own climate with lead time, and the drift is largest near their tops. No correction is applied here: the GEFS reforecast carries wind and temperature at 1, 2, 3, 5 and 10 hPa, so a GEFS correction is possible but not yet built, and the other three have no reforecast with these levels. What is measured is where each model starts: every time-series panel prints its day-0 value minus the GEOS FP 00Z analysis of the same day (for GEFS, the control), after the 00Z update; after a 12Z update the other three start 12 hours after GEOS FP, so no offset is printed. On the first run (27 September 2026) the four analyses agreed to within 0.7 m/s and 1.3 K at 10 and 5 hPa but differed by up to 2.6 m/s and 5 K at 1 hPa (GFS, GEFS and GDPS all 2–5 K warmer on the polar caps than GEOS FP), where the observations constraining an analysis are thinnest: a difference between two models at 1 hPa is partly a difference in where they began. The temperature anomalies on the sections carry the same offsets.

Reference. The grey band is MERRA-2 (NASA GMAO), mean and 10–90 % range by day of year: northern hemisphere from MERRA-2’s daily means, 2005–2024; southern hemisphere from the MERRA-2 GMI replay (MERRA-2’s meteorology, sampled at 00Z every 10th day), 2005–2019, which tracks MERRA-2 itself to within 0.2 m/s and 0.2 K on average at 1 hPa. Not 1991–2020: MERRA-2 begins assimilating Aura MLS temperatures in August 2004, and its polar-cap temperature steps by about +4 K at 1 hPa and −4 K at 2–3 hPa across that date, so a longer base would put a two-kelvin artefact into every anomaly near the stratopause. The black line is the GEOS FP analysis (the daily mean of the 00, 06, 12 and 18Z states), the same assimilation system as MERRA-2 in a newer version; on the same 142 days of 2018–19 it ran 0.9–1.2 K colder than MERRA-2 on the polar caps at 1 hPa and 1.1–1.4 K warmer at 3 hPa, with the winds within 1 m/s, so a departure of the black line from the grey band smaller than that is not a real anomaly. Everything here is descriptive: no statistical test is made or implied.

Eddy heat flux, 100 hPa

The explainer: what the 100 hPa eddy heat flux measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

The wave maps show how large the planetary waves are and where their ridges sit; they do not show whether they propagate upward. That is the vertical component of the Eliassen–Palm flux, which at 100 hPa is proportional to the zonal-mean poleward eddy heat flux [v′T′]. A large wave that is vertically stacked carries almost none; one that tilts westward with height carries a lot. Positive is poleward in both hemispheres, which is wave activity entering the stratosphere.

The flux is quadratic, so it is computed per member and then averaged: the flux of the ensemble-mean fields fades with lead as the members’ phases decorrelate. Top left: the daily flux, members and the analysed record against the climatological 10–90 % range. Top right: the wave-1 and wave-2 parts. Bottom left is the panel to read for the vortex: the trailing 40-day mean, standardised. A few strong days do little; weeks above +1σ weaken the vortex, weeks below −1σ let it strengthen (Polvani and Waugh 2004). In early autumn radiative cooling dominates, so a burst slows the vortex’s seasonal spin-up rather than reversing it. Bottom right: the ensemble-mean flux by latitude through the forecast.

SSW warning meter (bottom, Northern Hemisphere). Every member’s trailing 10-day mean flux is carried through the forecast and read against the level that preceded the sudden stratospheric warmings on record: 27.5 K m/s. The level comes from ERA5 1990–2026 and the stratosphere history’s 21 central dates (CP07 reversals since 1998, marginal cases left out): it is the value that best separates the 30 days before a warming from quiet November–March windows (Peirce skill 0.67, 90 % interval 0.58–0.75 by a bootstrap over winters). 20 of the 21 warmings had the 10-day mean at or above it in the 30 days before, first reaching it a median 13 days ahead (quartiles 9–23); the exception is March 2026. On a day at or above the level the chance of a central date within 30 days is 35 % [25–44] against 12 % on any winter day, and 28 % of quiet 30-day windows reach it: a warning is a tripling of the odds, not a forecast of a warming. The 10-day window was chosen over 20 and 40 days because it has the best skill on the record and the sharpest odds; the 40-day mean (the panel above it) says what the vortex has already absorbed, the 10-day mean says whether a pulse is arriving. The meter shows where the analysis stands now, the ensemble mean’s maximum through the forecast, the members’ maxima and the share of members reaching the level. No equivalent record exists for the Southern Hemisphere, so it carries no meter.

Climatology and analysed record. The 10–90 % range and the standardisation come from ERA5, 1991–2020: the same flux, wavenumbers 1–72, from single 00 and 12 UTC fields as the forecasts are single fields. It replaces NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis 1 (R1), which stopped updating in March 2026 and whose flux ran 6 % below ERA5’s in the north and 12 % in the south over 1991–2020 (6–8 and 25–30 % in December–January): a third of that gap in the north and half in the south is R1’s use of daily-mean fields, the rest the older, coarser reanalysis. ERA5 arrives about five days late, so the analysed record is the AIFS control’s 0-h field, ECMWF’s operational analysis.

Model error. Each model’s flux was compared with ERA5 on its own archived 00Z runs, every third day: AIFS-ENS since July 2025 (155 runs), IFS-ENS and GEFS since January 2025 (210 and 214), by season of the valid day and by lead (day 0, days 1–3, 4–7, 8–15, and 16–35 for GEFS). A lead block or season whose error is not significant counts as zero, and the correction runs linearly in lead and in season between the blocks, so it tapers rather than jumps; beyond the last block it keeps that block’s value only if significant. E–P flux

Open this figure

The explainer: what the E–P flux and its divergence measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

Where the torque budget shows how the Earth exchanges angular momentum with the atmosphere at the surface, the Eliassen–Palm flux shows how the waves redistribute it internally — the same quasi-geostrophic diagnostic used to diagnose sudden stratospheric warmings, computed live from the NOAA GEFS ensemble (the control and 30 perturbed members) and stepped through the 15-day forecast. Each member’s flux is computed and then averaged: the flux is quadratic in the waves, and the flux of the ensemble-mean fields would fade with lead time as the members’ wave phases drift apart.

Why GEFS (since 30 September 2026). GEFS carries wind and temperature at 31 levels, including 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 20, 30 and 70 hPa. ECMWF’s open data has nothing between 10 and 50 hPa, so the AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS versions this item used to show took the stratospheric divergence over 10–100 hPa in one step, and one-sided at 10 hPa. Those two loops are retired. Over 50–100 hPa, where all three models have levels, GEFS and AIFS-ENS agree: on the 30 September analysis their [v′θ′] at 40–80° correlates at 0.98–1.00 with amplitudes within 10 %. No GEPS: ECCC’s Datamart publishes GEPS winds and temperatures above 100 hPa only at 50 and 10 hPa, too few levels to resolve the flux’s divergence in the stratosphere.

How it is computed. Fφ = −a cosφ [u′v′] and Fp = a cosφ f [v′θ′]/[θ]p, with the zonal-mean static stability [θ]p at each latitude and level; the eddies are zonal wavenumbers 1–3. Vertical derivatives are taken in ln p on the actual levels; the divergence is smoothed along latitude only (2.5°), never across levels. The shading is the divergence as a force on the zonal-mean wind (purple: waves decelerating the westerlies). At the top level, 1 hPa, the derivative is one-sided, so the 1–2 hPa layer is hatched. Latitudes poleward of 82° are masked.

Checks. Grey marks levels that lie below the ground on more than 10 % of a latitude circle (Antarctica, Greenland, the Andes, Tibet), from each member’s surface pressure: the model fills those levels by extrapolation, and fluxes computed there would form spurious saturated bands near the surface. The static stability is the zonal mean at each latitude, because one global profile under-states the stability above the polar tropopause. On 26 September 2026 the stratospheric pattern (100–2 hPa, 40–80°) matched the GEOS-FP analysis momentum budget at a pattern correlation of 0.87 in the south and 0.81 in the north. The many purple areas are real: in southern spring the planetary waves drag on the westerlies over most of the extratropical stratosphere. In the MERRA-2 climatology for late September, 80 % of that region is convergent; in the GEOS-FP analysis of 26 September, 47 %; in the GEFS loop, 50 %. Single frames near the poles can be large because a strong high-latitude trough is a real wave-1 eddy: on 1 October 2026 the AIFS-ENS analysis had the same [u′v′], about −160 m²/s² at 75–78°N and 250 hPa.

The arrows use the Edmon, Hoskins and McIntyre (1980) display scaling written for a logarithmic pressure axis (Jucker 2021), multiplied by 1000 hPa/p so the stratosphere is legible. The factor leaves every direction exact but grows with height, so compare arrow lengths along a level, and read the convergence from the shading. One fixed scale serves every frame and every run, so arrow lengths can be compared from day to day; the rare arrow longer than the frame can hold is drawn at the maximum length in red. The dashed magenta line is where the zonal-mean wind equals the wave-1 Charney–Drazin critical velocity: wave 1 propagates upward only where 0 < ū < Uc.

Refractive index

Open this figure

Reading it. The refractive index a²n² describes the planetary waves’ waveguide, the way a lens guides light. Stationary waves travel where it is positive (blue) and bend toward higher values. Where it is negative (grey, inside the red zero line) they cannot travel, and they fade over a short distance. Where the wind is easterly or zero (blank) they meet a critical line and are absorbed. The top row is wave-1 and the bottom row wave-2. Wave-2’s guide is narrower (the k²/cos²φ term), so the polar cap closes to it first. Three patterns matter: a corridor of positive values from the tropopause up into the vortex lets waves reach and weaken it; positive values only on the subtropical side turn them away from the pole; and a negative layer over the polar cap, above a positive one, can turn them back down.

Reflection or not. The picture alone does not say whether waves are being reflected. Over a strong jet core the index stays positive, because the jet sharpens its own potential-vorticity gradient, and next to a critical line it runs off to ±infinity. So each wave-1 panel also gives the wind at 60°N at 1 hPa minus 10 hPa. A negative value (the wind weaker aloft) is the reflecting configuration (Perlwitz and Harnik 2003); read it with the sign of the heat flux in the Regional reflection index and Eddy heat flux items.

How it is computed. Following Matsuno (1970) and Andrews, Holton and Leovy (1987): a²n² = a q̄φ/ū − k²/cos²φ − a²f²/(4N²H²), where q̄φ = 2Ω cosφ − [(ū cosφ)φ/(a cosφ)]φ − (a f²/ρ)(ρ ūz/N²)z is the meridional gradient of the zonal-mean potential vorticity. It uses the GEFS ensemble-mean zonal wind (the control and 30 members) and the hemispheric-mean potential temperature for N², on log-pressure height with H = 7 km, at days 0, 5, 10 and 15, from 300 to 1 hPa. The wind is smoothed over 3° of latitude before the derivatives. The index is then smoothed over 3° of latitude and with a 1-2-1 filter in the vertical, and masked equatorward of 20° and poleward of 82°.

Limits. The index is linear and assumes the wind changes slowly compared with a wavelength, which fails near critical lines and sharp jets. The ensemble-mean wind is smoother than any one member’s, so late leads show a broader, blander guide. On five MERRA-2 cases, the extent of negative index at 1–5 hPa over 58–74°N did not separate three regional reflection onsets (25 January 2018, 4 February 2020, 6 February 2025) from the absorbing Novembers of 2009 and 2016, which had as much. The 1 minus 10 hPa shear was lower at all three onsets (−2, −1 and +17 m/s, averaged over five days either side) than in 2009 and 2016 (+28 and +35). That is why the panels give the shear, not a share of grey. Five cases are a check on the method, not a test of skill.

Regional reflection index

Planetary waves leave the troposphere mostly over Siberia and the North Pacific. When the stratosphere above turns them back, they come down over Canada, and the troposphere below answers with a ridge over Alaska and cold air over North America (Messori et al. 2022). The index is the contrast between the two: v*T* (the eddy heat flux, departures from the zonal mean) at 100 hPa over Siberia (45–75°N, 140–200°E) and over Canada (45–75°N, 230–280°E), each standardised by its ERA5 1991–2020 day-of-year mean and standard deviation: RI = z(Siberia) − z(Canada). An event is RI above 1 for ten days or more with the Siberian flux upward and the Canadian downward on average over it.

What followed in ERA5. Since 1990 there have been 32 such events in December–March, just under one a winter. Against random onsets in the same months, the East Pacific Oscillation fell to −0.43 sd over days 1–5 (p = 0.006) and −0.49 sd over days 6–12 (p = 0.002), and North America went from +1.6 K warm before onset to −1.9 K over days 6–12 (p = 0.0005): the Pacific Trough to Alaskan Ridge sequence Messori et al. found.

Link to the stratosphere. The members of one run share a model and a starting analysis, so the differences between them test the connection directly: do the members with the weaker lower-stratospheric vortex on days 6–10 go on to the higher Arctic heights and pressure on days 11–15? All pooled joins the ensembles after each one’s own means are removed.

Where the waves are absorbed. Planetary waves slow the westerlies in the layer that absorbs them, so a pulse too small for a sudden warming can still weaken the lower stratosphere if it is absorbed there and does not pass through. For two layers over 45–75°N, 100–50 hPa and 50–10 hPa, the upper panels give the waves’ driving of the zonal wind in every GEFS member (control and 30), from the Eliassen–Palm flux of zonal waves 1–3 through the layer’s walls: what comes in from below, what leaves through the top, what crosses the two latitude walls, and their sum, as the westerly wind it adds per day. Below zero, the waves are slowing the layer. The lower panels are the layer’s own zonal wind, member by member. The wind changes by much less than the driving, because the overturning circulation the waves force offsets much of it, so the test is between members: do those whose waves push harder from day 10 to day 15 lose more wind over those days? It is the partial correlation of the wind’s change with the summed driving, the member’s day-10 wind regressed out, and a number is printed only where the Freedman–Lane test passes, the two layers held to a 5 % false discovery rate. One field a day, quasi-geostrophic, raw model output with no normal to set it against. November to April.

Limits. The column must be unbroken on one day, which is strict: a descending anomaly that reaches the surface a week after it left 100 hPa, with the levels between already back to normal, is not counted. The members of a run are not independent draws, least of all in the first days, so a marked share says the ensemble holds more such columns than the calendar usually does, not how likely one is. A link inside an ensemble shows that the model couples its stratosphere to its surface in this run; whether the atmosphere will is what the verification of past runs answers. The ring and the link use raw anomalies, so a model’s drift moves the ring’s zero; the link compares members with each other and is not affected.

Descent over the polar cap

Open this figure

What it shows. Where the air over the Arctic is sinking or rising, from the stratosphere down through the troposphere. Sinking air warms by compression: a sudden warming is, in its vertical motion, a burst of descent over the polar cap, and where that descent reaches the 100–300 hPa layer it is pressing on the tropopause and the weather below. Column: the 65–90°N mean against height and time, one block per day and level, the last 21 days observed (GEOS FP analyses), then each ensemble’s members’ mean to day 15 (the colour steps widen away from zero, so a strong forecast does not swamp a weak analysis), and beneath it the cap’s 10–100 and 100–300 hPa layers with the members’ 10–90 % and the control run. Sectors: the same two layers over six 60° sectors of the cap (Greenland, Barents and Scandinavia, Kara and West Siberia, East Siberia, Alaska and Bering, Canadian Arctic): a displaced vortex sinks on one side of the pole and rises on the other, which the cap mean cancels. Maps: north of 50°N at 10, 50 and 100 hPa, each day to day 15.

The quantity. The log-pressure vertical velocity w* = −(H/p) ω with H = 7 km, the convention of the transformed Eulerian mean, in mm/s and drawn downward positive. The models publish ω (Pa/s); w* puts the thin stratosphere and the troposphere on one scale, where ω itself would be a hundred times larger below. Layer values are log-pressure means of the published levels (ECMWF open data: 850, 500, 300, 250, 200, 150, 100, 50 and 10 hPa; GEFS also 70, 30, 20 and 7–1 hPa).

The maps are smoothed before anything is averaged: each member’s field is cut to zonal waves 0–8 and a 3° Gaussian in latitude, because raw ω at these levels is dominated by gravity waves (over Greenland and the Rockies above all) whose up-and-down pattern is not the slow descent of a warming. An ensemble’s mean is shaded only where enough members agree on descent or ascent for a two-sided sign test at 5 % (33 of IFS-ENS’s 50); elsewhere grey. A control run is drawn as it is.

Eulerian and residual. The sectors and the maps are Eulerian: the air’s own up-and-down motion through each pressure surface, not the Brewer–Dobson (residual) circulation, which is a zonal mean and has no sectors. While planetary waves carry heat poleward, as before and during a sudden warming, the Eulerian mean over the polar cap rises: its adiabatic cooling offsets part of the warming the eddies bring, while the residual circulation, which carries the net warming and the ozone, sinks (Andrews, Holton and Leovy 1987). So the Column view shows the residual, with the Eulerian layer mean dotted beneath it, and a rising Eulerian cap under strong wave driving is the expected signature, not a contradiction; the sectors and the maps show where the air is actually sinking and warming by compression, on one side of a displaced or split vortex, and where it is rising on the other.

The residual descent. The Column view shows the cap mean of the residual vertical velocity ω* = [ω] + (1/(a cos φ)) ∂/∂φ (cos φ [v′θ′] / ∂[θ]/∂p), the descent of the transformed Eulerian mean that carries a warming down and that downward control is built on. Averaged over the cap it is the Eulerian mean less cos φ0 [v′θ′]/(∂[θ]/∂p) / (a (1 − sin φ0)), the eddy heat flux on its edge φ0 = 65.25°N over the stability there, so it needs v and T on that latitude circle only. The stability ∂[θ]/∂p is taken through ln θ against ln p, which is nearly linear: differenced in p directly, on MERRA-2’s own 42 levels, it made the eddy term 4–12% too weak between 10 and 100 hPa (every November–March day, 2005–2025). GEFS and GEOS FP carry v and T to 1 hPa. For IFS-ENS and the IFS control only the three grid rows on 65.0–65.5°N are fetched, at 10–400 hPa: the open-data fields are compressed in north-to-south order, so those rows decode from the first sixth of each field’s bytes, about a gigabyte a run instead of eight. On their levels the stability is within 2% at 50–100 hPa and 10% low at 10 hPa, where the nearest level below is 50 hPa (the same MERRA-2 test). The Eulerian 10–100 hPa mean is drawn beside it: the gap between them is the eddies’ part. It is shown from 300 hPa up; below that the polar stability is too weak for the eddy term to be read from daily fields.

GEFS. The vertical velocity GEFS publishes is not ω: its post-processor writes the model’s geometric vertical velocity in pressure units, whose mean over a whole pressure surface is far from the zero that mass conservation requires. So GEFS has no sectors or maps here, and its Eulerian cap mean comes from continuity instead: the zonal-mean meridional wind on the cap’s edge integrated down from its top level, 1 hPa, flowing into the cap above each level. The flow above 1 hPa is left out; tested on an analytic circulation that costs about 1% at 100 hPa and 10% at 10 hPa, so GEFS is drawn from 10 hPa down. The ECMWF models’ ω and GEOS FP’s are the models’ own pressure velocities.

Why not AIFS-ENS. AIFS-ENS publishes a vertical velocity, but it is a learned output of the machine-learning model, not one its dynamics must keep consistent with the winds, and it fails against IFS-ENS started from the same ECMWF analysis. On 24 October 2026 at 12 UTC it put a band of strong ascent at 250 hPa alone, with much weaker motion at 200 and 300 hPa, which mass continuity rules out, and its 10–100 hPa cap mean was −5.5 mm/s on day 1 against IFS-ENS’s −1.5 (GEOS FP’s analysis −1.3 the day before), about twice IFS-ENS’s through day 15. So it is left out here; its other stratosphere products use its winds, heights and temperatures.

Limits. Raw model output, and no normal: read a forecast against the observed weeks before it rather than against zero. The observed record is the daily mean of GEOS FP’s 00, 06, 12 and 18Z analyses, which cancels the atmospheric tides; a forecast value is the field at the run’s own hour each day, so a small tidal part remains in the sectors (in the cap mean the migrating tides cancel). Vertical velocity is a derived field in every model, so its analyses differ more between models than heights or winds do.

Eddy heat flux, days 1–35

Open this figure

The explainer: what the 100 hPa eddy heat flux measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

The wave maps show how large the planetary waves are and where their ridges sit; they do not show whether they propagate upward. That is the vertical component of the Eliassen–Palm flux, which at 100 hPa is proportional to the zonal-mean poleward eddy heat flux [v′T′]. A large wave that is vertically stacked carries almost none; one that tilts westward with height carries a lot. Positive is poleward in both hemispheres, which is wave activity entering the stratosphere.

The flux is quadratic, so it is computed per member and then averaged: the flux of the ensemble-mean fields fades with lead as the members’ phases decorrelate. Top left: the daily flux, members and the analysed record against the climatological 10–90 % range. Top right: the wave-1 and wave-2 parts. Bottom left is the panel to read for the vortex: the trailing 40-day mean, standardised. A few strong days do little; weeks above +1σ weaken the vortex, weeks below −1σ let it strengthen (Polvani and Waugh 2004). In early autumn radiative cooling dominates, so a burst slows the vortex’s seasonal spin-up rather than reversing it. Bottom right: the ensemble-mean flux by latitude through the forecast.

SSW warning meter (bottom, Northern Hemisphere). Every member’s trailing 10-day mean flux is carried through the forecast and read against the level that preceded the sudden stratospheric warmings on record: 27.5 K m/s. The level comes from ERA5 1990–2026 and the stratosphere history’s 21 central dates (CP07 reversals since 1998, marginal cases left out): it is the value that best separates the 30 days before a warming from quiet November–March windows (Peirce skill 0.67, 90 % interval 0.58–0.75 by a bootstrap over winters). 20 of the 21 warmings had the 10-day mean at or above it in the 30 days before, first reaching it a median 13 days ahead (quartiles 9–23); the exception is March 2026. On a day at or above the level the chance of a central date within 30 days is 35 % [25–44] against 12 % on any winter day, and 28 % of quiet 30-day windows reach it: a warning is a tripling of the odds, not a forecast of a warming. The 10-day window was chosen over 20 and 40 days because it has the best skill on the record and the sharpest odds; the 40-day mean (the panel above it) says what the vortex has already absorbed, the 10-day mean says whether a pulse is arriving. The meter shows where the analysis stands now, the ensemble mean’s maximum through the forecast, the members’ maxima and the share of members reaching the level. No equivalent record exists for the Southern Hemisphere, so it carries no meter.

Climatology and analysed record. The 10–90 % range and the standardisation come from ERA5, 1991–2020: the same flux, wavenumbers 1–72, from single 00 and 12 UTC fields as the forecasts are single fields. It replaces NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis 1 (R1), which stopped updating in March 2026 and whose flux ran 6 % below ERA5’s in the north and 12 % in the south over 1991–2020 (6–8 and 25–30 % in December–January): a third of that gap in the north and half in the south is R1’s use of daily-mean fields, the rest the older, coarser reanalysis. ERA5 arrives about five days late, so the analysed record is the AIFS control’s 0-h field, ECMWF’s operational analysis.

Model error. Each model’s flux was compared with ERA5 on its own archived 00Z runs, every third day: AIFS-ENS since July 2025 (155 runs), IFS-ENS and GEFS since January 2025 (210 and 214), by season of the valid day and by lead (day 0, days 1–3, 4–7, 8–15, and 16–35 for GEFS). Each mean error was tested with a block bootstrap over the runs (15-day blocks); only those passing a false discovery rate of 10 % over all 336 tests are removed from every member. A lead block or season whose error is not significant counts as zero, and the correction runs linearly in lead and in season between the blocks, so it tapers rather than jumps; beyond the last block it keeps that block’s value only if significant. The largest: AIFS-ENS runs low in the northern winter, by 0.3 K m/s at the analysis, 1.1 at days 1–3, 2.3 at days 4–7 and 4.1 at days 8–15 (95 % interval 1.5–6.0), a quarter of the December–February flux by week two; 0.1–0.8 in autumn, with no significant error in spring. In the south it runs 0.3–0.9 K m/s high at days 1–7 in winter and spring. IFS-ENS and GEFS err less, mostly 0.2–1.2 K m/s low in the northern autumn and winter and the southern summer (GEFS reads 0.4–0.7 high in the northern summer beyond day 8). The analysed record reads 0.3 K m/s low against ERA5 in the northern winter and 0.2 in the southern summer, and that is removed from it too. The latitude panel shows the models’ own flux.

The GEPS and GEFS versions carry the same calculation to day 35 (one 00Z field a day, as for AIFS-ENS), against the same climatology and the same analysed record, so the models read on one scale. GEPS is not corrected: the Datamart keeps only its last month of runs, too few to test, and its reforecasts run the 10 hPa vortex progressively too weak with lead (about 4–6 m/s by day 35), so a systematic flux error could sit behind it. Read week 3 onward as the model’s tendency, not a calibrated probability. The 40-day mean at the end of a 35-day forecast is almost all forecast.

The season ahead

December vortex: C3S multi-model

Open this figure

Monthly seasonal forecasts of the stratospheric polar vortex were tested before this card was built: every C3S centre’s 1993–2016 hindcast of the monthly-mean wind at 60°N, 10 hPa (and 100 hPa) against MERRA-2, with the tests and the multiple-comparison correction fixed in advance. From the October start, only December passed: the equal-weight mean of the seven centres’ ensemble-mean anomalies correlated with the observed December at r 0.67 (95 % interval 0.36–0.86, Holm-corrected p 0.003), the Met Office (r 0.73) and DWD (0.61) individually; January, February, the DJF mean and every 100 hPa target did not. (BoM was left out on 16 October 2026, as on the ENSO forecasts page, and the test re-run over the seven remaining centres, 32 tests instead of 36; with BoM the December result was r 0.66, Holm p 0.005.) The skill is robust to dropping any single winter and is not October persistence. This card is that one significant result, made into a forecast.

Why calibrated. Each model’s ensemble mean moves only a fifth to two-fifths as much from year to year as the observed vortex does, so a raw ensemble mean or member count would put almost every December near normal. The card therefore regresses the observed December mean on the multi-model anomaly over the 24 hindcast winters and uses that regression’s predictive distribution (Student-t, which carries the uncertainty of a fit to 24 points) for the forecast. The weak / near-normal / strong categories are the thirds of the 1993–2016 MERRA-2 Decembers. The calibration is checked by refitting it with each winter left out in turn and scoring the left-out winter: the ranked probability skill, the share of winters inside the 80 % range and the tercile reliability counts are printed with their tests.

What it does not say. A December-mean wind is not a sudden-warming forecast: the same tests found no skill for SEAS5’s sudden-warming risk (the SEAS5 item carries a no-track-record banner), and January–February are not predictable this way. The card updates with each C3S issue from August to December (December is then 5 months ahead to 1 month ahead; only the October start was in the pre-registered test, the others are reported with the study’s descriptive false-discovery-rate grid). From January to July there is no December ahead and the card keeps its last issue.

QBO zero-wind line

Open this figure

Holton and Tan (1980) proposed that the quasi-biennial oscillation reaches the polar vortex through a critical line. Planetary waves coming up and equatorward from the winter midlatitudes cannot cross the latitude where the zonal-mean wind [u] turns easterly. With easterly QBO winds in the lower stratosphere that line sits in the winter subtropics, the waveguide is narrower, and more wave activity is steered toward the vortex. This item tracks where that line is, day by day, at 70, 50, 30 and 10 hPa. The QBO and the vortex item in the history group shows the equatorial wind itself.

The index. [u] is smoothed along latitude (Gaussian, σ 2°). The scan starts at the strongest westerlies between 25 and 45°N and moves toward the equator. The zero line is the first crossing whose easterly region beyond it reaches at least 1 m/s. Shallower dips are stepped over, because a −0.3 m/s wobble is within analysis noise. When there are no northern westerlies to start from, the index is undefined; at 50 hPa that is most of June to August. When no crossing qualifies before 45°S, the day is marked with a triangle. The line is broken where it jumps by more than 8° in a day: that is a switch between two crossings, not motion. The grey band is the MERRA-2 1980–2025 spread of the index for the date. The bottom panels are the mean [u] of past winters, split by the sign of the Singapore 50 hPa wind in October–November, with this year’s analysed line laid over them.

Checks. On the 46 years of MERRA-2 daily data, the 50 hPa index never goes undefined or censored in October–March. Its median change from one day to the next is 0.3°. On 1.3 % of days it jumps by more than 10°, when a shallow subtropical easterly band crosses the 1 m/s depth threshold. Varying the smoothing (0 or 4°) or the depth threshold (0 or 2 m/s) moves October–November days by less than 1° on 94–99 % of days. The live analysis is GEOS FP at 00 UTC. On the 82 days it overlaps MERRA-2 (12 June to 1 September 2026), its [u] between 30°S and 30°N differs by 1.0–1.9 m/s RMS, and its zero-line latitude differs by a median 0.3–0.4° at 70 and 50 hPa. No offset is applied. The forecasts compute the index per member (AIFS-ENS control + 50 members, IFS-ENS 50 members) and show the median and 10–90 % range. The forecast shading is the AIFS-ENS ensemble-mean [u].

Does it predict the winter vortex? No, not significantly, so this is a diagnostic. We tested it on the 46 winters 1980/81–2025/26. The outcome was the December–February zonal-mean wind at 60°N, 10 hPa (MERRA-2), plus whether the winter had a major sudden warming. The rival predictor was the Singapore 50 hPa wind in October–November. Intervals are 95 % bootstrap, over winters, and p-values come from 10,000 permutations (Freedman–Lane for the partial tests).

  • Singapore 50 hPa: r +0.39 [+0.13, +0.61], p 0.008. It is still +0.40 (p 0.008) with CPC’s DJF RONI held fixed. MERRA-2’s own equatorial 50 hPa wind gives +0.40 (p 0.006). This is significant.
  • The zero line at 50 hPa (from the October–November mean profile, the version chosen before the test): r +0.17 [−0.06, +0.39], p 0.34. Beyond Singapore and ENSO its partial r is +0.20 [−0.19, +0.39], p 0.21. Neither is significant. The reason is plain in the data: in autumn the 50 hPa zero line hardly moves with the QBO (r −0.06 with Singapore). It sat between 24 and 29.5°N in 45 of the 46 autumns, bounded by a subtropical easterly band that is present in both phases. The exception was 1997.
  • All eight versions (70, 50, 30 and 10 hPa, each taken from the mean profile or as a mean of daily values): none is significant on its own (smallest p 0.08). None adds to Singapore and ENSO (smallest p 0.12, and none passes a 10 % false-discovery rate). Varying the rule changes nothing either. One of five variants, a 4 m/s depth threshold, reached p 0.02 on its own, but that does not survive the 13 versions tried, and it adds nothing beyond Singapore (p 0.20).
  • Sudden warmings (24 of the 46 winters): no version separates warming winters from the rest (area under the ROC curve 0.39–0.66, p ≥ 0.06). Singapore 50 hPa does not either, in this sample (0.59, p 0.29).

The null is consistent with Garfinkel et al. (2012). In their model experiments the QBO acted on the vortex less through the position of the critical line than through its mean meridional circulation, which creates a barrier to planetary waves in the middle and upper stratosphere and so focuses their convergence on the polar vortex. So read this chart for where the waves can and cannot go this autumn, not as a forecast of the vortex. For that, the equatorial wind itself is the better (significant) predictor.

Momentum budget

Open this figure

The zonal momentum budget says, term by term, what is speeding up or slowing down the stratospheric westerlies. It is written in the transformed Eulerian mean (TEM) form of Andrews, Holton and Leovy (1987), in which the waves' effect on the mean flow is a single term, the divergence of their Eliassen–Palm flux: $$\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial t} \;=\; \hat f\,\bar v^{*} \;-\; \bar w^{*}\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial z} \;+\; \frac{\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf F}{\rho_0\,a\cos\phi} \;+\; X_{\rm GWD} \;+\; X_{\rm ANA} \;+\; \text{residual},\qquad \hat f = f-\frac{1}{a\cos\phi}\frac{\partial(\bar u\cos\phi)}{\partial\phi}$$ The left side is the observed change of the zonal-mean wind over each day. On the right: the Coriolis force on the residual (Brewer–Dobson) meridional flow $\bar v^{*}$; vertical advection by the residual vertical flow $\bar w^{*}$; the drag of the resolved waves (planetary and synoptic, also split into zonal wavenumbers 1, 2 and 3+); the model's parameterized gravity-wave drag, orographic and non-orographic together (GEOS FP publishes only the total); and the analysis increment, the push the data assimilation gives the model to keep it on the observations. The residual is what is left over: numerical damping and the sampling error of the eddy fluxes. A negative term pushes westward and slows a westerly vortex.

The same budget, with the same code, averaged over every major sudden warming of 1980–2019 is in Momentum budget of past warmings (Stratosphere history).

The vortex view. Every term averaged over 55–65° at 10 hPa (1 and 50 hPa as options), as trailing 7-day means or daily values; the legend gives each term's mean over the last 30 days. Above it, the vortex wind itself against the MERRA-2 normal and its 10–90 % range of single days; below it, the resolved-wave term by zonal wavenumber against its MERRA-2 normal. Each day is the mean of eight 3-hourly analyses (00–24Z) with each term computed per analysis: the terms swing by several m/s per day within a day, so a single daily analysis can be off by several m/s per day, and on a test day six-hourly sampling missed the model's own daily-mean dynamical tendency by 0.2–0.5 m/s per day at 1–5 hPa where three-hourly came within 0.05–0.15.

How well it closes. GEOS FP publishes its own wind tendencies, so the budget can be checked twice. First, the day's change in the analysed wind equals the sum of the model's five published tendencies (dynamics, gravity-wave drag, increment, moist, turbulent) to 0.02 m/s per day rms on the three days checked; the moist and turbulent tendencies are exactly zero above 100 hPa. Second, the TEM terms computed here reproduce the model's own dynamical tendency, so the residual is small. Over the first 12 days (15–26 September 2026, with the operator before its 25 October stability fix; the figure's footer gives the current operator's statistics), daily values: at 10 hPa the five terms explain 99.2 % (60°N) and 98.5 % (60°S) of the day-to-day variance of the observed change, with a residual of 0.07 and 0.24 m/s per day rms against 1.2 and 2.4 for the resolved waves; at 50 hPa 99.9 % in both, residual 0.04 and 0.10. At 1 hPa it is 97.8 % in the north but only 93 % in the southern spring, where a systematic residual of about −2.3 m/s per day appears: the model's dynamics hold a westward tendency that the TEM terms on the published pressure levels (0.7, 1 and 2 hPa) do not capture, partly the coarse vertical grid there and partly, it seems, the model's damping near its top (the residual tracks the wind, r −0.6). Read 1 hPa in the south as indicative. The figure's footer gives these statistics for the whole record shown.

The stability. The eddy fluxes divide by the static stability ∂θ/∂p, taken through ln θ against ln p. Until 25 October 2026 it was differenced in p directly, which curves sharply and made the resolved-wave drag near 10 hPa about three times too weak in winter (MERRA-2 at 60°N, December–February: −1.5 against −5.2 m/s per day). Days computed the old way are refetched and never drawn beside the new ones, so for a few days after that date the record shown is shorter.

The analysis increment is not small. Over the same 12 days it averaged 0.43 m/s per day in size at 60°S, 10 hPa against 0.13 for the gravity-wave drag, 0.13 against 0.01 at 50 hPa, and 1.5 against 1.1 at 1 hPa; at 60°N it matches the drag at 10 hPa and is three times it at 1 hPa. Only in the tropics at 10 hPa does the drag dominate (0.35 against 0.04). Part of the forcing the model's own physics does not supply is supplied by the observations.

Tropical upwelling. Brewer–Dobson circulation

Open this figure

The Brewer–Dobson circulation is the slow overturning of the stratosphere: air rises through the tropical tropopause, drifts poleward and sinks over the winter pole. It sets how fast air, water vapour and ozone move through the stratosphere, and it is driven from above — waves breaking in the extratropical stratosphere pull the circulation along. This product estimates it by downward control (Haynes et al. 1991): the zonal momentum forcing — the divergence of the Eliassen–Palm flux of the resolved waves plus the parameterized gravity-wave drag — divided by the Coriolis term gives the residual meridional wind, integrated from the top down into the residual streamfunction Ψ*. The tropical upward mass flux is Ψ* at the two subtropical turnaround latitudes, fixed for each level from MERRA-2’s annual-mean circulation (41°S and 36°N at 70 hPa, 23°S and 33°N at 100 hPa): sought day by day, the turnaround sat on the edge of its search range on 39% of days and jumped by up to 18° as the flat summer-hemisphere profile changed, which moved the index with every change of the climatology. Estimating it from the analysed mean meridional wind or vertical velocity directly does not work: in both GEOS FP and MERRA-2 that version shows no northern-winter maximum at all, because the tropical upwelling (~0.3 mm/s) is buried in the noise of a daily zonal mean.

The reference is the MERRA-2 GMI replay on 29 years (1991–2019), sampled at 00Z every 10th day, 1,070 days in all: at 70 hPa the annual mean is 6.0×109 kg/s with one December–January maximum, 2.3× the June minimum (December–February 1.85× June–August), as in the published reanalysis estimates. GEOS FP is put on the MERRA-2 scale by the same-day difference measured on 38 days of 2018–19, fitted as a function of the season (a mean and annual and semiannual harmonics): on average −0.6 at 70 hPa, nearly all of it GEOS FP's weaker gravity-wave drag; at 100 hPa it swings from about −1.6 in January to +1.5 in October (F-test against a constant, p 0.001). The fit’s uncertainty at the days compared enters every test. The forecast holds the last five days' drag fixed, because GEOS FP publishes no forecast wind tendencies. Values are trailing 30-day means: a single day scatters by about ±3 at 70 hPa and ±4 at 100 hPa. The stability ∂θ/∂p in the eddy fluxes is taken through ln θ against ln p: differenced in p directly it weakened the resolved-wave part by 2–5%. The analysis increment, the reanalyses' own correction to the momentum budget, is left out.

Water-vapour tape recorder

Open this figure

Air enters the stratosphere through the cold tropical tropopause, which freeze-dries it to the water-vapour saturation of its coldest point. That cold point is coldest in northern winter and warmest in northern summer, so the air entering carries an annual cycle of water vapour — about 3 ppmv in February, 5 in September at 100 hPa — and the slow tropical ascent of the Brewer–Dobson circulation lifts it like a signal written on a rising tape: the tape recorder (Mote et al. 1996). The slope of its dry and moist bands is the ascent speed; the value at 100–70 hPa is the entry value the cold point has just set.

The data. The long record is Aura MLS version 6 (ML2H2O v006, NASA JPL / GES DISC), the reference for stratospheric water vapour: 10°S–10°N daily means of the profiles that pass the v6 data-quality screening (status, quality, convergence, precision), August 2004 on, 7,344 good days. MLS is reached with an Earthdata login, which the site's GitHub Actions do not hold, so the MLS record is built offline and committed; it currently ends on the last MLS day marked on the figures. Two limits: since May 2024 MLS's 190-GHz radiometer, which measures water, has run about one week a month to save its life, and about four days a month since early 2026, so the recent record is monthly snapshots (the tape-recorder section fills the gaps of up to seven weeks by interpolation, marked in its strip); and the radiometer is ageing: a drift against frost-point balloon sondes remains after the v5/v6 correction (MLS v6 data-quality document).

GEOS FP after the last MLS day. The newest weeks come from the GEOS FP analyses (NASA GMAO, one 12Z analysis a day, updated twice daily). GEOS FP does not assimilate stratospheric water vapour, and it shows: after the January 2022 Hunga eruption, which injected about 150 Tg of water, MLS measured +1.0 to +1.4 ppmv at 30–10 hPa over the tropics in 2022–2023 while GEOS FP stayed at its old values (−0.1), a gap of about 20 %. Its water at 100 hPa does follow MLS (month-to-month anomaly correlation 0.82 in 2018–2021, 0.92 since 2024) because the entry value is set by the analysed temperatures; from 70 hPa up it does not (r from −0.35 to +0.55 by level and period). So GEOS FP is used only after the last MLS day, shifted onto the MLS scale by the mean difference over the 60 days before it. That offset was chosen by a hindcast of 87 MLS months since 2019; a monthly mean predicted that way is off by 0.17 ppmv (rms) at 100 hPa and 0.40 at 70 hPa, and that error is added to every test that uses GEOS FP.

Entry value. The last 30 days are tested against the MLS 2005–2021 normal for the same dates (years before Hunga), with the year-to-year spread of 30-day means plus the GEOS FP error. At 100 hPa the water tracks GEOS FP's analysed 100 hPa temperature: monthly anomalies correlate at r = 0.85, +0.36 ppmv per kelvin (p < 0.001, autocorrelation-adjusted; the temperature anomaly is against GEOS FP's own 2018–2025 years).

Ascent speed. At each MLS level an annual harmonic is fitted over a calendar year; the time of the moist maximum rises with height, and the speed is the height gained per day (log-pressure height, scale height 7 km, the coordinate of the residual w*). The 100 hPa level is left out: MLS's ~3 km vertical resolution smears the entry level. Over 2005–2021 the band rises at 0.27 mm/s at 68 hPa, slows to 0.23 mm/s near 46 hPa and speeds up to 0.34 mm/s at 26 hPa — 83–46 hPa averages 0.26 ± 0.05 mm/s (single years), 46–26 hPa 0.27 ± 0.03, about 45 days per kilometre. Those match the published MLS and SWOOSH estimates (Brehon et al. 2026: 0.21–0.33 mm/s, slowest near 50 hPa). The latest 12 months are compared with the single years by a z-test with a bootstrap over calendar months (the duty-cycled data come in monthly bursts).

Against w*. The tape moves with the residual vertical velocity plus vertical mixing into the tropical pipe, so it runs a little faster than w*. The site's Brewer–Dobson product gives, by downward control, a MERRA-2 w* of 0.22 mm/s at 70 hPa and 0.23 at 50 hPa (annual means over the 15 reference years between 1991 and 2009, averaged between the turnaround latitudes, about ±25–35°): the MLS tape is about 20 % faster at 70 hPa and within 10 % at 50 hPa. The figure also marks GEOS FP's w* over the last 30 days, by downward control (on the MERRA-2 scale) and from its analysed ω*. GEOS FP's own tape recorder (2018–2025) rises at 0.29–0.35 mm/s between 50 and 30 hPa, 10–20 % faster than MLS's.

The explainer: what sudden stratospheric warmings measures, the equations and how to read the chart.

A major sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) follows Charlton and Polvani (2007): the first day the zonal-mean wind at 60°N and 10 hPa turns easterly, November to March. A second event in the same winter needs 20 straight westerly days in between, and a reversal that never recovers (at least 10 westerly days before 30 April) is the spring final warming, not an SSW. On MERRA-2 that finds 30 reversals since 1980. Two of them, 17 February 2002 and 28 November 2025, were one day of easterlies weaker than 0.2 m/s that NCEP R1 never shows; they are listed as unconfirmed and left out of every composite and rate. That leaves 28 events in 24 of 46 winters.

Deep or shallow: the mean standardised polar-cap height at 100–150 hPa over the 30 days after, split at the median. It was the best single predictor of the surface response in our earlier tests, better than how warm the stratosphere got or how far down the warming reached. After the 14 deep events the AO averaged 1.42 below the same dates in other years over days 1–30 and 0.69 below over days 31–60, both significant (t-test, false-discovery rate 10 %). After the 14 shallow events there is no significant change. Against dates in the same season with the same AO over the previous 20 days and no warming, deep events ran 1.2 lower over the next 45 days, also significant (95 % interval 0.7–1.7); shallow events were no different. Blocking before an event does not predict its depth (r = 0.03). Depth is measured over the same weeks as the surface response, so this is association, not proof of direction. Depth is also only known two to three weeks in: it tells you what an event is doing once it has happened, not whether one is coming.

Split or displacement, from the 10 hPa height maps (MERRA-2 here is zonal-mean only, so the maps are NCEP R1): a split if, on any day from −3 to +7, two separate low centres lie below the vortex edge (the DJFM mean 10 hPa height at 60°N, minus 150 m), the smaller at least a quarter the size of the larger; otherwise a displacement. It matches the type usually given in the literature for all 20 events that have an established one (Charlton and Polvani 2007 for the events of 1981–2002, plus the well-studied later events), but it was tuned on them. The moment method’s aspect-ratio threshold of 2.4 (Seviour et al. 2013) got 17 of 20 on this 2.5° grid. 10 of the 28 are splits.

Strong-vortex events are the opposite extreme: the 10 hPa annular index (the standardised polar-cap height anomaly with its sign flipped) first crossing +1.5, November to March, at least 60 days apart, after Baldwin and Dunkerton (2001). There were 27. The AO averaged 0.76 above normal over the 30 days after them, which is significant, and was back to normal by days 31–60.

Who moves first

Open this figure

Does anything in the tropics give warning of a sudden warming? This chart answers the part of that question about the Brewer–Dobson circulation: whether the tropical lower stratosphere starts to rise faster (and so to cool) before the polar vortex breaks, or only once the planetary waves that break it are already arriving. It is a composite of the 28 major warmings in the MERRA-2 catalogue (1981–2026), each series in standard deviations of its own day-of-year variability and measured from that event’s level 60–90 days earlier. Each event is compared with dates that had the same westerly history: the same calendar days (±15) in other winters on which the wind at 60°N, 10 hPa had blown from the west for at least as long as it had before that event, outside the 20-day recovery period the warming definition imposes (3,000 random draws). A line is drawn in colour where the composite differs significantly from those dates after controlling the false discovery rate at 10 % over the 131 lags, and grey where it does not; the dashed line marks the first day of a significant run of at least five days.

The order is the textbook one. The 100 hPa eddy heat flux (the upward wave flux into the stratosphere) turns significant 15 days before the central date and peaks 3 days before; the 10 hPa polar cap starts warming 18 days before and peaks on the day; the wind at 60°N falls from 8 days before to its lowest 2 days after. The tropical cooling rate (the 10-day change of the 100–50 hPa layer temperature over 0–15°N from ERA5 heights; cooling means faster upwelling) turns significant 14 days before and peaks 2 days before, in step with the heat flux, and the tropical temperature itself is lowest about 13 days after the central date. Over all December–March days, not only events, the cooling rate correlates best with the heat flux at lags 0 to +5 days with the flux leading (r −0.51), and not at all with the tropics leading.

The circulation itself, by downward control (the same operator as the Brewer–Dobson item above, MERRA-2 GMI every 10th day 1991–2019, 16 events): tropical upwelling across 70 hPa is 3.1×109 kg/s (about a third) above normal in the last 10 days before the central date, the only significant 10-day window from day −60 on (leaving out any one event: 2.7–3.7). About 85 % of that is the drag of resolved planetary waves and 15 % orographic gravity-wave drag; the non-orographic drag does not change. In five CMIP6 models with the data, the monthly upwelling anomaly is nine times larger in the month of the warming than in the month before (+9 % against +1 %; all 5 agree on the first, 3 of 5 on the second).

So the upwelling surge is a response to the wave driving, not a precursor, which is what downward control predicts. Before the wave pulse nothing differs significantly from comparable dates. The vortex is not anomalously strong in the weeks before a warming: an earlier version of this chart, compared with any dates rather than ones with the same westerly history, showed a significantly strong vortex 3–7 weeks before and a warm tropical layer 2–4 weeks before, and neither survives the fairer comparison. On the plain anomaly the vortex is weak two to three months before a warming (−0.3 standard deviations, p 0.05) and near normal after that, and the tropical layer is cold throughout, because warming winters tend to have an easterly QBO at 50 hPa. A five-day warming of the tropical layer about six weeks before the central date does pass the test, but it was spotted on this chart rather than predicted, and it carries no forecast information. Tested on every eligible November–March day of 1980/81–2025/26 (logistic models fitted with one winter left out at a time, Brier skill against the seasonal climatology, significance from resampling whole winters): nothing — vortex strength, sixteen measures of vortex shape and stability, the tropical temperature or its 10-day trend, the heat flux or its 40-day mean, the QBO or ENSO — predicts a warming three to six weeks ahead better than the calendar does. Only the next two weeks are predictable: a weak vortex under a strong heat flux (Brier skill score +0.19, significant).

Holton and Tan (1980) found the winter vortex weaker when the equatorial winds of the quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO) blow from the east in the lower stratosphere. Left: the correlation between the October–November equatorial wind at each level (FU Berlin/KIT, Canton, Gan and Singapore soundings) and the zonal-mean wind at 60°N, 10 hPa in November–December and January–February, over 68 winters from 1958/59 (MERRA-2 from 1980, NCEP R1 before). Coloured bars pass a false-discovery rate of 10 % over the seven levels; grey ones do not. The relation is strongest at 30–50 hPa: r = +0.44 to +0.52 with the November–December vortex (p < 0.001) and +0.30 to +0.39 with January–February (p ≤ 0.012). It changes sign at 10 hPa, where the QBO phase is usually the opposite one. Middle: the 50 hPa relation winter by winter: each metre per second of westerly QBO adds about 0.27 m/s to the November–December vortex.

Whether a warming happens is a coarser question than how strong the vortex is, and the observations cannot settle it: 67 % of winters with an easterly 40 hPa QBO had a warming against 36 % with a westerly one (Fisher p 0.016), but that does not survive correction for having tried seven levels, and at the conventional 50 hPa it is 62 % against 44 % (p 0.21). Those shares are therefore not drawn. Right: five CMIP6 models that make a realistic QBO (UKESM1-0-LL, MRI-ESM2-0, CESM2-WACCM and -FV2, HadGEM3-GC31-LL; 50 hPa monthly spread at least 8 m/s against about 12 observed) do settle it: with an easterly October–November QBO at 50 hPa, 55 % of 5,700 winters had a December–March warming against 44 % of 9,833 westerly ones (odds ratio 1.53, Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel p ≈ 10−31, 4 of 5 models), and the effect holds within neutral, El Niño and La Niña winters alike (odds ratio 1.6–1.7). The models’ effect is smaller than the observed vortex relation suggests, consistent with their too-weak QBO below 30 hPa.

The QBO sets the odds for a whole winter; it says little about when. Once the vortex’s current state is known, the 50 hPa QBO adds only a little to the chance of a warming in the next 10–50 days (models: odds ratio 0.86–0.88 per standard deviation of westerly wind, p 0.01–0.03). The equatorial wind higher up, around the stratopause, shows no relation with warmings in the observations (1991–2025) and is not drawn.

The live Momentum budget item says, day by day, what is speeding up or slowing the vortex now. This item asks the same question of the past: every term of the same transformed-Eulerian-mean budget, with the same operator (the live product’s code), averaged over the major sudden warmings of 1980–2019 at 55–65°N and 50, 10 and 1 hPa: the observed change of the wind, the Coriolis force on the residual (Brewer–Dobson) flow $\hat f\bar v^*$, vertical advection $-\bar w^*\partial\bar u/\partial z$, the drag of the resolved planetary waves (the Eliassen–Palm flux divergence, also split into zonal wavenumbers 1, 2 and 3+), and the parameterized gravity-wave drag split into its orographic and non-orographic parts. Events: the 23 MERRA-2 catalogue warmings of 1980–2019 plus 29 February 1980 from NCEP R1, 24 in all (listed below); deepest = the top third (7 events) by the catalogue’s depth measure (the 100–150 hPa polar-cap height over the 30 days after).

How it is measured. The record is NASA’s MERRA-2 GMI replay (MERRA-2’s meteorology) at 00Z each day, 2° × 2.5°, with the model’s daily-mean gravity-wave drag. A single analysis per day is a weakness: the zonal-mean terms swing by several m/s per day within a day (tides and inertial oscillations), so single days scatter widely (the residual’s day-to-day spread is 2.2, 2.9 and 7.7 m/s per day at 50, 10 and 1 hPa), and always sampling 00Z biases the absolute Coriolis term (on ordinary winter days the terms leave a mean residual of +1.5, +0.3 and +1.7 m/s per day; the live item’s MERRA-2 normals, built from the same 00Z replay, show the same imbalance). Everything is therefore drawn as a 5-day mean departure from the same calendar days in other winters, sampled at the same hour, which cancels that bias; the departures do add up (before the central date the residual’s departure is small and not significant at every level: −0.07, −0.09 and +1.1 m/s per day at 50, 10 and 1 hPa over days −20 to −1; after it, −0.4 at 50 hPa and up to +2.4 at 1 hPa, where single 00Z snapshots are least representative). Colour marks where a departure is significant against 2,000 season-matched random dates (same calendar day ±10 days, another winter, p < 0.05); the box gives the mean over days −20 to −1 with its p. Leaving out any one event moves the window means of the larger terms by about 13 per cent (the median); the deepest events’ means at 10 hPa, which are not significant, move by more than their size. The replay has no analysis-increment tendency, so its residual includes the assimilation’s push.

What drives a warming. In the 20 days before the central date the resolved waves are the force: at 10 hPa their drag departs by −3.7 m/s per day from normal (p ≤ 0.001, the smallest the 2,000 random dates can give), the wind decelerates by −1.5 (p ≤ 0.001), and the Coriolis force on the circulation the waves drive pushes back by +2.4 (p ≤ 0.001). Wave 1 does most of it at 10 and 1 hPa. Over days −20 to +5 the Coriolis term offsets 71 % (90 % interval 58 %–81 %) of the extra wave drag at 10 hPa, 67 % (90 % interval 47 %–83 %) at 50 hPa and 72 % (90 % interval 59 %–82 %) at 1 hPa: this is the thermal-wind adjustment, the part of the wave forcing that goes into the residual circulation (and so into warming the polar cap and cooling the tropics) instead of into slowing the wind. In the deepest events the share at 50 hPa is 78 % (90 % interval 64 %–87 %). (Before the 25 October 2026 stability fix the 10 hPa share came out at 41 % and was undefined for the deepest events: the old stability was worst at that level.)

Does anything besides the waves and the Coriolis force matter at 50 hPa? No. Averaged over days −20 to +5, the departures there are, in size, 1.32 m/s per day for the resolved waves and 1.01 for the Coriolis term against 0.03 for vertical advection, 0.02 for gravity-wave drag and 0.28 for the residual (all events); in the 7 deepest events 2.09 and 1.76 against 0.04, 0.03 and 0.30. Vertical advection stays a few per cent of the wave drag in both sets (days −20 to −1: +0.01, p 0.44, all; −0.01, p 0.55, deepest). Its departure is significant only after the central date, and small there too: −0.06 m/s per day at 50 hPa and −0.13 at 10 hPa over days 0 to +20 (p ≤ 0.001 both), against −0.83 for the waves at 50 hPa (at 10 hPa the wave drag is back to normal by then, +0.26, not significant). Gravity-wave drag departs by no more than a few hundredths at 50 hPa; at 1 hPa it turns less westward after the reversal (+0.53, p ≤ 0.001), the filtering of eastward-propagating waves by the easterlies.

The analysis increment, which the replay lacks, is available for the 7 warmings since 2018 in GEOS FP (00Z states with the mean of four three-hourly tendency windows each day). It stays small through them: over days −20 to −1 it averages +0.01 m/s per day at 50 hPa and −0.03 at 10 hPa (neither different from zero, t-test across events; seven events are too few for a random-date null, so these are plain means), against a resolved-wave drag of −8.8 at 10 hPa; at 1 hPa it is −1.0, about its usual winter value (MERRA-2’s December–February mean is about −1.5), so it does not respond to the warmings either.

Age of stratospheric air

Open this figure

The age of stratospheric air is how long ago, on average, the air at a place was last at the ground. Air enters the stratosphere through the cold tropical tropopause, is lifted by the tropical branch of the Brewer–Dobson circulation, spreads poleward and sinks at high latitudes, while breaking waves mix old air back toward the tropics. Age therefore grows with height and toward the poles. It sums up the whole circulation in one number — a faster overturning makes younger air — and it is the quantity on which models and observations disagree (Waugh and Hall 2002).

The data. The MERRA-2 GMI replay (NASA GMAO): the GMI stratosphere–troposphere chemistry model driven by the MERRA-2 reanalysis winds and temperatures, 1980–2019, carrying a clock tracer released at the surface. Its mixing-ratio deficit is the mean age in days. Monthly means from NCCS, zonal means of 72 longitudes, 2° latitude, 250–0.1 hPa. The age is referenced to the surface; balloon and aircraft estimates are usually referenced to the tropical tropopause, about 0.3 years later.

Magnitudes. Annual mean at 50 hPa (~20.5 km): 1.7 years over the equator (10°S–10°N), 3.7 years at 35–55° in both hemispheres, 4.4–4.6 years over the poles; at 10 hPa (~31 km) 4.4–4.6 years at mid-latitudes. Observations: about 1 year over the equator at 20 km from ER-2 aircraft CO2 (tropopause-referenced), 4–5 years at high latitudes, and 5 ± 0.5 years in the mid-latitude middle stratosphere (Andrews et al. 2001; Waugh and Hall 2002). GMI sits at the young end of that range aloft and a little old over the equator at 20 km, as most models and reanalysis-driven transport do.

Record checks. No spin-up: the global-mean age anomaly at every level from 100 to 1 hPa is flat through 1980–1982 (slope < 0.05 years per year), so no years are dropped. What the record does show is a decline of about 0.5 years in the middle and upper stratosphere between the early 1990s and 2003, after which it is flat. The same drop appears in MERRA-2-driven age in another transport model (BASCOE; Chabrillat et al. 2018, a decrease of 0.8 years 1989–2015 at northern mid-latitudes, mostly before 2000). It coincides with changes in what MERRA-2 assimilates — a new production stream from January 1992, AMSU radiances from 1998, Aura MLS temperatures from 2004 — so it cannot be read as a clean climate trend. The step across each stream start (12 months after minus 12 before) is at most 0.2 years.

Trend. Over 1980–2019 the age decreases almost everywhere (1,502 of 1,547 cells significant), by 0.17 ± 0.05 years per decade at 30–50°N, 30–5 hPa — the latitudes and heights of the balloon record of Engel et al. (2017), which found +0.15 ± 0.18 years per decade for 1975–2016, not significant. This is the known disagreement: chemistry–climate models simulate air getting younger as greenhouse warming speeds the circulation, and the balloon record does not confirm it. Here the decline is the 1990s step; over 2004–2019 the balloon box shows no significant trend (+0.02 ± 0.09). Of the 105 cells (of 1,547) with a significant 2004–2019 trend, 85 show air getting older, by up to 0.16 years per decade, mostly in the northern extratropical lower stratosphere; 20 show a tiny decline (under 0.03 years per decade) in the southern subtropical lowermost stratosphere.

ENSO. El Niño makes the tropical lower-stratosphere air younger, as a faster Brewer–Dobson ascent should: −0.26 months per +1 °C of the relative Niño-3.4 index at 70 hPa (p = 0.002) and −0.35 at 50 hPa (p = 0.001), with the index one month earlier. The sign is the same at every lag from 0 to 8 months and significant at lags 0–2 and 7–8. Across the section 99 cells from 20°S to 22°N and 200–30 hPa are significantly younger, and 101 cells in the mid-latitude lowermost stratosphere (85–200 hPa) significantly older. The effect is small: a strong El Niño of +2 °C takes about half a month off air that is about ten months old at 70 hPa.

QBO. The quasi-biennial oscillation's secondary circulation sinks at the equator under westerly shear and rises in the subtropics. With westerlies at 50 hPa the equatorial air at 50 hPa is older by 0.52 months per standard deviation (7.5 m/s; p < 10−9), and 446 of 1,547 cells respond significantly to the 50 hPa index, 166 to the 30 hPa index.

Statistics.
