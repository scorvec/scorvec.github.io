How the page works

The page

Four global ensembles side by side, to day 15: 2 m temperature, 500 hPa height and sea-level pressure, each as the ensemble's mean anomaly, its members' spread and how the run moved from an earlier one. Two further views sort the members into weather regimes and into clusters.

Reading a map. Colour is the ensemble-mean anomaly. Dots mark where enough members agree on its sign to pass a test; colour without dots means the members are split, so read it as a hint and not a forecast. Hovering gives the number of members that agree.

When a run appears. About 5½ hours after its start for GEPS, 6½ for GEFS, 8 for AIFS-ENS and 8½ for IFS-ENS. A run is shown as it arrives: days 1–5 first, then days 6–10, then the rest with the change maps, regimes and clusters. IFS-ENS's 2 m temperature comes first from its 0.25° files and is replaced by the 0.1° one about 13½ hours after the start (11 for a 06 or 18 UTC run). The page keeps each ensemble's four newest runs; the buttons beside “Run” pick one.

Grids. Each ensemble is shown on its own published grid: finer grids are not smoothed and coarser ones are not interpolated before colouring. The 0.1° files carry no control member, which is why IFS-ENS has 50 members here. GEPS's 0.5° is the finest its source publishes.

The maps

Days and periods

Open this figure

  • A day is a calendar day in UTC: the mean of the 06, 12, 18 and 24 UTC fields.
  • A 00 UTC run has 15 full days, and day 1 is the day it starts. A 06, 12 or 18 UTC run has 14: day 1 is the next day, and its part-days at each end are left out. GEFS and GEPS run to 16 days and have one more. An IFS-ENS run from 06 or 18 UTC has 5.
  • The periods are days 1–5, 6–10 and 11–15 (11–14 for a 06, 12 or 18 UTC run), each the mean of its days.
  • A member missing any field in a period is left out of it. A period with fewer than 80 % of the members is not drawn.

Anomaly and the dots

Open this figure

Each member's mean less the 1991–2020 normal for the same days; the map is the mean of those anomalies.

The dots mark where at least k of the n members give the anomaly the same sign as their mean, with k the smallest number that passes a two-sided sign test against an even split at 5 %: 33 of 50 or 51, 22 of 31, 16 of 21.

The anomalies are the models' raw output against the reanalysis. They include the warming since 1991–2020 (in 500 hPa height, a positive anomaly across the tropics) and each model's own biases; no drift correction is applied to the maps.

The normals

Open this figure

  • 2 m temperature: ERA5-Land over land and ERA5 over the sea, monthly means at 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC so the normal samples the daily cycle as the forecast does, on the IFS's 0.1° grid. ERA5 is interpolated from its sea points only, so a coastal mountain does not chill the water beside it.
  • On the coarser grids that normal is averaged over each cell and moved by 6.5 °C per kilometre from the IFS's height to the model's own, so a valley or mountain cell is compared with a normal at its own height.
  • 500 hPa height and sea-level pressure: ERA5 at 1.5°, daily means of the four synoptic hours, interpolated to each grid.
  • Through the year each normal is a smooth curve (the mean and three annual harmonics, fitted so its month averages match the twelve monthly normals), read at each day.
  • High ground: the sea-level pressure anomaly is not drawn where the model's ground is above 1,500 m. Pressure “at sea level” there is an extrapolation that each model and the reanalysis make differently.

Standardized anomalies (σ)

Open this figure

For 500 hPa height and sea-level pressure the anomaly can be shown in standard deviations: the ensemble-mean anomaly divided by how much an anomaly over the same averaging period normally varies. A 5-day mean varies less than a single day, so a period is divided by the spread of 5-day means, not of days.

  • The standard deviations come from ERA5 at 1.5°, 1991–2020: each cell's anomalies from the normal curve, a linear trend removed, averaged over the period's length, their variance fitted as a smooth curve through the year.
  • Colours run to ±3σ. The dots use the same members-agree test as the anomaly.
  • Across the tropics 500 hPa height reads several standard deviations high: its day-to-day spread is small there and the warming since 1991–2020 is not.
  • The change maps stay in metres and hectopascals.

Change from an earlier run

Open this figure

This run's ensemble mean less that of the same ensemble's run 12 hours earlier, or of the run picked with the “vs … h earlier” buttons (6 to 48 hours earlier, whichever the ensemble ran; GEPS runs twice a day, so 12, 24, 36 and 48 only), over the same calendar days, on common 0.5° cells.

  • Dots mark a significant change: Welch's t-test on the two runs' members at every point, with the false discovery rate held at 10 % over the map, so at most a tenth of the dotted points are expected to be chance.
  • Shading without dots is a change within what the two runs' members could give by chance.
  • In the first days, where the ensembles are confident, even a small change can be significant: read the size from the colours.
  • The last period of a 00 UTC run is compared over days 11–14, the days the earlier run holds.

Spread

Open this figure

How far the members disagree: their standard deviation for each day or period, on the same 0.5° cells as the change maps. On the finer grids it is therefore the spread of 0.5° cell means. The super-ensemble has no spread maps.

The super-ensemble

Open this figure

The IFS-ENS, AIFS-ENS, GEFS and GEPS runs from the same start, pooled with equal weight per ensemble and not per member. Each ensemble counts a quarter, so AIFS-ENS's 51 members count as much as GEPS's 21; by member, the two ECMWF ensembles would carry two thirds of the vote.

  • Where: on 0.5° cells, on the days all four runs hold (15 from 00 UTC, 14 from 12 UTC), and only where every ensemble has enough complete members.
  • Anomaly: the mean of the four ensemble-mean anomalies, each against its own normal.
  • Dots: where the weighted share of members agreeing on the sign passes a two-sided sign test at 5 % on the weighted count (about 59 % with all 153 members).
  • Change: from the super-ensemble run 12, 24, 36 or 48 hours earlier, Welch's test on the weighted means, dotted at the same 10 % false discovery rate.
  • Runs: 00 and 12 UTC only, because GEPS has no 06 or 18 UTC run and IFS-ENS's reach day 5. It is made again when one of its four runs is.

Weather regimes

How a member gets its regime

Open this figure

Every member's 500 hPa height on every day is sorted into one of four recurring patterns, or into none, in two sectors: the Euro-Atlantic (20–80°N, 90°W–30°E) and North America and the Pacific (20–80°N, 170–30°W). They are the regimes of the extended-range page, classified the same way.

  • The patterns: four per sector and season from NCEP/NCAR reanalysis 1991–2020 (k-means on 5-day mean height anomalies), one set for October–March and one for April–September.
  • The label: a member's 5-day running mean anomaly goes to the nearest pattern, or to “no regime” when its pattern correlation with it is below 0.25.
  • Drift: none of these models has a reforecast, so each ensemble's drift is learned from its own past runs (the ensemble-mean anomaly at each lead less what the run started on that valid day showed on its first day) and removed once ten runs verify a lead, shrunk towards zero by n / (n + 10).

The timeline

Open this figure

The percentage of members in each regime on each valid date: first the four ensembles' mean, then each ensemble's most frequent regime with its share. A click on an ensemble's name gives all of its shares.

  • Pale or coloured: a share is pale unless it differs from that regime's 1991–2020 frequency within 15 days of the date, by a 95 % interval from resampling the members and the reanalysis years 1,000 times.
  • The four-ensemble rows average the ensembles' shares with equal weight per ensemble, and are pale unless the mean differs from the normal by more than 1.96 standard errors.
  • Every ensemble is on the same dates; each row names its run. An ensemble whose newest run is still arriving keeps the run before.

Scenarios and changes

Open this figure

  • Scenarios are the regimes that hold at least six members of the chosen ensemble on the day, or on a period's middle day. Each shows that regime's share in every ensemble, the normal as a tick, and the mean anomaly of just its members, dotted where enough of them agree on its sign (fewer than six members can never pass the test).
  • Since the previous run: each regime's share against the same ensemble's run 12 hours earlier, coloured where Fisher's exact test on the two runs' member counts is significant with the false discovery rate held at 10 %. Only the regimes that moved are drawn.

Clusters

How the groups are made

Open this figure

The members of the four ensembles' runs from the same start (the newest 00 or 12 UTC run all four have published) are pooled and grouped into the scenarios their spread supports.

  • When: days 3–5, 6–10 and 11–15, and each day on its own.
  • Where: the Euro-Atlantic, North America and the Pacific (the regimes' boxes, outlined on the maps), and the hemisphere north of 20°N.
  • What is compared: each member's 500 hPa height and sea-level pressure anomalies on a 2.5° grid, each divided by its typical 1991–2020 spread for the time of year. Each ensemble carries a quarter of the weight.
  • Grouping: the leading patterns of the members' differences from the four-ensemble mean (enough to explain 80 %) are grouped by weighted k-means (100 starts) into 1 to 5 clusters.

When more than one scenario is shown

Open this figure

A split is shown only when it passes all of these:

  • it cuts the spread by more than it does in 95 % of 100 synthetic ensembles with the same spread and no clusters;
  • every cluster holds at least 5 % of the weight;
  • resampling the members gives the same number of clusters at least 60 % of the time;
  • the split is not just the models differing from each other: a permutation test of ensemble against cluster (2,000 permutations) must pass at 5 %. Where it fails and two ensembles' means are at least 0.4 climatological standard deviation apart over the region (the root-mean-square of their difference, both fields), the page says the ensembles differ and shows each ensemble's own mean (“Disagree” in the strip of periods). With 150 members the test alone finds offsets too small to matter, so where the means are closer than that the period is one scenario.

Two ways. Most often the members form one spread and no split passes. The page then shows that spread's main difference when one pattern clearly leads it: the mean of the members on each side of the four-ensemble mean along their leading pattern, when its lead over the second pattern is larger than in 90 % of 1,000 synthetic ensembles. Where neither passes, the page shows one scenario, the four-ensemble mean.

Once a model's drift with lead is known (from ten verified 00 UTC runs per lead), the clustering and these tests run on the corrected fields.

Reading a scenario

Open this figure

  • Probability: the scenario's share of the weight, with each ensemble's own share beside it.
  • Nearest regime: the regime whose pattern correlates best with the scenario's mean 500 hPa anomaly (“no regime” below 0.25).
  • Shading: the anomaly, where the scenario's members agree on its sign. Lines are its mean height (every 6 dam) and pressure (every 4 hPa).
  • Dots: where the scenario's mean differs from the four-ensemble mean (500 random sets of as many members, false discovery rate 10 %).
  • Letters: a scenario keeps its letter while more than half of its members stay together from one day to the next; a new letter appears only when a scenario splits off. Windows are lettered among themselves, so a window's A and a day's A need not be the same scenario.
  • “Why this answer” shows how the members moved from the earlier period's groups, with the tests behind the answer and the runs pooled.

Under the hood

How the page works

Open this figure

Nothing on the page is a picture. Each model, field and period is shipped as the ensemble's own numbers (the mean anomaly to 0.25 °C, 4 m or 0.4 hPa, the number of members agreeing, and the ensemble mean) as a lossless image, and your browser colours, dots, contours and reads it. The 0.1° maps come in 36 tiles loaded as you zoom in; the world view draws every fifth point.
