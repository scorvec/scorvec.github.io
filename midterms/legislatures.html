State legislatures, 2026

← Congress and governors

Chances of control of twenty-two state legislative chambers.

Experimental. This part of the forecast is new and rests on less evidence than the Congress and governor forecasts. Tested on 2018 and 2022 with only what was known on October 1, on the fourteen original chambers (24 chamber elections): the control chances scored a Brier score of 0.115, against 0.250 for simply assuming the current majority holds; the winner was called right in 20 of 24 (the naive rule: 18), and the final seat count fell inside the 80% range in 23 of 24 (these figures move by a chamber or so between re-runs of the simulation, because two of the test chambers were near 50%). The Georgia, Iowa and Texas Houses were tested separately (5 chamber elections with complete district data; Georgia 2018 lacked two districts): all five called right, as the naive rule also did, Brier 0.006, seat counts inside the 80% range 5 of 5 - a test with no close chamber in it. The California Senate and Assembly, the Kentucky Senate and House and the Texas Senate, added later, were tested the same way (10 chamber elections): all called right, as the naive rule also did, seat counts inside the 80% range 8 of 10 (the 2018 California Assembly came in 6 seats more Republican than expected, the 2018 Kentucky House 12 seats more Democratic). Known gaps:
  • New Hampshire House (400 seats): no candidate lists are available, so every seat is treated as contested by full slates of both parties, and floterial districts are simulated as ordinary multi-member districts; it was not part of the 2018 test.
  • Michigan Senate: no 2024 Senate election, and the 2026 court-ordered redraw of the Detroit-area districts is not yet in the Census boundary files; district leans are 2020 results moved by the 2024 swing, on the 2022 lines.
  • Minnesota Senate (and part of the New Hampshire Senate): no candidate lists; sitting senators are assumed to run unless they have announced their retirement.
  • Iowa House: candidates are described in prose; retirements are read from it, but most nominees are not, so a seat counts as contested unless the page says a party has no candidate.
  • Georgia House: nominees come from the Georgia Secretary of State's official results of the May 19 primary and the June 16 runoff; a party with no primary candidate in a district has no nominee (40 seats have only a Democrat, 21 only a Republican). A nominee who withdrew after the primary would not show.
  • California: the top two primary finishers meet in November regardless of party; a seat with two candidates of the same party is decided before the general election.
  • Texas House: a seat is fixed as one-party only when the chamber page's general-election listing lacks the other party.

District map

Shade

Hover or tap a district for its forecast; scroll or pinch to zoom. Shading is the chance a Democrat wins the seat (for districts electing several members, the expected Democratic share of them). District boundaries: U.S. Census Bureau TIGER/Line Shapefiles 2025 (state legislative districts), simplified; New Hampshire floterial districts are drawn as the base districts that vote in them.

How it works: each seat's expected margin is the district's 2024 presidential lean (precinct returns joined to legislative districts), the national environment from the generic ballot and incumbency, with errors that move every seat in a state together and with the national mood. (The state's own Senate and governor polls were also tested as a signal; they did not improve the backtest and are not used.) Multi-member districts elect their top candidates.
