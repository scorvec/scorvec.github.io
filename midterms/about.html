How the forecast works

The forecast simulates the 2026 House, Senate and governor elections 20,000 times. Each simulation draws a national polling miss shared by every race, regional and demographic swings shared by similar places, and each race's own uncertainty, so close races tend to break together, as they do in real elections.

The national mood slider

What the national mood is. It is our estimate of how much more voters across the country prefer one party's House candidates over the other's today. It starts from the generic-ballot polling average (pollsters ask "would you vote for the Democratic or the Republican candidate for Congress in your district?"), with each pollster's lean, the gaps between likely voters, registered voters and adults, and sponsored polls corrected. The model takes that average as it stands: it does not assume which way the polls will miss.

What the slider does not mean. Setting it to Democrats +8 does not simulate elections in which Democrats win the national vote by 8. It says "suppose the country's true mood right now is Democrats +8" and then asks what could happen by Election Day. Each of the 20,000 simulated elections draws its own national miss around that setting (in the House, a standard deviation of 4.7 points: the 4.3-point typical miss of the generic-ballot average at this distance from Election Day, in either direction, plus about 2 points for the year-to-year wobble in how the national vote turns into margins in contested seats; it covers both polling error and opinion moving before November). Senate and governor races share a separate statewide polling miss of 4.2 points (again in either direction: the size of the cycle-wide Senate polling miss in 2018 to 2024), which in past elections moved with the national miss only loosely (a correlation of about 0.3). So the national result in the simulations ranges well above and below the slider, and the chances shown include that spread.

Votes already cast. Early and mail ballots are not counted until Election Day, so they say nothing about who is ahead, and the model does not read anything into who has voted so far. What a ballot already cast does change is how much a late swing can move the result: opinion that shifts next week cannot change a vote cast last week. Part of the national error above is exactly that kind of late movement (about 1.9 points at this distance, measured from how much the generic-ballot average moved in the final weeks of past elections). Each state's share of that part is scaled by how much of its vote will already be in when a late shift happens, compared with past elections, which also had early voting: states that vote almost entirely by mail before Election Day (for example Washington, Oregon and Colorado) are slightly less exposed to a late swing, and states that vote mostly on the day (for example Mississippi and New Hampshire) slightly more. The scaling uses each state's 2022 share of ballots cast before Election Day and its 2026 count so far, and it changes the chances shown by well under a point.

How it moves the races. Moving the slider shifts every simulated race at once: House margins move one point for each point on the slider, Senate races by 0.8 points (fitted on 326 Senate races from 2006 to 2024), and governor races by about half a point. The rest of each race, its polls, lean and candidates, stays fixed, so the slider answers "what if the national mood today were different?".

Why there is no correction for the direction of the miss. Generic-ballot averages have usually overstated Democrats: in 13 of the 15 House elections from 1996 to 2024, by about three points a month out. But most of that record comes from older averages built differently from ours. Rebuilt with our own method for the seven elections with poll-by-poll data (2012 to 2024), the average overstated Democrats in 5 of 7 a month out, the size varied from nothing to five points, 2012 and 2018 went the other way, and three midterms are too few to tell whether midterm years differ. So the model does not assume a direction. The size of past misses, in either direction, sets the width of the national error instead: about 3.4 points on Election Day, 4.3 a month out and 5.1 four months out.

How the poll trend is built

The line on the poll charts is fitted to every individual poll, not to other people's averages. Each pollster's house effect (how far it runs from the field) is estimated and removed, as are the offsets between likely voters, registered voters and all adults. Polls fielded over several days are dated at their midpoint, and several versions of one survey (for example likely and registered voters) share one poll's weight.

The shaded band is the 95% range for where the polling average stands today: sampling error plus uncertainty in the house effects. It is not an Election Day range. Polls can miss the result by much more, and the model carries that separately: 4.3 points, one standard deviation at this distance from Election Day, for the national vote.

House

Every district is on its 2026 map, including the mid-decade redraws. A district's expected margin starts from its partisan lean, incumbency and the candidates' fundraising, then blends in district polls in proportion to how informative they are. Polls from campaigns and party committees are corrected for their typical lean, and polls that disagree with each other count for less than their number suggests. On the map, outlined districts are open seats.

Senate

Senate races use the same approach with a prior refitted on every Senate race since 2006. Only polls naming the candidates actually on the ballot are used; three-way races use polls of the full field. Where an independent is the main challenger (for example in Nebraska), the chance shown is the independent's, and an independent win is not counted as a Democratic seat. Control is counted the way it is settled, by who elects the president pro tempore: a party needs more senators voting for it than the other side, and a 50–50 tie goes to the Republicans because Vice President Vance breaks it. So 51 Democratic seats is control, 50 is not, and 50 Democrats, 49 Republicans and one independent is control only if the independent does not side with the Republicans. The headline assumes independent winners stay out, as Dan Osborn (Nebraska) has said he would; the line underneath gives the answer if every independent winner sides with either party. This matches how betting markets such as Kalshi settle Senate control.

Ranked-choice races

Six 2026 races are decided by a ranked-choice count: in Maine the Senate race and both House seats (Maine's ranked-choice law covers federal general elections and primaries; the state constitution requires a plurality for governor, so Maine's governor race is an ordinary count), and in Alaska the Senate race, the at-large House seat and the governor race (top-four primary and a ranked-choice general election, adopted in 2020). If no candidate has a majority, the last-placed candidate is eliminated and those ballots move to their next choice, until two remain. The model's margin in these races is the expected final-round margin.

Polls. When a poll reports its own final round (the head-to-head after its simulated count, or a question naming only the two finalists) that number is used as the poll, and not averaged with the same poll's first round. When a poll reports only first choices, the other candidates' shares are moved to the finalists at the rates measured in past counts. Maine's three 2026 races have only two names on the ballot, so nothing transfers there.

Transfer rates. From the official round-by-round tabulations of every ranked-choice general election in Maine and Alaska from 2018 to 2024 whose final round was a Republican against a Democrat or an independent: 13 counts (Maine's 2nd district 2018 and 2022; Alaska's House seat in the August 2022 special, November 2022 and 2024; eight Alaska legislative races), 16 eliminated candidates, each followed to the final round. A Republican-leaning candidate's ballots (Republican, Alaskan Independence, Constitution; 10 cases) went 49 % to the Republican finalist, 12 % to the other finalist and 39 % nowhere (no further choice); a Democrat's (2 cases) 54 % to the Democratic finalist and 12 % to the Republican; an independent's or Libertarian's (4 cases) split 35 % to 29 % for the non-Republican finalist. The model uses one partisan loyalty, a net 38 % of an eliminated candidate's first-round share toward its own side (12 cases, standard deviation 18 % from one count to the next), and a net 6 % (standard deviation 26 %) toward the non-Republican for independents. That count-to-count spread is carried into each race as extra polling uncertainty, in proportion to the share that has to be transferred.

Tested before use. Each count was predicted from its official first round with rates fitted on the other counts only. Transfers missed the final margin by 2.5 points on average, against 5.9 points for the first-round margin between the two finalists (better in 10 of 13 counts) and 8.4 points for adding up each party's first-round vote, the rule this model used for Alaska's governor race before (better in 12 of 13); the stated uncertainty matched the misses (root-mean-square standardised error 1.05). On the final month of polls in the five federal races (10 polls), the transfer handling missed by 3.4 points against 3.7 for averaging each poll's rounds together and 5.3 for party sums: in the same direction, but five races cannot settle it on their own. In Alaska's August 2022 special election the last poll's own count put the wrong Republican in the final round, which no transfer rule can fix.

Alaska's House seat. The likely final round is the Republican, Nick Begich, against an independent, Bill Hill; the Democrat polls near 10 %. The House model scores Democrats against Republicans only, so this seat stays on its fundamentals and the panel says so.
