What if? Change the forecast's assumptions

The forecast  How it works

A forecast is a set of assumptions plus the polls. Move a slider and every race is simulated again in your browser, 5,000 times, to show how much each assumption matters. The starting position is the model's own forecast; nothing here changes it. It is meant for exploring, not as a prediction.

…chance Democrats win the House
…chance Democrats control the Senate
…chance Democrats win both
…chance of a Democratic majority of governors

The national mood

How the country would vote for the House if the election were today. It moves every race: House seats one for one, Senate and governor races less.

Republicans +6Democrats +16

Which way the polls miss

The model assumes state and district polls are as likely to miss toward either party. Here you can assume a direction. The marks show how far Senate and governor polls missed in past elections.

polls overstate Republicanspolls overstate Democrats

How big polling misses are

The size of every error in the simulation, from the national mood to each race. Smaller errors make favourites safer; larger ones give underdogs more chances.

half as bigtwice as big

Do races move together?

Polls tend to miss in the same direction across many races at once. If misses were independent, they would cancel out and control would be nearly certain. If all races missed together, one bad night for the polls would decide everything.

each race on its ownall races together

Polls or fundamentals?

Each race blends its polls with a "fundamentals" estimate from the state's lean, incumbency and the national mood. Turn the polls down to see the fundamentals alone, or up to lean on the polls harder.

fundamentals onlypolls count 4x

Trust the best pollsters more

Give more weight to pollsters whose past polls missed less than others in the same races (Selzer, Siena, Emerson...) and less to those that missed more. In testing on 2018-2024 this did not improve the forecast, so the model weights established pollsters equally.

all equalstrongly by track record

Pick the winners yourself

Call any race for either party and the chances above show only the simulations in which your calls come true: what has to happen elsewhere if, say, Texas goes Republican. The bars show each race's chance with your assumptions; the black mark is the model's.

How this works: the page uses the same race means, error sizes and correlations as the forecast, and the same random draws every time, so a change in the numbers comes only from the assumption you moved. When a slider changes how polls count, each race's polls and fundamentals are re-blended with a simple precision-weighted average, which matches the model's own blend to about a tenth of a point. Senate control follows the forecast's rule: Democrats need more seats than the Republicans (the Vice President breaks a 50-50 tie), and independent winners are not counted for either side.
