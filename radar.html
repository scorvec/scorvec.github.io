US Radar Explorer

The national NEXRAD radar picture, refreshed every two minutes: base reflectivity, the lowest scan of every WSR-88D in the lower 48, merged into one map by NOAA's Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor system (MRMS). Your browser reads NOAA's own data files and decodes them, so the hover readout is MRMS's number. Lightning flashes seen from space by the GOES satellites are drawn on top. Zoom from the whole country to a single city, loop the last hours at the speed you like, and hover or tap for the reflectivity in dBZ or a flash's time. Colours follow the traditional NWS reflectivity scale; the short-range model maps are one click away for comparing with the HRRR.

About this radar map: data, decoding, timing and limits

What the map shows

MRMS merged base reflectivity (MergedBaseReflectivityQC) from NOAA’s Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor system, run by NOAA/NSSL and the National Weather Service: the lowest elevation scan of every WSR-88D (0.5° on most radars) and of the Canadian radars near the border, quality-controlled to remove ground clutter, anomalous propagation, birds and other non-weather echo, and merged onto a 0.01° latitude–longitude grid (about 1.1 km north–south, 0.7–1.0 km east–west) from 130°W to 60°W and 20°N to 55°N. A new mosaic is made about every two minutes. Where radars overlap, NSSL describes the mosaic as taking the lowest-altitude observation where there is precipitation and otherwise a weighted mean of the radars, weighted by distance from each radar and the height of the beam; it is not the strongest value of the column (that product, MRMS’s composite reflectivity, is not used here). Grid cells no radar can see are marked as having no coverage, separately from cells with no echo, and the page hatches them faintly (Map options: “Hatch no coverage”).

How your browser reads it. Each mosaic is one gzipped GRIB2 file on NOAA’s Open Data Dissemination bucket on AWS (noaa-mrms-pds), 0.45–1.8 MB depending on how much rain there is. The page lists the bucket for the file times, downloads each file once, and decodes it itself in a background worker: it unzips it, reads the grid from section 3 of the GRIB2 message (template 3.0, 7000 × 3500 points, first point 54.995°N, 129.995°W) and the scaling from section 5 (template 5.41: the values are packed as a 16-bit greyscale PNG image, value = (R + X · 2E) / 10D, with R = −9990, E = 0, D = 1 so far), then inflates and unfilters the PNG row by row. −999 means no coverage and −99 no echo; reflectivity comes in 0.5 dBZ steps and is kept exactly. If a file ever arrives in a layout the page does not expect (another grid or packing template, a bitmap, several fields), the page says so and switches to the IEM mosaic rather than guessing.

Checked on 9 October 2026 against eccodes (the ECMWF GRIB library) reading the same four files, from the bucket’s first day (14 October 2020) to today, including the outbreaks of 7 May 2024 and 14–15 March 2025: the grid, corners and spacing, the number of no-coverage, no-echo and echo points (for example 8,192,739, 15,294,167 and 1,013,094 at 15:06 UTC on 9 October 2026) and all 24.5 million values (histogram and a position-weighted checksum of the whole grid, plus 801 single points per file) are identical. Every file since October 2020 uses the same grid and packing.

Why not the IEM mosaic

Until 9 October 2026 this page drew the N0Q national composite of the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (IEM): also each radar’s lowest scan, merged onto a 0.005° grid every five minutes. IEM does not document how it combines overlapping radars; the result looks like the strongest value wins, and it has one code for both no echo and no coverage. Measured on the page’s own decodes at three times (7 May 2024 03:00 UTC, 15 March 2025 00:30 UTC and 9 October 2026 15:00 UTC; lower 48, 126–66°W, 24–50°N, IEM taken as the maximum of the four 0.005° cells in each 0.01° cell), where both show 20 dBZ or more IEM’s N0Q read a median 5.5–7 dBZ higher than MRMS base reflectivity, and 3.5–4.5 dBZ higher than even MRMS’s column-maximum composite at the same minute; it covered 3.4–4.9 times MRMS base’s area of 35 dBZ or more and, on the two storm days, 6.5 and 9 times its area of 50 dBZ or more (2.6–7 times even the composite’s). That is why the old map looked like composite reflectivity. These are three times, not a climatology. The IEM mosaic stays as a choice under Map options (“IEM N0Q mosaic”) and as the automatic fallback; its pixel values are decoded as k × 0.5 − 32.5 dBZ, checked against IEM’s documentation and its 5-dBZ N0R composite.

Colours

The colour table is the traditional NWS base-reflectivity scale in 5 dBZ steps: cyan and blues from 5 to 19 dBZ, greens 20–34, yellow and orange 35–49, reds 50–64, magenta and purple 65–74, white from 75. The model maps draw HRRR and RRFS simulated reflectivity with their own rain ramp and colour snow and ice by the model's surface type; radar reflectivity carries no precipitation type, so every echo here uses the one NWS scale. The model maps show simulated reflectivity 1 km above the ground; the radar's lowest scan rises with distance from each radar (at 0.5°, about 0.7 km above the radar at 60 km range and 3 km at 160 km), so far from every radar the mosaic sees higher in the cloud and can overshoot shallow snow and drizzle; MRMS's merging favours the lowest beam wherever radars overlap.

Loops, speed, resolution and your connection

Opening the page loads one file listing (about 25 KB) and one frame (the newest, about 1 MB on a quiet day). A loop is downloaded only when you play, step or drag the slider: 2 hours every 10 minutes is 12–13 files, the file nearest each 10-minute mark, about 13 MB on a quiet day and up to about 23 MB on a stormy one (the IEM mosaic: about 26–32 MB). They are fetched two at a time, newest first, each requested once and kept in memory for the visit. The loop menu also offers every file of the last hour (about 30 frames two minutes apart, about 30 MB). The speed control next to the time slider plays the loop at ½×, 1×, 2× or 4× (1× is 2.4 frames a second; the keys [ and ] change it), and “Hold last frame” sets how long the newest frame stays before the loop starts again; both are kept in the address.

Measured on 9 October 2026 (headless Chrome, browser cache off, lightning off): on a desktop connection the first frame was on screen 0.6 s after the page started (IEM: 1.2 s) and the whole 2-hour loop in 3 s (IEM: 6 s); on a simulated phone (9 Mbit/s, 100 ms latency, processor slowed four times) 2.1 s (IEM: 2.8 s) and 13 s (IEM: 23 s). Decoding one MRMS file takes about 0.17 s on a laptop.

Loop frames are kept as a 0.02° grid (the strongest value in each 2 × 2 block, so cores are not lost; 0.03° on phones and devices with 4 GB of memory or less), which is finer than a screen shows at the national view; when you stop on a frame zoomed in past that, the page decodes the full 0.01° grid of that frame from the copy already in memory and draws it at native resolution. The hover readout uses the full grid once it is decoded (otherwise it says the value is the block maximum).

Timing and freshness

The time shown is the mosaic's own time, from the file name (UTC, and your local time); it matches the reference time inside the file. MRMS files reach the bucket about a minute after their time. While this tab is visible the page lists the bucket for new files about every two minutes, just after each new file is due (a few hundred bytes when nothing is new) and adds the newest frame; nothing is downloaded while the tab is hidden. The time chip turns amber (“late”) when the newest file is more than 8 minutes old and red (“stale”) past 20 minutes (12 and 25 for the five-minute IEM mosaic). A loop mark with no file within half a step of it is marked in red under the slider and skipped by the loop. A time in the address (#t=YYYYMMDDHHMM, UTC) opens the loop ending at the last file at or before it, back to 14 October 2020 (the first MRMS file in the bucket); earlier times, back to November 2010, come from the IEM mosaic. The model maps link here that way.

Lightning

The dots are lightning flashes from the Geostationary Lightning Mapper (GLM) on GOES-19 (GOES-East, over 75.2°W) and GOES-18 (GOES-West, over 137.0°W). GLM is an optical sensor: it sees the light of lightning at the cloud top, in-cloud and cloud-to-ground alike, day and night, and NOAA’s Level 2 product groups its 2-millisecond detections into flashes, one file every 20 seconds per satellite. For the frame on screen the map shows the flashes whose first light came in the 5, 10 or 15 minutes before the frame’s time (10 by default, 5 on phones and with Save-Data), coloured by 5-minute age, the newest on top. The chip at the bottom right counts the flashes inside the map. Hover or tap a flash for its time to the second, its optical energy in femtojoules (the light that reached the satellite, not the electrical energy of the flash) and the satellite.

Two satellites, one flash. Both satellites see the central US, so every flash there is reported twice. Each flash is taken only from the satellite whose sub-satellite point is nearer in longitude: GOES-East east of 106.1°W, GOES-West west of it (the meridian halfway between the two). The nearer satellite looks at the storm more steeply, so its position error is the smaller. A satellite’s files are downloaded only when the view reaches its side of that line (with a 1.5° margin); if one satellite’s listing fails, the other covers the whole view and the chip names the satellite. Archive times use the satellite operational then (GOES-16 as East before 7 April 2025, GOES-17 as West from February 2019 to January 2023); GLM data begin in December 2017.

Quality and position. Only flashes with flash_quality_flag = 0 (good) are drawn. The product marks a few percent as degraded — mostly the largest and longest flashes, which the algorithm splits at its event-count and 3-second limits, and flashes whose events arrive out of time order — and those are left out rather than drawn as if they were complete flashes. A flash’s position is the energy-weighted centre of its events, navigated to an assumed cloud-top height: GLM pixels are about 8 km across under the satellite and 10–14 km over the US, and storms much taller or shallower than the assumed height appear shifted by a few kilometres (away from the sub-satellite point for tall storms). At these latitudes the offset is small against the pixel size, but a flash can sit a few kilometres from the radar core it belongs to: read the dots at storm scale, not cell by cell. GLM does not see every flash: it misses more in the brightest daytime storms and toward the edge of each satellite’s view.

How it is read. Your browser lists NOAA’s GLM-L2-LCFA files on the AWS Open Data buckets (noaa-goes19, noaa-goes18) and reads each netCDF-4 file with HTTP range requests: only the flash variables (count, time of the first event, latitude, longitude, energy and quality flag), 1–10 KB of a 170–650 KB file. Once per satellite per visit the first file is parsed with jsfive, an open-source HDF5 reader in JavaScript, to learn where those variables sit and how they are scaled (scale_factor, add_offset, _FillValue, _Unsigned); the same file is then decoded a second time by the range reader and the two must agree value for value. Every later file is checked against the learned layout (index signatures, chunk sizes, value ranges, the file’s start time) and the layout is learned again if anything differs. Checked on 9 October 2026 against netCDF4 and xarray reads of the same GOES-18 and GOES-19 files (69 to 789 flashes each): identical flash counts, latitudes, longitudes and quality flags, energies equal to float32 precision and times within 0.002 ms.

Your connection. A 10-minute window over the whole country is 64 files (32 per satellite), roughly 250–500 KB in about 470 small requests, taking 3–6 seconds; the first window of a visit adds about 0.3 MB to learn the layout. Zoomed in on one satellite’s side it is half that. Lightning is fetched only for frames that are shown, newest files first; it never holds up the radar or the loop (a playing loop shows each frame’s lightning once it has arrived), and it pauses at 40 MB per visit (15 MB on phones, 6 MB with Save-Data) until you choose to continue.

If a source is down

If NOAA's bucket cannot be listed or a file cannot be decoded, the page switches to the IEM N0Q mosaic and says why. If IEM is down too, it falls back to NOAA's rendered base-reflectivity images (mapservices.weather.noaa.gov, the last four hours). Those arrive already coloured in NOAA's own scale (shown as received, not recoloured) and carry no dBZ values, so there is no readout and no floor in that mode. An automatic fallback is not written into the address, so a reload tries MRMS again.
