    How east-based will it be?

    El Niños differ in where the warmth sits. East-based events, like 1982–83 and 1997–98, warm the eastern Pacific all the way to the coast of South America. Central-Pacific events, like 2009–10, keep the warmth near the date line. The two index numbers below, E for the eastern Pacific and C for the central Pacific (Takahashi and others, 2011), measure each in standard deviations of their normal variability, so the same scale places this forecast among every El Niño since 1950.

    Method and sources

    The forecasts

    The Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) collects seasonal forecasts from ECMWF (SEAS5), the UK Met Office (GloSea6), Météo-France (System 9), DWD (GCFS2), NCEP (CFSv2), ECCC (CanSIPS, whose two component models are pooled into one) and the Australian Bureau of Meteorology (ACCESS-S2). Each model’s anomaly is its forecast minus its own 1993–2016 hindcast average for the same start month and lead, which removes each model’s drift and bias before anything is compared. The issue shown is the newest month for which at least five models have published.

    The multi-model average weights each model equally, and so do the shaded spreads and every probability on this page: a model with 124 members counts the same as one with 40.

    ONI and RONI

    ONI is the three-month running mean of the Niño-3.4 anomaly (5°N–5°S, 170–120°W). RONI is the Niño-3.4 anomaly minus the 20°S–20°N average anomaly, rescaled month by month so it keeps ONI’s thresholds. NOAA CPC made RONI its official ENSO index in February 2026. The observed values are CPC’s published tables; CPC revises them for up to two months, and months after its latest value are estimated from NOAA OISST daily data, which is itself preliminary for about two weeks. Those months are drawn with open markers. The models’ anomalies are on a 1993–2016 base and CPC’s on 1991–2020 (ONI uses shifting 30-year bases), so the two can differ by a tenth of a degree.

    East or central: the E and C indices

    Following Takahashi, Montecinos, Goubanova and Dewitte (2011, Geophysical Research Letters 38, L10704): the two leading patterns (EOFs) of monthly sea-surface temperature anomalies over 10°S–10°N, 140°E–80°W, from NOAA ERSSTv6 on a fixed 1991–2020 base, trained on 1979–2020. The first is a basin-wide warming (66% of the variance), the second an east–west seesaw (14%). Rotating them by 45° gives E = (PC1 − PC2)/√2 and C = (PC1 + PC2)/√2, each in standard deviations. Every month since 1950 is projected on the same two patterns.

    Each model member’s forecast anomaly field (C3S’s own anomaly product, same hindcast base as above) is moved onto the 1991–2020 base using the observed difference between the two climatologies (a few hundredths of a degree) and projected on the observed patterns. On this page an event or a member is east-based when E exceeds C by at least 1, central when C exceeds E by at least 1, and basin-wide in between. With that rule 1982–83 and 1997–98 are east-based; 1972–73, 1991–92, 2015–16 and 2023–24 basin-wide; 2002–03, 2004–05 and 2009–10 central.

    The centre of the warming is the anomaly-weighted mean longitude of the stretch of the equator (2°S–2°N, smoothed over 10° of longitude) that is within 30% of the warmest point. A bare maximum jumps between two near-equal humps: December–February 2023–24 reads 102°W by its maximum but 131°W this way.

    How the verdict is decided. A class is named only when three things hold: the models, counted once each, agree on the direction of E minus C (one-sample t-test against zero, p < 0.05); at least two-thirds of the members fall in the class; and all models but at most one have their average in it. A significant lean that misses the other two tests is reported as a lean, and anything else as a split. How well the models forecast the east–central balance has not been verified here.

    Caveat. On the fixed base, the western Pacific’s faster warming puts a slow upward drift into C (+0.16 standard deviations per decade since 1950) and very little into E (+0.03), so events from the 1950s to 1970s sit a little lower on C than they would against their own era’s climate.
