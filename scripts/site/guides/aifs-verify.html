The page

The question: as deterministic forecasts, how does ECMWF's AIFS single compare with member 0 of AIFS-ENS? The two are trained differently. The single is trained to minimise squared error, which rewards hedging: it blurs what it is unsure about as lead time grows. The ensemble members are trained on a CRPS-type objective that keeps each run sharp. RMSE and anomaly correlation reward smoothing, so a raw comparison would partly measure blur rather than forecast quality. Here member 0 is first passed through a spectral filter that gives it the single's power spectrum at each lead, then both are truncated at T120 and scored against independent radiosondes from the sounding explorer, with ERA5 alongside. Member 0 as published and the ensemble mean are shown as references; the ensemble mean averages 26 of the 51 members (the control and 25 perturbed), so it is not a single model run.

Why the matching. The AIFS single minimises squared error, so as its forecast loses predictability it damps the features it cannot place; that lowers RMSE whether or not the forecast is better. Member 0 of AIFS-ENS is trained to stay sharp. To compare them as deterministic forecasts on equal terms, member 0's spherical-harmonic coefficients are multiplied, degree by degree, by f(l) = min(1, √(Psingle(l) / Pmember 0(l))), where P are the two models' mean power spectra at the same lead over the previous 40 runs (a 5-degree running mean of the ratio; the global mean, l = 0, is left alone). The filter uses only past forecasts, never the verifying data, so it is a transformation the forecaster could apply in real time. It only removes power (f ≤ 1). The spectra panel shows how closely the filtered member 0 reproduces the single. The filter matches the average spectrum; it cannot copy the single's flow-dependent damping (smoothing more where the flow is less predictable), so part of what remains may still be hedging done well rather than a better-placed forecast.

Same scale. Every 0.25° global field is expanded in spherical harmonics (pyshtools) and all series are truncated at T120 (about 1.5°) before scoring, so no model is judged at scales it does not share with the others. A 1.5° block mean would still pass much of the 300–600 km power.

Independent truth. ERA5 is an IFS analysis that both models were trained on. The primary truth is the radiosonde network the sounding explorer mirrors every hour: 500 hPa height and 850 hPa temperature are interpolated in log-pressure from each station's sounding, and the forecasts are sampled at the stations by bilinear interpolation. ERA5 scores (NH 20–80°N, cosine-weighted, 1.5°) come from the same truncated fields and are the only truth for MSLP and 2 m temperature. Radiosonde scores arrive within hours; ERA5 lags about 6 days, and its latest 2–3 months are the preliminary ERA5T release.

The ensemble mean. The mean of the control and 25 perturbed AIFS-ENS members is shown for reference. Averaging removes the unpredictable part of the forecast, so it usually wins on RMSE at longer leads while having much less variance than the atmosphere; it is not a single model run and is not the page's question.

Scores

Scorecard

RMSE, bias, anomaly correlation and activity ratio at leads 24–240 h, per run. Station anomalies use each station's own day-of-year median from its full IGRA record; ERA5 anomalies use the ERA5 1991–2020 ±7-day climatology (WeatherBench2), including 850 hPa temperature since September 2026. Differences from the single are paired by start time and tested with a t-test whose standard error allows for serial correlation: it is the larger of a circular-block bootstrap's (2,000 resamples, block length chosen from each cell's own autocorrelation, never under four runs or two days) and the one implied by the series' lag-1 autocorrelation (its effective number of independent runs). Differences in anomaly correlation drift with the season and stay correlated for a week or more, so they count as far fewer independent runs than RMSE differences. The scorecard is one family of 480 tests (eight rows, ten leads, three comparisons, RMSE and anomaly correlation): a cell is called significant only when its p is below 0.05 and it passes the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery control at 10 % across all 480, so at most about one in ten solid cells is expected to be a false alarm. The 95 % interval in a cell's tooltip is for that cell alone. A difference that wanders over the whole archive (a seasonal drift as long as the record) cannot be tested from one summer and autumn, and these tests do not claim to.

Scores by lead

Each point is the mean over the same verified runs for all four models (a run enters a lead only when all four have it), so the lines are comparable point by point. The set of runs differs from lead to lead and between the two truths: the newest runs cannot yet be verified at long leads, and ERA5 arrives later than the radiosondes. Station anomalies are against each radiosonde station's own day-of-year median; ERA5 anomalies against the ERA5 1991–2020 climatology. The activity ratio is std(forecast anomaly) / std(observed anomaly): a model can buy RMSE by damping variance, and this ratio shows the purchase. Whether two lines differ significantly is in the scorecard.

Power spectra

Spherical-harmonic power of each model's global field against total wavenumber, averaged over the archived runs and shown as a ratio to the AIFS single at the same lead. Member 0 as published sits above 1 wherever the single has smoothed small scales away; the matched member 0 should lie on 1 — that is what the filter is for. The dotted line is the T120 truncation applied before scoring; nothing to its right enters any score.
