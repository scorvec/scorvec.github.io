The page

The page uses five of the seasonal forecast systems that the Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) collects: ECMWF (SEAS5), the UK Met Office (GloSea6), Météo-France (System 9), NCEP (CFSv2) and ECCC (CanSIPS, whose two component models are pooled into one). DWD (GCFS2) is left out of everything on this page (excluded for poor ENSO skill and drift). The Australian Bureau of Meteorology’s ACCESS-S2 is not used anywhere on the site, for the same reason. Each model’s anomaly is its forecast minus its own 1993–2016 hindcast average for the same start month and lead, which removes each model’s drift and bias before anything is compared. The issue shown is the newest month for which at least four of the systems shown have published.

The multi-model average weights each model equally, and so do the shaded spreads and every probability on this page: NCEP’s CFSv2, with about 120 members, counts the same as CanSIPS with 40.

The figures

The outlook in brief

The odds in the brief are the share of members whose own peak (the highest of the four three-month averages lying wholly inside the six months) reaches each level, each model weighted equally. Raw ensemble shares are often overconfident or biased, so they were tested on the same models’ hindcasts for each start month, 1993–2016, built exactly as the forecast is (each member against its model’s own hindcast average, leaving out the year being verified) and verified against CPC’s official ONI and RONI over the same four seasons. The calibration tried is a shift and scale of the members’ peaks (a new centre, a rescaled signal and a rescaled spread: three numbers per index), fitted with each year left out in turn and scored with the ranked probability skill score over +0.5, +1.0, +1.5, +2.0 and +2.5 °C, with a 95% block-bootstrap interval and a permutation test. Calibrated odds replace the raw shares only if they score better with the interval above zero and p below 0.05/24, allowing for the 24 tests (twelve start months, two indices).

How strong will it get?

Every model’s average forecast, their combined average in bold, and the spread of all members as a shaded fan, with the observed index leading in from the left.

ONI is the three-month running mean of the Niño-3.4 anomaly (5°N–5°S, 170–120°W). RONI is the Niño-3.4 anomaly minus the 20°S–20°N average anomaly, rescaled month by month so it keeps ONI’s thresholds. NOAA CPC made RONI its official ENSO index in February 2026. The observed values are CPC’s published tables; CPC revises them for up to two months, and months after its latest value are estimated from NOAA OISST daily data, which is itself preliminary for about two weeks. Those months are drawn with open markers. The models’ anomalies are on a 1993–2016 base; CPC’s RONI is on 1991–2020 and its ONI on shifting 30-year bases. On Niño-3.4 the 1991–2020 and 1993–2016 climatologies differ by under 0.1 °C in every calendar month (most in late winter and spring); the observed month that starts each smoothed model line is moved onto the models’ base by that difference. A fixed-base ONI can run well above CPC’s: from April–June to July–September 2026, this site’s fixed-base OISST estimate ran 0.06–0.39 °C above CPC’s ONI, while the two RONIs agreed to within 0.08 °C. RONI is the fairer comparison with what CPC publishes.

The next twelve months

C3S carries ECCC’s CanSIPS for six months; ECCC publishes the same 40-member forecast for twelve on its MSC Datamart, posted on the last day of the previous month, with its full 1990–2020 hindcast. That copy is read directly, each of its two models (GEM5.2-NEMO, members 1–20, and CanESM5, 21–40) is taken against its own 1993–2016 hindcast for the same start month and lead, exactly as for the C3S systems, and the Niño-3.4 box and the 20°S–20°N average for RONI use ocean cells only (the Datamart’s sea temperature is filled over land). On the plume, CanSIPS alone continues past month six, drawn dashed over a shaded band, and the multi-model average and fan stop where the other systems stop.

Canada’s CanSIPS is the one system here that runs for a full year: 40 members from two models, read straight from Environment and Climate Change Canada. The other systems stop at six months, so everything after month six is one model’s view, not a multi-model forecast, and is shaded to say so.

How east-based will it be?

El Niños differ in where the warmth sits. East-based events, like 1982–83 and 1997–98, warm the eastern Pacific all the way to the coast of South America. Central-Pacific events, like 2009–10, keep the warmth near the date line. The two index numbers below, E for the eastern Pacific and C for the central Pacific (Takahashi and others, 2011), measure each in standard deviations of their normal variability, so the same scale places this forecast among every El Niño since 1950.

Following Takahashi, Montecinos, Goubanova and Dewitte (2011, Geophysical Research Letters 38, L10704): the two leading patterns (EOFs) of monthly sea-surface temperature anomalies over 10°S–10°N, 140°E–80°W (Takahashi et al. used HadISST between the coasts), from NOAA ERSSTv6 on a fixed 1991–2020 base, trained on 1979–2020. The first is a basin-wide warming (66% of the variance), the second an east–west seesaw (14%). Rotating them by 45° gives E = (PC1 − PC2)/√2 and C = (PC1 + PC2)/√2, each in standard deviations. Every month since 1950 is projected on the same two patterns.

Each model member’s forecast anomaly field (C3S’s own anomaly product, same hindcast base as above) is moved onto the 1991–2020 base using the observed difference between the two climatologies (a few hundredths of a degree) and projected on the observed patterns. On this page an event or a member is east-based when E exceeds C by at least 1, central when C exceeds E by at least 1, and basin-wide in between. With that rule 1982–83 and 1997–98 are east-based; 1972–73, 1991–92, 2015–16 and 2023–24 basin-wide; 2002–03, 2004–05 and 2009–10 central. The impacts study on the El Niño monitor classes thousands of CMIP6 winters by the same rule on the same observed patterns; for those 65-year model runs it takes each winter’s anomalies from the other winters within ±10 years, so that the models’ forced warming does not move winters between classes.

The centre of the warming is the anomaly-weighted mean longitude of the stretch of the equator (2°S–2°N, smoothed over 10° of longitude) that is within 30% of the warmest point. A bare maximum jumps between two near-equal humps: December–February 2023–24 reads 102°W by its maximum but 131°W this way.

How the verdict is decided. A class is named only when three things hold: the models, counted once each, agree on the direction of E minus C (one-sample t-test against zero, p < 0.05); at least two-thirds of the members fall in the class; and all models but at most one have their average in it. A significant lean that misses the other two tests is reported as a lean, and anything else as a split. How well the models forecast the east–central balance has not been verified here.

Every C3S forecast starts from the ocean on the first of its issue month, so its first month can be checked against the ocean while the month is still running. Each day, NOAA OISST daily anomalies (1991–2020 base) are averaged into the same 2° cells, projected on the same two patterns, and compared with each model’s forecast for that month. The observed track on the charts switches from ERSSTv6 to OISST after ERSST’s last settled month, because ERSST’s newest month is provisional; over 1982–2025 the two agree closely in E and C (correlation 0.985 and 0.982, mean difference under 0.01), although in mid-2026 OISST reads the coastal warming about 0.4–0.5 higher in E than ERSST’s provisional months.

First-month-corrected shifts each model’s members in E and C by that model’s first-month error. Whether that error persists was tested on each model’s own hindcasts for the same start month (1993–2016, raw sea-surface temperature, anomalies against the hindcast mean exactly as C3S takes them for the forecast), verified against ERSSTv6: the correction is weighted by how much of the first-month error carried forward, pooled over the models and fitted with each verified year left out.

The page leads with the ocean as observed; the forecast as issued and the first-month-corrected forecast are shown side by side, neither ahead of the other. A linear regression of the observed E and C on each model’s E and C, lead by lead and cross-validated the same way, was also tested.

Caveat. On the fixed base, the western Pacific’s faster warming puts a slow upward drift into C (+0.16 standard deviations per decade since 1950) and very little into E (+0.03), so events from the 1950s to 1970s sit a little lower on C than they would against their own era’s climate.
