The page

The Madden–Julian Oscillation in the global ensembles: every model's forecast on one phase diagram (AIFS-ENS, IFS-ENS, GEFS and GEPS, standard or ENSO-removed index), a time–longitude chart of the MJO with the El Niño pattern taken out, the ECMWF runs one after another, and what each phase has meant for temperature, rain and the flow. The phase diagram and the impacts explain themselves on the page, under their own figures. The waves around the MJO are on the tropical waves page.

Forecast

Time–longitude chart

Time runs down the chart and longitude across it, so anything that moves east slopes down to the right: an MJO at about 5 m s−1, a Kelvin wave three times as fast. The field is the 200 hPa velocity potential averaged 15°S–15°N. Green is upper-level divergence, the air spreading out above deep convection; brown is convergence, above sinking and dry air. The last 110 days are analyses (the AIFS-ENS control at 0 h, the 00 and 12 UTC runs averaged); below the line is the AIFS-ENS forecast to day 15, the mean of the control and the 50 members.

What was taken out. A strong El Niño holds rising air over the central and eastern Pacific and sinking air over the Maritime Continent for months. Indices that remove only a running mean, as the RMM does with the previous 120 days, leave part of a growing El Niño in and read it as an MJO stalled in phases 6 to 8; a shorter running mean would take the slow MJO out with it. Here the standing pattern is removed by regression instead. From each day's field come out, in turn: the ERA5 1991–2020 normal for the day and its trend; the pattern that goes with Niño-3.4 and with Niño-3.4 three months earlier in ERA5, scaled by today's values (NOAA OISST, detrended, 31-day mean); and the mean of what is left over the record at each longitude, the standing part the regression misses. The strip above the chart shows what was removed: the black line is the whole standing pattern, the blue line its El Niño part, drawn where that regression is significant. The regression is taken out at every longitude all the same, because a field cut at the edge of the significant region would carry that edge into the filtered contours. At +3.4 °C the regression is stretched beyond most of the El Niños it was fitted on, which is one reason the record's own mean is removed after it.

The contours. What remains is filtered in wavenumber and frequency. The black contours are the MJO's band, eastward wavenumbers 1 to 5 and periods of 30 to 96 days, every 1.5 × 106 m2 s−1: solid where the upper air diverges (active), dashed where it converges (suppressed). The purple contours are the active phase of Kelvin waves (eastward wavenumbers 1 to 14, periods of 2.5 to 20 days). A band-pass filter needs the future as well as the past, so each member's own forecast stands in for it: the filter is run once per member on the analyses joined to that member, and the contours are the mean.

The forecast. Each member has the model's drift removed first: the mean error of the last 60 days of runs against the analyses, by longitude and lead time. It is not small, about 3 × 106 m2 s−1 by day 15, as large as the signal itself. Below the line, shading and contours are drawn only where the members agree on the sign (a two-sided sign test with the false discovery rate held at 5 %); blank areas are where they do not. The filtered contours of the last 30 analysis days are tested the same way, because the forecast still shapes them.

The phase. The numerals 1 to 8 above the chart mark the longitude where each RMM phase has its active centre, from the 1991–2020 record: 25°E, 61°E, 86°E, 107°E, 136°E, 170°E, 107°W and 23°W. A solid black contour under a numeral is an active MJO in that phase's position. The column on the right does the same reading with numbers. The MJO band is fitted, by longitude, with the two patterns that go with the observed RMM1 and RMM2 in ERA5, each scaled as the RMM is, so an amplitude of 1 means what it means there. The bar is the amplitude, with the dashed line at 1, and a phase is printed only where the amplitude is 1 or more and at least three members in four point within 45° of the mean. Below 1 there is no MJO and no phase is given.

How good the phase is. Tested on ERA5 1991–2020 with the patterns fitted on other years: on days when both this index and the observed RMM are active, the phase is the same on half of them and within one phase on 94 %. Run as the chart runs, on 130 days of record and a 15-day future, the agreement holds on the latest analysis day (51 % and 94 %) and at day 15 (51 % and 93 %), but the filter has less to work with at the end of a record: the amplitude reads about 10 % low on the latest analysis day and about 30 % low at day 15.

Why it can differ from the phase diagram. The RMM is mostly wind and is not filtered in time, so a fast Kelvin pulse, a burst of westerlies or a pattern only a few days old can push it past 1. This index answers only to a planetary-scale signal that has moved east for weeks: it is active on about two-thirds of the days the RMM is. When the diagram shows an MJO and this chart says there is none, look at the shading: a single fast streak is a Kelvin wave, and two or three green bands around the equator at once are too short a wave for the RMM's patterns.

Reading it with care. Velocity potential is a smooth field made mostly of the largest scales, so it shows a weak MJO's circulation more readily than its rain: a contour here is not a promise of rain under it.

ECMWF successive runs

The Real-time Multivariate MJO (RMM) phase diagram of Wheeler and Hendon (2004) for the ECMWF AIFS-ENS ensemble, 51 members to day 15, one run after another. The projection uses three fields: the 850 and 200 hPa zonal winds and a stand-in for outgoing longwave radiation built from the standardised tropical precipitation with its sign reversed (rain and OLR are closely anticorrelated); a run whose precipitation has not arrived is projected on the winds alone. The physics-based IFS-ENS (50 members) from the same start is drawn in blue by the same method once its data has landed, an hour or two later, so the two can be compared directly.

Amplitude is the distance from the centre (rings at 1, 2 and 3). The ellipses, every five days, span one standard deviation of the members either side of the mean in RMM1 and RMM2. At the same valid time the AIFS-ENS 12 UTC runs sit off the 00 UTC runs by an amount that grows with lead, so each 12 UTC run has the mean difference of the last seven pairs removed, lead by lead. The observed track is ERA5 up to mid-July 2026 and, since then, the AIFS-ENS control at step 0 of each run (winds only; from 30 May to 12 October 2026 its phase sat within a median 5° of the ERA5 RMM's on active days).

This index keeps a changing El Niño in it, so the caption under the figure opens with what the same forecast shows once ENSO is removed.
