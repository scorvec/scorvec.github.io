The page

NOAA's GFS, ECMWF's physics model (the IFS control, the single unperturbed run of the IFS), ECMWF's AI model (AIFS) and Environment Canada's global model (GDPS) are scored against the same observations: hourly surface stations, about 650 radiosondes a day through the whole troposphere and, for rain over the US, the Stage IV radar and gauge analysis. Every model gets the same treatment. Each is first averaged onto one 0.5° grid, then interpolated to each station over land only, 2 m temperature is moved to the height of the station, and stations where the model's terrain is more than 100 m off are left out. Each radiosonde level is compared with the model where and when the balloon actually was. Four ensembles are scored the same way to day 15, and the GEFS and GEPS weekly maps against ERA5 to week 5.

The page shows one view at a time, picked from the list. It opens on the latest ten 00Z runs; the switch at the top (Runs scored) takes the scores from the newest 30 or 90 runs of the verification database instead, where more of the differences between the models can be told apart. The note under a figure names the runs it holds. How the scores are made, the last part of this guide, covers the charts, the intervals and tests, and the observations, the grid and the matching.

The verification package behind the page, mverify, was written by Claude, Anthropic's AI model, with guidance from Shawn Corvec. The scores are a first sample and will grow as new runs are added. The package is open source (github.com/scorvec/nwp_verification), so anyone can score their own model against the same observations. It reads GRIB, netCDF and Zarr:

pip install git+https://github.com/scorvec/nwp_verification
mverify db ghcnh --year 2026 --since 2026-10-01     # every station, from that date
mverify score MYMODEL=forecasts/ GFS=gfs/ --out scores.json

Days 1–10: single runs

Summary

The headline variables in one region: 500 hPa height, 850 hPa temperature, 250 hPa vector wind and 700 hPa specific humidity against radiosondes, and 2 m temperature, dew point, sea-level pressure and 10 m vector wind at surface stations. For each variable and day every pair of models goes through the scorecard's paired test at the 5% level: a model drawn alone in its colour has a significantly smaller error than every other model, and faint stripes mark the models that cannot be told apart from the smallest. The head-to-head table counts, for each pair, the variable-days on which each model is significantly ahead, and the band at the top adds up each model's wins and losses over every pair.

With ten runs the test is strict: a tie says the runs so far cannot separate the models, not that they are equal. The longer windows of the run switch, from the verification database, separate more of them.

Scorecard

One model's RMSE against another's, for every variable, day and region. Each model pair is tested with a paired t-test on the per-run mean squared errors. Runs a day apart share much of their weather, so the test counts fewer effective runs when the run-to-run differences are correlated (Geer 2016). A cell gives the difference in percent where the test passes at the 5% level, and a dot where it does not. The scorecard is a picture made each night; a phone draws it in the page instead, one region at a time, and a tap on a square gives both errors and the test.

Headline variables

Each headline variable's RMSE by day in one region, upper air and surface apart, as a difference from the IFS control's in % or as the RMSE itself. A marker is filled where the 95% interval of the difference leaves out zero.

One variable

One variable in one region by lead: its RMSE, the same as a percentage above or below the IFS control's on the runs both have, and its bias, each pooling every observation and run at that lead. The whiskers are 95% intervals; the note under the chart names the observations and the runs.

500 hPa skill

The anomaly correlation of the 500 hPa height is the headline score of the WMO's lead centres, drawn here as NCEP's Environmental Modeling Center draws it, one lead at a time against the date the forecast verifies. Every model's 500 hPa height at 00Z of each day of its 00Z run (each ensemble's mean over every member the open data carries) and ERA5's at the same hour are averaged by area onto the WMO's 1.5° verification grid, and each is taken as an anomaly from the ERA5 1991–2020 normal of that calendar day. The correlation of the two anomaly maps over 20–80°N or 20–80°S, each point weighted by the cosine of its latitude and each map's mean anomaly removed first (WMO-No. 485), is 1 for a perfect forecast and 0 for one no better than the normal; by convention a forecast stops being of synoptic use below 0.6.

Every model is scored against ERA5, not against its own analysis as the lead centres do, so that four centres and the AI models share one truth; ERA5 arrives about a week late, so the newest days fill in as it does. The time series is a running mean of seven runs: a single run's score swings with the day's weather far more than the models differ. An ensemble mean smooths away what its members disagree on, which a correlation rewards, so past day 5 it beats its own centre's single run without having the same detail; the two are drawn together, and compared within their own kind.

Upper air

Error against radiosondes at the standard levels from 1000 to 100 hPa, every model on the same soundings; humidity stops at 300 hPa, above which sonde humidity is unreliable. A profile's RMSE against the IFS control is the square root of the ratio of the two models' summed squared errors on the same soundings, its interval from the runs taken as clusters of observations that share their weather, and its marker filled where that interval leaves out no difference.

Station map

Each dot is a station, coloured by one model's error at one lead averaged over the window's runs, or by the difference between two models. The map's file is the page's largest, so it loads when the view is first opened.

A station's series. A click on a surface station draws what it reported against every model's forecast for the same hours at the map's lead, over the newest 45 days of the database (a run joins it once its day 10 is observed, so the newest point is about two weeks old), with each model's error beneath. One station is one place and a few dozen runs: the series is for seeing what happened there, not for ranking the models, which the map's colours and the scorecard's tests do.

Wind speed

Wind speed is skewed and cannot go below zero, so RMSE and bias, which reward a forecast that hedges towards the middle, are joined by the whole distribution, on the same station pairs for every model: forecast and observed quantiles from 1 to 99.5%; the hybrid Weibull of Takle and Brown (1978), that is the share of calms (under 0.5 m/s, as reports come in whole knots) and a Weibull shape k and scale c fitted by maximum likelihood to the rest (a smaller k is a wider spread, a smaller c weaker winds); and how often 5, 10 and 15 m/s are reached. Each run's speeds are binned at 0.05 m/s and the runs resampled whole, model and observation together, for a 95% interval on every difference. The chart draws each model's quantiles less the observed ones with those intervals, so zero is the observed distribution, and beside it the fitted distributions themselves.

US rain scores

24 hour totals ending 12Z against the Stage IV analysis, from the verification database's newest runs (the window's, at most a year), on the land cells of the common 0.5° grid as the rain maps, every model on the same runs and cells, each cell weighted by its area. The equitable threat score is the share of rain areas the model gets right beyond chance (1 is perfect, 0 no better than chance). The fractions skill score asks the same question in neighbourhoods of growing size, 50 to 400 km (Roberts and Lean 2008), so it forgives a band forecast a few tens of kilometres off. Both are summed over every run before they are scored, and their 95% intervals treat each run as one block of cells that share its weather: the threat score's from a jackknife that leaves out one run at a time, the skill score's from the ratio of its two sums, each with the allowance for runs that resemble the day before. A threshold that too few cells reach in the analysis (100 over the window) is left out. The table beneath compares the window's runs against Stage IV and against the rain gauges, once the gauges give every model 100 readings at the window chosen.

Rain gauges. 24 hour totals at stations (GHCNh), ending at 00Z and 12Z: the totals stations report and, where a station gives the amount of every hour (most US and Canadian stations), totals added up from those amounts, dry days included. A US station reports a 24 hour total only at 12Z after a wet day, so the built totals are most of the sample there; they enter with the runs of November 2026. A built total needs all 24 hours, and an hour without an amount counts as dry only where the gauge is known to work: the station reported an amount in the 30 days before, its report does not say the gauge is out, and no rain or snow is falling in it. A reported total that differs from the built one by more than 1 mm and 10% is left out. So is a total above 400 mm, and one of 25 mm or more that stands above the station's totals 12 hours before and 12 hours after added together (its rain must show in one of them) or that fell through hours the station's weather reports show as dry. A station with one such false total loses all its totals of that year: nothing in the values tells a faulty gauge's true totals from its false ones. No check looks at a forecast. A total that fails after its run was scored leaves that run's scores too: each night the gauge scores of every run in the database are made again without the totals that have failed since. The totals are scored like the other station variables, each model's total for those 24 hours interpolated to the gauge from the common grid, every model on the same readings. In the US they check the gridded scores at points rather than independently: Stage IV's analyses take in ASOS gauges.

US rain maps

The Stage IV totals averaged onto the common 0.5° grid wherever they cover at least half a cell, against each model's total for the same 24 hours, cell by cell, over the newest year of runs in the verification database and the windows of a lead group (days 1–2, 3–5, 6–9). Four maps per lead group: the ratio of the model's total rain to Stage IV's, and for 1, 10 and 25 mm the frequency bias, the number of days the model reaches the amount over the number of days Stage IV does (above 1, the model makes that much rain too often). How much it rained on a day is mostly noise for a single cell; whether it rained is not, so the frequency biases find what the totals cannot.

Each cell is tested over the valid days: the day's difference (the model's rain less Stage IV's, or the share of the group's runs that reach the amount less whether Stage IV did), averaged over the runs that verify that day, goes through the scorecard's paired t-test with its allowance for days that resemble the day before, once a cell has 8 days (and for a frequency bias, 5 days on which Stage IV reaches the amount). The p values of all the cells of a map then go through the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at a 10% false-discovery rate: coloured cells are ones whose bias is unlikely to be noise, grey ones were tested and are within it. The models with 95% of the year's runs are mapped on the runs they all have, and one with fewer (GDPS, whose runs reach back only to September 2026) on its own runs, which the note under the maps gives. The maps are pictures made each night; a phone shows them one model at a time.

Days 1–15: ensembles

Scorecard

IFS-ENS, AIFS-ENS, GEFS and GEPS are verified member by member against the same stations, radiosondes and Stage IV, at 00Z of every day to day 15, averaged first onto the same 0.5° grid. ECMWF's open data carries the IFS ensemble's 50 perturbed members but not its control; the others are scored with theirs (AIFS-ENS 51 members, GEFS 31, GEPS 21), and GEFS and GEPS dew points come from their 2 m relative humidity. The database keeps every case of every run (each observation against each run), its members summarised: their mean, spread, range, the CRPS and where the observation falls among them.

The CRPS (continuous ranked probability score) measures the whole forecast distribution against the observation, in the variable's unit, and reduces to the absolute error for a single forecast; the fair CRPS used here does not favour larger ensembles. A wind is scored as its two components, each level's CRPS their mean. The scorecard compares two ensembles' CRPS on the cases both have, with the same paired test across runs as the single runs' scorecard, and marks only significant differences. Of the three ensemble views, only the scorecard tests a difference between ensembles; the other two describe them.

Error and spread

The CRPS by forecast day for one variable and region, with spread/skill beside it: the members' spread, with the observation's own error added where it is known, divided by the RMSE of the ensemble mean. It is 1 for a calibrated ensemble and below 1 for an overconfident one. A rank histogram counts where each observation falls among the members: flat when the ensemble is calibrated, U-shaped when it is too narrow.

Probabilities

Reliability compares the share of members forecasting an event with how often it happened, with the Brier skill score against the sample's own frequency: a reliable ensemble lies on the diagonal. A curve is drawn once the sample holds 30 events and 30 non-events, each of its points 30 cases, and a point where the event never came, or always did, has no interval.

Weeks 1–5

GEFS and GEPS

The weekly anomaly maps of the two days 1–35 pages, GEFS and GEPS, against ERA5: the ensemble mean's anomaly from the 1991–2020 normal of 2 m temperature, 500 hPa height and sea-level pressure, averaged over days 1–7, 8–14, 15–21, 22–28 and 29–35, on the WMO's 1.5° grid. Each run's score is the centred anomaly correlation over the region (each map less its mean, every cell weighted by the cosine of its latitude, 2 m temperature over land only): 1 for a perfect forecast, 0 for the normal, which is the climatology benchmark. Persistence, the other benchmark, forecasts every week with the observed anomaly of the 7 days before the start.

The forecasts. GEFS is scored on its Monday and Thursday 00Z runs, GEPS's start days, from 1 November 2025; the runs from 18 August 2025 feed only the drift correction. GEFS's ensemble mean is NCEP's own (the 0.5° geavg files on NOAA's open-data bucket) rather than the mean of the members the page reads, reduced to days and to 1.5° as the page reduces its members and taken as the page takes its anomalies: the reforecast climatology for the lead and start date, the ERA5 shift to 1991–2020, and the operational drift, which is estimated by the page's method from the runs started 36 to 80 days before but against ERA5 rather than GEFS's own analyses (the difference from day 1 removes a steady offset between the two) and from this series' Monday and Thursday runs only, about 13 rather than the page's 45. GEPS is filed by its own page's build, each run's weekly maps as the page draws them, from the 2 November 2026 run on; its earlier runs were not kept, so it has no backfill.

The truth. ERA5 comes from Google's ARCO copy at 00Z and 12Z, averaged onto the grid by area; a day is the mean of its 12Z and the next day's 00Z, as GEFS's day, while GEPS averages four six-hour steps, which the two samples come nearest. The newest months are ERA5's preliminary release (ERA5T), kept as they were read. The normal is ERA5 1991–2020 from WeatherBench 2's 1.5° copy, the same two samples a day, each day of a 366-day calendar the mean of the days within a week of it in all thirty years.

The test. A score in the table is the mean over the runs with a 90% interval from a moving-block bootstrap over weeks of starts, in blocks of four weeks, since runs a few days apart share their weather. It is printed only where its test passes the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at a 10% false-discovery rate over every test of every field, region and week (each mean against zero, each model against persistence, and GEPS against GEFS on the starts both have), so its interval leaves out the benchmark; n.s. marks a test that did not pass, and a mean over fewer than 16 weeks of starts is not tested. A week is scored once ERA5 covers all its days, so the later weeks hold fewer runs; the number is in each cell's tooltip.

Data

The database

Every 00Z run, once 14 days old, is kept for good: every model's pairs against every GHCNh station, the radiosondes and Stage IV, including the pairs the scores leave out (marked, with the terrain mismatch and sensor height that decide it), and each run's sums, from which a window of any length is scored without its pairs. A model is scored in a window when it has at least 95% of its runs, on the runs every such model has, so all are judged on the same runs; GDPS, whose runs reach back only to September 2026, joins the longer windows as the database grows. Each run's observations are those every model it has was matched to. The database begins with the 13 May 2026 run, the first of the current IFS (Cycle 50r1) and AIFS (version 2), and is kept in two places, the site's GitHub releases and its file store, every copy checked against the original.

How the scores are made

The charts

Every line chart is drawn in the page from the scores' own numbers. Each model keeps one colour and one marker shape throughout (an ensemble takes its centre's); a whisker is a 95% interval, and pointing at a chart, or stepping through it with the arrow keys, lists every model's value and interval at that lead. A lead or a pressure level is charted once every model has 100 pairs of forecast and observation there over the window.

Uncertainty

Every score comes from a sample of runs, and a different few weeks would give different numbers: the intervals and tests say how different. Runs a day apart share most of their weather, so they are not independent samples; every interval and test cuts the number of runs to an effective number from the lag-1 autocorrelation of the runs' scores (often a half to a third of them). For the intervals that autocorrelation is estimated once for each lead of a window, pooled over every model, variable and region, with the small-sample correction of Kendall (1954): from one series of ten runs the estimate swings so widely that a high draw would leave about one effective run and an interval ten times too wide.

Intervals are 95% Student t intervals on the runs, their variance inflated for that autocorrelation; a score that cannot go below zero (an RMSE, a CRPS, a ratio of two) is taken on its logarithm, so its interval stays above zero and reaches further up than down; and the anomaly correlation is averaged through Fisher's z = artanh(r) and turned back, so that a run's score near 1 is not given the same room as one near 0.5. A pooled score, an RMSE or a bias summed over every station and run, takes its interval from the runs' own sums, each run one cluster of observations that share its weather.

Two models are compared run by run (the paired difference): most of a model's run-to-run swing is the weather, which both models share, and an interval on each model alone would hide differences that are real (Geer 2016, Tellus A 68, 30229). A difference counts as significant when its paired test passes the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at a 10% false-discovery rate over every model and lead of a figure, so that testing many leads does not manufacture a winner; a hollow marker or grey cell is within the noise of the runs so far, not proof that the models are equal.

Observations, grid and matching

  • Observations. Surface stations come from NOAA's GHCNh hourly archive (METAR and synoptic reports), screened for range, spikes and stuck sensors. Radiosondes come from NOAA's IGRA2 archive and the scorvec.com sounding mirror and are reduced to the standard levels from 1000 to 100 hPa, each level at the position the balloon reached it. Specific humidity is computed from the reported dew point. Precipitation is NCEP's Stage IV analysis.
  • One grid. Every model is averaged onto the same 0.5° grid before it is compared with anything, by the area each of its cells shares with each 0.5° cell, and its terrain and land-sea mask with it. Interpolated from their own grids, the 0.15° GDPS and the 0.25° models would be scored from cells of different size: a finer grid carries small-scale detail whose misplacement counts twice, and a model published on a grid finer than its own resolution looks sharper than it is. 0.5° is coarser than every model's own resolution.
  • Matching. Forecasts are interpolated bilinearly to each station from land points only, so coastal stations are not scored against sea temperatures. 2 m temperature is moved to station height with a standard lapse rate, 10 m wind is adjusted to the anemometer height, and stations where model and station heights differ by more than 100 m are left out of the scores (the database keeps them, marked).
  • Pressure. Mean sea-level pressure, at stations where every model's terrain is at most 500 m. The reduction to sea level runs through the ground under the station, each model and station makes it its own way, and the differences grow with the height reduced.
  • Scores. RMSE, bias, MAE and correlation for each variable, region and lead; precipitation on the common grid's land cells with the equitable threat score, the frequency bias and the fractions skill score (Roberts and Lean 2008).
  • Same sample. Each model is scored only on the observations every model was matched to, so a model whose terrain or coastline drops a station is not judged on an easier set.
  • Data. GFS from NOAA on AWS; the IFS control and AIFS from ECMWF open data (Google Cloud mirror), all published at 0.25°; GDPS from ECCC's HPFX server at 0.15°; all scored on the common 0.5° grid.
