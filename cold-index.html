How it works

What it predicts

A cold spell here means the US population-weighted temperature, averaged over days 15–28 after today, ranks among the coldest 15% of two-week spells in December–February. "Normal" is set by the surrounding 21 winters, so the warming climate cannot pose as skill. The index runs from 16 November to 31 January, when the whole window falls in winter.

The clues

Atlantic block: high pressure over Greenland, a negative AO and a negative NAO. Blocked patterns tend to last or come back. Weak vortex: high heights over the pole at 100 hPa. Sudden warming: the 10 hPa wind at 60°N turning easterly in the last 30 days. Recent cold: the last week of US temperature. MJO: tropical convection over the west Pacific (phases 6–7) raises the odds; phases 2–3 lower them.

Where the weights come from

From 146 historical runs of 28 CMIP6 climate models, 1850–2014: about 1.6 million issue days, so the weights are tight and none was fitted to the real winters it is scored on. The clues overlap, so their combined push is damped by one factor (0.64) learned in the same models. The models' MJO link to the Atlantic is too weak to use. That part comes from observed winters, held out one at a time and shrunk halfway.

What it can’t do

It isn't a yes/no forecast. On most days it sits near the 15% base. The average gain is small (Brier skill about +4%), and the value lies in the uncommon days that move the odds. Even trained on 24,000 model winters, these clues can’t do much better, so this is the limit of the observed state, not of the sample. By weeks 4–5 nearly all the signal is gone.
