South America model maps

The newest IFS control (ECMWF's unperturbed 9 km forecast) and GDPS (Canada's 15 km global model) over South America to ten days: hover for values, click an airport for its meteogram, double-click for a sounding.

About these maps

The two models

IFS control: the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts' Integrated Forecasting System run without perturbations, at about 9 km (the unperturbed member that anchors its ensemble), read here on the 0.1° grid of ECMWF's open data, interpolated from the model's own octahedral grid. 00 and 12 UTC runs, every 3 hours to +144 h and every 6 hours to +240 h; a run appears here about 10 hours after its start time, when its last files are published. GDPS: Environment and Climate Change Canada's Global Deterministic Prediction System (the GEM model, about 15 km), on its 0.15° latitude-longitude grid, 00 and 12 UTC to +240 h; surface fields every 3 hours (the precipitation rate and type 6-hourly after +168 h), the convective fields and the 925 and 850 hPa winds on the IFS's steps (every 3 hours to +144 h, then 6-hourly), so the two models can be compared at the same times. It appears about 6 hours after its start. Both are cropped to 60° S–15° N, 95–30° W; only the newest run of each is kept.

How the convective maps are computed

Every column of the grid (about 490,000 for the IFS, 220,000 for the GDPS, every step) goes through the site's ECAPE kernel: SHARPlib, through the same wrapper the sounding explorer runs in the browser, so a column here and a sounding there come from identical code. The column is the surface (surface pressure, terrain height, 2 m temperature and dewpoint, 10 m wind) and then the model's pressure levels above the ground: the IFS's 13 open-data levels (1000, 925, 850, 700, 600, 500, 400, 300, 250, 200, 150, 100, 50 hPa) and 20 of the GDPS's (1015, 1000, 985, 970, 950, 925, 900, 850, 800, 750, 700, 600, 500, 400, 300, 250, 200, 150, 100, 50 hPa); a level less than 5 hPa above the ground is left out. Heights are hypsometric (virtual temperature, trapezoidal in ln p) up from the model's terrain and surface pressure, and each run checks them against the model's own 500 and 850 hPa heights. The most-unstable parcel comes from the effective-inflow search in the lowest 400 hPa, the mixed-layer parcel is the lowest 100 hPa mean; ECAPE takes its storm-relative inflow from the Bunkers storm motion.

Southern Hemisphere storms. South of the equator the cyclonic supercell is the one that turns left of the mean wind. Each column there is handed to the kernel with its wind profile mirrored, so the kernel's right-moving storm is the real profile's left mover: ECAPE gets that storm's inflow, and the helicity is that storm's with the sign turned, positive for cyclonic rotation in both hemispheres. The conventional Southern Hemisphere value, negative for a cyclonic storm, is the negative of the number shown. North of the equator nothing is mirrored; near it, where there is little Coriolis force, neither storm is favoured.

Limits. Pressure levels are coarse near the ground: the IFS has only 1000, 925 and 850 hPa below 700 hPa, so the mixed-layer parcel and the 0–1 km helicity rest on the surface and at most those three levels (fewer over high ground), and a shallow inversion between levels is missed. The GDPS's levels are 15–50 hPa apart down there. Measured with the kernel on idealised soundings (Amazon wet season, a capped Chaco warm sector with a low-level jet, a Pampas elevated mixed layer), each run once on a column every 10 hPa and once on the model's levels: CAPE came out within 3 % of the fine column on both models' levels, but ECAPE 12–36 % higher on the IFS's levels and 2–19 % higher on the GDPS's (the entrainment term needs the mid-level humidity profile and the 0–1 km inflow, which coarse levels smooth), and the IFS's 0–3 km helicity up to 30 % lower where a low-level jet sat between its levels (the GDPS's within 8 %). Treat the IFS's ECAPE and low-level helicity as a guide to where they are large, not as values to threshold; the GDPS's are the closer of the two.

Simulated IR

Neither model's open output has a simulated satellite image: ECMWF's open data and the GDPS files on the MSC Datamart carry no brightness temperature and no cloud-top field. The map is made from the one quantity both publish that sees cloud tops, the outgoing longwave radiation at the top of the atmosphere. Its flux temperature (OLR/σ)¼ is turned into a window-channel brightness temperature with the Ohring–Gruber relation (Ohring, Gruber and Ellingson 1984; as in the site's GDPS simulated-satellite loop), which warms clear skies by up to about 20 K (water vapour absorbs and emits from colder air outside the window) and leaves deep cloud tops nearly unchanged. Against a satellite's 10.8 µm image it differs in three ways: it is the whole longwave spectrum converted, not one channel, so thin cirrus and low cloud over warm ground are only approximate; the IFS's is an average over the 3 or 6 hours before the valid time, so moving storms are smeared along their track; and the clouds are the model's own.

Airports and meteograms

Large and medium airports with scheduled service in South America, from OurAirports (public domain): the large ones show at continental scale and the rest as you zoom in; hover for the model's value there, click or tap one (or type its code above) for the meteogram of both models through the whole run. The series are sampled from each step as it is computed: bilinear between the four nearest grid points for temperature, dewpoint, wind, pressure, precipitation, precipitable water, θe and the simulated IR; the nearest point for the precipitation rate and type, CAPE, ECAPE, shear and helicity.

Forecast soundings

Double-click the map (on a phone, double-tap or press and hold) for that spot's forecast sounding from the model on screen, opened in the site's sounding explorer. The columns are the ones the convective maps are computed from, every 4th grid point of the IFS control (0.4°) and every 3rd of the GDPS (0.45°), the nearest one to the spot: the surface (2 m temperature and dewpoint, 10 m wind) and the pressure levels at least 5 hPa above the ground, 13 for the IFS and 20 for the GDPS, so the profile is much coarser than a balloon's. Heights are rebuilt from the surface as in the maps. Temperatures are stored to 0.1 K, dewpoints to 0.2 K and winds to 0.25 m/s; each run decodes them back as this page does and checks them against the columns. The winds are the real, earth-relative ones and the hodograph is the true Southern Hemisphere one: the mirrored, cyclonic-positive helicity of the maps does not apply here, and the explorer's storm motions and helicity follow its usual right-moving convention, so south of the equator its helicity has the conventional sign (negative for a cyclonic storm).

How the page works

Nothing on this page is a picture. Each field and step is shipped as the model's own numbers, rounded to 256 levels, on its native grid, and your browser colours, projects, contours and reads it: zooming shows the model's grid cells, and the hover value is the stored one. The maps are drawn on an oblique stereographic projection centred at 22.5° S, 62.5° W (conformal: shapes and wind directions are right everywhere). Contours are drawn by your graphics card from the same numbers after a light smoothing; their labels and the highs and lows are placed once the map is at rest. Data use: opening a map loads only the step on screen (0.1–0.5 MB); playing, stepping or dragging the time slider downloads every step of that map (65–81 files, typically 5–20 MB a map). A meteogram reads only that airport's series (a few kilobytes per model), and a sounding only its column's tile, hour by hour as they are shown.
