Tropical Pacific

MSLP and 10 m wind · ensembles and single runs, to day 15
The full-field Pacific surface forecast behind the equatorial diagnostics: mean-sea-level pressure (contours, with H/L centres) and 10 m wind (speed shaded in knots, plus barbs), animated over the tropical and subtropical Pacific. An equatorial westerly push between the Maritime Continent and the dateline is a wind-burst signature. Mid-latitude weather

Dynamic tropopause · θ on 2 PVU, hemisphere and five regions · explainer
The upper-level flow as PV thinkers read it. Ertel potential vorticity is computed from the temperature and wind of the IFS control (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026) on nine levels from 700 to 100 hPa — a single run, because PV does not survive ensemble averaging: a mean of fifty folds is a smear. The dynamic tropopause is the 2-PVU surface, found by searching upward from 700 hPa; its potential temperature is shaded (low θ, blue, is stratospheric or polar air reaching down; high θ, red, is a tropical tropopause), its pressure contoured, and the wind on it drawn. Troughs, cut-off lows and ridges are the θ pattern itself; a tight gradient is the jet; a tongue of low θ wrapping up is a Rossby wave breaking. The other two loops give PV on the 330 and 350 K isentropes with the 2-PVU line — 330 K sits near the winter jet, 350 K near the summer and subtropical one. 6-hourly to day 10, on a hemispheric view and five regional ones (North Pacific, North America, North Atlantic & Europe, East Asia and South America; in the Southern Hemisphere the dynamic tropopause is the −2-PVU surface and the isentropic maps show −PV). θ runs purple and blue (cold, low tropopause) through green and yellow to red (warm, high tropopause) in 5 K steps; PV is yellow to orange below 2 PVU (tropospheric air) and blue to purple above it (stratospheric air), with the 2-PVU line in black. Where PV is still below 2 PVU at 100 hPa (the deep tropics) the 2-PVU surface lies above the model levels, and the maps show the 100 hPa values there.

North Pacific jet · 250 hPa maps, jet axis, phases
What this shows. The two jet streams of the cold season told apart, and where they stack: a polar jet, a subtropical jet, and a superposition, where both sit in one column over a single steep tropopause wall from the polar to the tropical tropopause, with unusually strong winds and the vertical circulations of high-impact winter storms (Winters & Martin 2014). The forecast is IFS-ENS’s 50 members at 12-hour steps to day 10, each member classified on its own: potential vorticity does not survive averaging, so the ensemble mean is never classified.

What this shows. The jet stream over the North Pacific at 250 hPa (about 10 km up), from 100°E to 60°W so the downstream half over North America is in view, for the next two weeks. Jet maps: the week-1 and week-2 ensemble-mean wind speed with isotachs, the jet axis (the latitude of the fastest wind at each longitude, where it reaches 30 m/s) against the ERA5 1991–2020 normal axis, and the share of member-days with a jet core of 50 m/s or more against the line where such winds occur on 20% of days normally; the loop steps the same two panels day by day. Jet axis: every member’s axis on days 3, 7, 10 and 14 against the normal axis and its usual (10–90%) range — where the lines fan out, the forecast does not know where the jet, and the storm track beneath it, will be. Members: AIFS-ENS control + 25 perturbed members (the 250 hPa level is only fetched for 25), IFS-ENS all 50 perturbed members (open data has no IFS control on pressure levels), all on the same 0.5° grid.

Wave activity flux · Rossby packets, 250 hPa · explainer
The Takaya–Nakamura (2001) wave activity flux is a phase-independent vector diagnostic of quasi-stationary Rossby wave propagation: unlike watching ridges and troughs move, the flux W points along the wave packet's group velocity — where the wave energy itself is travelling, ducted along the jet-stream waveguides — and its convergence marks where the downstream flow will amplify days later. That makes it a practical forecast tool for downstream development, blocking onset, and teleconnection excitation: watch packets radiate out of the tropical Pacific convection (the El Niño–PNA pathway) or off a mountain-torque event, arc along the subtropical jet, and pile into an amplifying ridge.

Tropical cyclones

Tropical cyclones · storms and odds, maps, phase space, shear, track record
What it shows. Every tropical storm in the Northern Hemisphere that ECMWF's two ensembles carry, from ECMWF's own tropical-cyclone tracker in the open data: the 50 perturbed members of the AI ensemble (AIFS-ENS) and the 50 of the physics ensemble (IFS-ENS), from each run started at 00 or 12 UTC. A storm is listed when it was observed at the start, or when at least 40 % of the members of either ensemble take a developing system to tropical-storm strength (17 m/s). The board describes the storms: where they go, when they turn, when they lose their tropical character and how deep they get afterwards. It says nothing about what a storm does to the weather downstream.

What it shows. Each storm on the board (the first four) on the dynamic tropopause of the IFS control (ECMWF's unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), 6-hourly to day 10, on a map around the members' tracks. The shading is θ on the 2-PVU surface, as on the hemispheric dynamic-tropopause maps: low θ (blue, purple) is stratospheric air reaching down in troughs, high θ (red) a high tropical tropopause; black contours give its pressure. Where 2 PVU lies above 100 hPa (the deep tropics) the 100 hPa values are shown.

What it shows. Each storm on the board through Hart's (2003) cyclone phase space along its own track in the IFS control (ECMWF's unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), every 12 hours to day 10: the thermal asymmetry B (900–600 hPa thickness right of the motion minus left, within 500 km; near 0 tropical, above 10 m frontal) against the lower-tropospheric thermal wind −VTL (900–600 hPa; positive warm core), and −VTL against the upper one −VTU (600–300 hPa), with the central pressure against time beside them. The geopotential height is block-averaged to 1° first, the grid Hart's thresholds were set on; 900 hPa is interpolated in ln p between 925 and 850 hPa; the parameters are 24-hour running means.

Most shear products quote one number: the 200–850 hPa wind difference around the storm (SHIPS, CIMSS), and how it is computed alone changes it by several knots (Velden & Sears 2014). This product looks at the whole environmental wind profile instead: where in the column the shear sits, what the vortex is doing about it, how much dry air it drives into the core, and which trough is causing it. It does this along every AIFS-ENS member's own track and in four deterministic models.

The sample. Every 00 and 12 UTC run of ECMWF's tropical-cyclone tracker in the open data since August 2024: IFS-ENS (50 perturbed members) and the IFS control (ECMWF's unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), and AIFS-ENS (50) and AIFS single since their tracker files began in May 2026. Every Northern-Hemisphere storm with an official id is matched to the IBTrACS best track nearest its observed position at the start (within 300 km).

Tropical circulation

Walker circulation · analysis, forecasts, by basin
The equatorial companion to the Hadley-cell plot above: the zonal overturning streamfunction $\Psi_W(\lambda,p)$ built from the 5°S–5°N divergent zonal wind (velocity-potential decomposition, so the rotational flow that dwarfs it near the equator is removed).

Hadley cells · the cells, outflow and descent, turnover, sea-level pressure · explainer
Four parts: the cells, the zonal-mean overturning; outflow and descent, its upper and sinking branches on the map with the subtropical ridges and highs; turnover, back-trajectories timing how fast the air goes round; and sea-level pressure, the equatorial trough and subtropical highs at the surface day by day, analysed and forecast.

The surface end of the Hadley cells (the cells): the sea-level pressure from 40°S to 40°N — the equatorial trough under the rising branch, the subtropical highs under the descent — day by day, as the departure from ERA5’s 1991–2020 normal for the date, either in standard deviations of ERA5’s daily means (normalised) or in hPa. Normalising puts the deep tropics, where a day’s standard deviation is about 1.3 hPa, on the same footing as 30–40°, where it is about 6 hPa and 2 hPa is routine; the hPa view keeps the size. The scales are fixed at ±3σ and ±4 hPa. The contours are the pressure itself, every 4 hPa; grey is ground above 1,500 m, where sea-level pressure is a reduction rather than a measurement.

The Hadley cells’ upper and descending branches on the map rather than in the zonal mean (the cells): the outflow that carries air poleward along the tropopause from the deep tropical convection, the descent that brings it down over the subtropics, the subtropical ridges and surface highs that the descent builds, and how fast the air makes the trip.

Velocity potential · 200 hPa, irrotational wind
The large-scale divergent circulation aloft from the AIFS-ENS ensemble mean: the 200 hPa velocity-potential anomaly shaded, with the irrotational (divergent) wind as vectors. Green is upper-level divergence (the outflow above deep convection); orange is convergence (subsidence). The vectors point out of the divergence centres, tracing the rising and sinking branches of the Walker and Hadley circulations; in El Niño the divergence shifts east over the central Pacific. Anomaly is versus the ERA5 1991–2020 climatology. The first frame is the analysis, the rest is the forecast to day 14.

GDPS 150 hPa wind as isotach fill and contours with wind barbs and geopotential height (6 dam interval), 3-hourly frames to day 10 — the Hadley/Walker outflow layer and the subtropical jets. White is below 25 kt.

Rain-forced circulation · Gill model vs the observed χ200
The question. Rain is the tropical atmosphere's main heat source: every millimetre per day releases about 29 W m−2 of latent heat into the air column. Where it rains more than usual the air rises, spreads out near the tropopause and sinks somewhere else. That overturning is what the 200 hPa velocity potential χ200 maps (green = air diverging aloft over rising motion, orange = converging aloft over sinking). This figure asks, week by week, how much of the observed χ200 anomaly the rain anomaly alone accounts for — and, for the next two weeks, whether the AIFS ensemble's forecast rain and its forecast circulation tell the same story.

Atmospheric angular momentum · series, trend, phase, by latitude · explainer
Relative AAM is the angular momentum carried by the winds: $M_r=(a^3/g)\iiint u\cos^2\!\phi\,d\lambda\,d\phi\,dp$, the atmosphere's total westerly momentum about the rotation axis. It is dominated by the upper-tropospheric subtropical jets: the $\cos^2\phi$ weight peaks at the equator, but the deep tropics carry weak or easterly mass-weighted flow, so they contribute little to the total. The variability is carried mostly by zonal-wind changes aloft in the tropics and subtropics, where the weight is large; the shallow trade-wind layer holds little mass, so the trades act through the friction torque rather than as a store of momentum. It is shown here for the globe and each hemisphere from the winds of the 50 IFS-ENS members on 14 pressure levels (10–1000 hPa — effectively the full column; the same integral on ERA5's 37 levels is the reference check). IFS-ENS is ECMWF's physics ensemble: its change of angular momentum closes against its own torques, which AIFS-ENS’s does not (the torque budget below). The top panel is this year's observed AAM (black: ERA5 at 12 UTC every third day; ERA5 is published several days behind real time, so the line ends one to two weeks before the forecast starts) and the 15-day forecast (red; the shaded band is the members’ 10–90% range, the raw ensemble spread, not a calibrated interval) on the climatological annual cycle; the hemispheres are strongly out of phase (each peaks in its own winter). The bottom panel is the anomaly (departure from the 1991–2020 normal), which removes the seasonal cycle; its right-hand axis restates the anomaly as a percentage of the normal averaged over the forecast days, so it reads the forecast, not the months before it. El Niño strengthens the subtropical jets and weakens the trades, so the atmosphere holds more westerly momentum; conservation then requires the solid Earth to slow, so AAM ties directly to the measured length of day. The green trace is GFZ's ESMGFZ wind-term series (computed from ECMWF's operational analyses for the Earth-rotation community, its published anomaly rescaled onto absolute momentum) — an external check on the pipeline's day-to-day variability.

Surface torques · friction and mountain · explainer
Atmospheric angular momentum changes only through torques the Earth exerts on the atmosphere (measured here in Hadley: 1 Hadley = 1018 N m). Three torques act: friction, the surface wind stress; mountain torque, pressure pushing on resolved topography; and the gravity-wave drag the model exerts on sub-gridscale terrain. The first two are resolved live — the open-data forecast stream carries no gravity-wave stress, so that term is left in the residual. The budget applies to total AAM — the wind (relative) part plus the mass term the rotating Earth carries in its surface-pressure field. Only the wind term is computed here; it tracks the total closely on subseasonal timescales but is not identical to it.

About this page

How the atmosphere moves air, heat and momentum around the globe, from today’s analysis to two weeks ahead: the mid-latitude jets and Rossby waves, the tropical overturning (Walker and Hadley cells, velocity potential, rain-forced circulation), tropical cyclones, and angular momentum with the torques that change it. The forecasts are ECMWF’s two ensembles (AIFS-ENS and IFS-ENS), the AIFS single run, the IFS control (ECMWF’s unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026) and Canada’s GDPS, most of them refreshed with each 00 and 12 UTC cycle (the rain forecast also uses NOAA’s GEFS and Canada’s GEPS ensembles); most anomalies are against ERA5 1991–2020. The ocean side of El Niño is on the El Niño monitor, the polar vortex on the stratosphere page. One figure at a time, picked from the menu.

How to read the panels
  • Map: potential vorticity on the 330 K isentrope; the black line is 2 PVU, so inside it is stratospheric air, the upper trough. Grey arrows: 200–850 hPa shear of the total wind; red: the storm's environmental shear (its own circulation removed). The IFS control (ECMWF's unperturbed 9 km run, called HRES until May 2026), 6-hourly.
  • Hodograph: the environmental wind (storm's own circulation removed) at every level from 1000 to 100 hPa. Bold: the IFS control, coloured from low levels (warm) to high (blue); thin: other models; grey: the 50 members. Red dashed: the 200–850 hPa shear vector; black: storm motion. Storm-relative subtracts each run's own storm motion at that hour from its winds, the frame in which the low-level inflow into the storm is read; the 200–850 hPa shear is a difference between two levels, so it is the same in either frame. Under the hodograph and in the summary: the shear's direction against the storm's track (along it, against it, or how many degrees to its left or right).
  • Tilt: the 850→500 hPa vorticity-centroid displacement in the shear's frame, downshear up. Lines: each run's path to day 5, the dot at the chosen hour; grey dots: members; red diamonds: the observed 2–6 km tilt from NOAA's tail Doppler radar, rotated by the IFS control's shear within 3 hours of the radar time (radar analyses from before the run are left out here; the time series shows them).
  • Time series: the chosen measure along every run, with the AIFS-ENS member spread; the dark vertical line is the hour on the slider. Keyboard: the arrow keys step the slider once it has focus.
