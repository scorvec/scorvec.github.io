Our WeatherNext 2 vs Google's

We run Google DeepMind's open WeatherNext 2 weights ourselves on rented TPUs, starting from ECMWF's open data. Is the result Google's forecast? Here our 64 members from 00 UTC 15 October 2026 are set against Google's own 64 members from the same start, along with the one input we have to improvise: the sea-surface temperature.

In short

  • It is the same ensemble. Globally, at every lead from day 1 to day 15, the two 64-member means differ by 1.0–1.7 times what sampling alone would produce. The largest gap is 2 m temperature in the first three days. The spreads agree within 2%.
  • The consistent gaps are small and sit in the tropics, and in 2 m temperature in the far south. In the tropics 500 hPa heights differ by 1.4–2.5 times the sampling noise. South of 20°S, 2 m temperature reaches 2.2 times.
  • The sea-surface temperature is close but runs cold. ECMWF's open data has no SST field, so we use the sea's skin temperature. It sits 0.19 K below Google's SST everywhere and at every local time, which is the cool ocean skin. Adding 0.2 K brings it within 0.14 K RMS, closer than NOAA's OISST (0.49 K) or 6-day-old ERA5 (0.57 K).

How far apart are the two ensembles?

Each line shows the area-weighted difference between our ensemble mean and Google's, divided by the difference that two independent 64-member samples of the same ensemble would show by chance. A value of 1 means indistinguishable.

Where they differ

The difference between the means at each grid point, in units of sampling noise. Values within about ±2 are chance. A real difference shows up as broad patches of one sign.

The sea-surface temperature

WeatherNext 2 needs the SST at its start. Google's initial SST comes from its operational ECMWF analysis. ECMWF's open data has no SST field, so our run uses the skin temperature over the sea, floored at the freezing point of sea water (−1.69 °C), where ERA5 puts sea ice. Each map shows one candidate minus Google's SST, over ice-free sea.

The skin temperature is cold everywhere by almost the same amount, with no daytime warming signal: 0.20–0.24 K at every local solar time. That's the ocean's cool skin, a film a tenth of a millimetre thick that sits a little below the water beneath it. A constant +0.2 K removes nearly all of it. OISST and old ERA5 are each bulk SST analyses too, but they differ from ECMWF's own by more, through other satellites and a different blend, or six days of change.

What we'll change

  • Add 0.2 K to the sea skin temperature for the SST input. It costs nothing, and against Google's SST it beats both alternatives.
  • Re-run 8 members with the corrected SST to see whether the tropical gap in the first chart closes. If part of it stays, the next suspect is the six-hour-earlier input state, which comes from a different ECMWF stream.
  • Score both ensembles against the analyses once the 15 days have passed (after 30 October).

How it was done

  • Our run: WeatherNext 2 weights (Google DeepMind, CC BY 4.0), all four checkpoints × 16 noise draws = 64 members, 60 six-hour steps. It ran on eight spot TPU v5e chips at 2.9 seconds a step: about 27 minutes per chip and about $2.24 in all. Initial state: the ECMWF IFS open-data analysis at 18 UTC 14 October and 00 UTC 15 October, from the Google Cloud mirror.
  • Google's run: the operational WeatherNext 2 64-member ensemble from the same start, read from Earth Engine. A 1° request made Earth Engine average its 0.25° fields, which smooths out mountains and coasts. An earlier version of this comparison fell into that trap. Every number here samples the native 0.25° points.
